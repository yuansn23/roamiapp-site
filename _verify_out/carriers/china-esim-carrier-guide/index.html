A China eSIM looks, on the surface, like any other travel purchase: pick a data allowance, scan a QR code, land, connect. That part is genuinely easy. Treat this as the pre-flight China briefing — the one we would send a friend, jargon removed.

Quick answer: Most travellers in China will be happiest with plug-and-play; Great Wall makes sense for tighter budgets and city-only trips. Run your device through the compatibility checker first — the step China visitors skip and later regret.

The Ookla Speedtest Global Index for August 2026 ranks China 12th in the world for mobile download speed, with a median of 176.16 Mbps and 32 ms latency, well above the worldwide median of 109.05 Mbps. Cable.co.uk prices 1 GB of local mobile data at roughly USD 0.38 — 28th of 237 markets, against a USD 2.59 global average. The radios are superb and the data is cheap.

So why does connectivity in China trip up so many visitors? Because in mainland China, connectivity is an identity-and-routing problem, not a speed problem.

Every SIM, physical or embedded, is registered against a real name under national rules, and the three state-owned carriers only began a tightly controlled eSIM trial in October 2025 that short-stay visitors cannot practically use. The apps many travelers live on also behave differently depending on where their profile routes its traffic.

In brief: You cannot buy a mainland eSIM from China Mobile, China Unicom or China Telecom as a short-term visitor — the carriers’ China eSIM service opened as an in-store trial in October 2025 for residents with mainland-certified devices.

Visitors register a physical SIM with a passport at a carrier service hall, or more commonly install a travel eSIM issued outside mainland China. A travel-issued China eSIM roams onto China Mobile or China Unicom towers and routes data internationally, so it installs in minutes, needs no registration, and is the default choice for trips under two or three weeks. Direct carriers suit long stays where a Chinese phone number matters. Check your handset against the eSIM compatibility quiz first, and test a free coverage test before you commit to a plan.

China’s 5G build: why the network is rarely your bottleneck

At the State Council’s April 2026 industry briefing, MIIT reported 4.958 million 5G base stations at the end of March 2026, 5G-Advanced rollout in 330 cities, gigabit optical access in every county and 5G in every town.

Administrative villages reach 100% broadband coverage, more than 95% on 5G, and continuous signal runs along 654,000 km of highways and railways. The limiting factor on a China eSIM is almost never the radio layer.

China Mobile vs China Unicom: which is better in China?

Inbound roaming is bought at wholesale, so your profile registers on whichever host network the provider has agreements with — usually China Mobile or China Unicom in the big cities. Seeing the operator name change in your status bar as you cross a province is normal, not a fault to chase.

China real-name registration rules

Mainland China has operated mandatory real-name registration for mobile services since 2013, and enforcement is total: no SIM activates without an identity check.

  • Registration document: your passport with a valid entry stamp, or a Foreign Permanent Resident ID Card, at an official service hall of China Mobile, China Unicom, China Telecom or China Broadnet — the procedure set out in the government’s own guidance for foreign nationals.

  • One identity, five numbers: a single ID can hold at most five numbers, eSIM lines included, and none may carry an outstanding balance.

  • eSIM trial rules are tighter: under the trial that began 13 October 2025, carriers issue phone eSIMs only to people 18 or older on mainland-certified devices. Several carriers accept only the Foreign Permanent Resident ID Card from foreign nationals; China Unicom’s published rules are the broadest, accepting passports.

  • Payment: service halls take cash and Chinese mobile wallets. Alipay and WeChat Pay both let foreign cards bind to an account, worth doing anyway since QR payments are near-universal in daily life.

The trial itself needs one clear paragraph, because most advice on this question predates it. On 13 October 2025 all three carriers announced MIIT approval for a commercial eSIM trial for phones, with three constraints attached.

Activation happens in person at a carrier service hall, only, on the certified devices above, and a device holds at most two domestic eSIM numbers. For residents it opened the door to eSIM phones such as the iPhone Air, on sale in the mainland from 22 October 2025. For a two-week visitor the answer is still, practically, no.

Buy direct or use a travel eSIM in China?

Every other country guide treats “carrier versus travel eSIM” as a price comparison. In China it is closer to a lifestyle decision, because the two products differ in registration burden, phone number, and how traffic is routed:

Direct from China Mobile / Unicom / Telecom Travel eSIM issued outside mainland China
Registration Passport, in person, at a service hall; real-name rules apply None — bought online, delivered by QR code or app
Device requirements Any unlocked phone (physical SIM); mainland-certified device for the trial Unlocked, eSIM-capable phone; profile installed before you fly
Phone number Yes — a real Chinese number for calls, SMS and codes Usually data-only, no local number
Traffic routing Domestic — through the carriers’ national gateways International — tunnels to the provider’s gateway outside the mainland (often Hong Kong, China or Singapore)
Typical cost Inexpensive prepaid bundles, plus top-ups A fixed upfront price, well under home-carrier roaming
Best for Stays of a month or more, or needing a local number Trips of two or three weeks, arrival-day connectivity

The routing line is the one people most often misread, so let’s state it neutrally. Inbound international roaming is a standard technical architecture: your phone uses the Chinese radio network, but the data session leaves via the issuing provider’s gateway outside the mainland. The result is a browsing experience closer to the one at home than to the one on a domestic line.

A locally registered SIM routes domestically. On mainland networks many international services — Google, WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook and numerous foreign news sites — are restricted. Neither route changes your device’s capabilities; they exit the country at different points. If you depend on specific apps, choose the route that carries them and decide before departure.

The most affordable China plans

A mainland prepaid bundle from a service hall is genuinely cheap per gigabyte — Cable.co.uk’s USD 0.38 average is real — but it arrives with a hall visit, a registration record and a number you must keep funded. A travel eSIM costs more per gigabyte and less per hour of your trip.

What a China eSIM cannot do for you

Being honest about the trade-offs saves a lot of mid-trip frustration:

  • No local number. Most travel profiles are data-only, so bookings and ticket pre-sales that expect a Chinese mobile number still need another route.
  • No SMS. Verification codes for Chinese apps arrive on your home number, which matters if you are activating a local account.
  • No voice calls. Calling is app-based over data; there is no dial tone on a data-only profile.
  • No mainland registration record. Useful if you want privacy, inconvenient if you later need a local number and must start the hall process from scratch.

Two ways to get connected: service hall or travel eSIM

Two working routes exist. They differ in paperwork, not in difficulty, and neither takes more than an hour when you arrive prepared.

Who are China’s mobile operators?

The official path for foreigners, per the government’s own guidance, runs through the carriers’ halls rather than airport kiosks:

  1. Locate an official service hall for China Mobile, China Unicom or China Telecom — flagship halls in big cities handle foreign passports most smoothly.
  2. Bring your passport with a valid entry stamp (or Foreign Permanent Resident ID Card). Staff register the SIM against your identity on the spot.
  3. Choose a prepaid package with a data allowance that matches your stay; staff can adjust or top up later.
  4. Pay by cash or mobile wallet. If you have already bound a foreign card to Alipay or WeChat Pay, that works.
  5. Insert the SIM, confirm the number is active, and keep the registration receipt.

One app-store wrinkle: mainland Android phones ship without Google Play, using Chinese app stores instead, and some carrier apps live only there. The carriers’ own apps are generally obtainable on iPhone, but budget time for friction.

How to activate a China eSIM

  1. Check your device against the compatibility list and confirm it is unlocked.
  2. Buy online — delivery is a QR code or app-based install, no ID required.
  3. Install the profile while you still have normal internet. Profile-download servers are not reliably reachable from inside mainland China.
  4. On arrival: enable the eSIM line, turn on data roaming, and it registers on China Mobile or China Unicom automatically.
  5. Label the line clearly and keep your home SIM active for calls and SMS verification codes.

For the install mechanics, the generic sequence (Settings → Add eSIM → scan → label → set data line) is in the activation how-to.

APN settings are the last thing most travelers touch and the first to rule out when data fails on a locally purchased SIM.

Enter these only if you bought a SIM directly from a carrier and automatic configuration did not run. On iPhone: Settings → Cellular → the line → Cellular Data Network. On Android: Settings → Network & Internet → Access Point Names → new APN. Restart after saving, and confirm the eSIM is the selected data line.

On a travel eSIM, change nothing. The profile carries its own configuration, and overwriting it is the most common way to break a working connection.

Activating your China eSIM step by step

Activation mechanics are universal: install on Wi-Fi, enable the line, switch on data roaming, set it as your data line, reboot if needed. The generic sequence and the wider error catalogue live in our eSIM activation error guide, and the background on what happens when a profile downloads is in what eSIM activation is and how it works.

Four failure patterns are specific to mainland China.

A. The profile will not download. Almost always an in-China download attempt: provisioning servers are not reliably reachable from mainland networks. Install while abroad, on home Wi-Fi. A profile that is already installed needs only enabling on landing.

B. Installed, but no registration. Check the eSIM line is enabled, data roaming is on, and the phone is unlocked. Then try manual network selection — pick China Mobile or China Unicom rather than automatic.

C. Signal bars, but “the internet is broken”. On a domestically routed SIM this is not a fault: many international apps and websites are restricted on mainland networks, and no setting changes that. On a travel eSIM, confirm the eSIM is the selected data line first.

D. Data stops mid-trip. Prepaid Chinese packages switch to per-MB billing when the allowance ends; travel plans hard-stop or throttle. Top up through the carrier’s channels or your provider’s app, and check usage.

China eSIM troubleshooting: the order for a dead connection

  1. Is data roaming on for the eSIM line, and is that line set as the data line?
  2. Does the phone show an operator name at all? If not, try manual network selection.
  3. Is the allowance exhausted? Check in the provider’s app before assuming a breakage.
  4. Is the phone locked? A locked handset installs a profile and then refuses to register.
  5. Has the profile ever worked? If it worked yesterday, the network is fine and the plan is the suspect.

Taking a China eSIM across the Hong Kong, China and Macao, China borders

One consequence of China’s arrangement as separate markets trips up more visitors than any technical fault: Hong Kong, China, Macao, China and Taiwan, China each run their own networks under their own regulators, and a mainland China eSIM does not automatically cover them. Crossing from Shenzhen into Hong Kong, China is a genuine border for your SIM.

At that border a mainland-routed line stops working once it loses the mainland network. It then roams on the destination’s carriers at roaming rates, if the plan covers the destination at all — and most mainland tourist products do not. Plan the crossing rather than improvising it.

The pattern that works on almost any mainland-plus-neighbours trip is one regional profile rather than three single-country ones. The questions travelers ask once they are in China

Will WhatsApp calls connect in China?

On mainland networks — including a locally registered China Mobile, Unicom or Telecom SIM — no: WhatsApp, Google services, Instagram and Facebook are among the apps and sites restricted there. Travel eSIMs whose data routes internationally before exiting behave differently, and on those profiles such services generally work as they do at home. That is a routing characteristic, not a setting you can change on a local SIM.

ID needed to buy a China SIM

Registration cannot be skipped. Foreigners register a physical SIM at a carrier service hall with a passport plus a valid entry stamp — that is the official procedure. A Chinese national ID is only needed for residents; foreign permanent residents use the Foreign Permanent Resident ID Card.

Can a visitor get a resident-style eSIM in China?

Practically, no. Since the MIIT-approved trial began in October 2025, phone eSIMs are issued in person, to people 18 or older, on mainland-certified devices, and China Mobile’s published rules exclude overseas-user applications entirely. A short-term visitor’s usable option is a profile issued abroad.

Can I use Alipay or WeChat Pay with a foreign credit card?

Yes. Both wallets let overseas visitors bind international cards — Visa, Mastercard, JCB, Diners Club, Discover and UnionPay among them — after a passport and facial-recognition check that usually clears in minutes. Overseas-issued UnionPay cards have been bindable since November 2024. Expect a small cross-border fee above a low transaction threshold, and keep a physical card for hotels.

How much data does a China trip need?

Most two-week trips sit comfortably in the 10–20 GB range: maps, translation, ride-hailing, messaging and video calls are the heavy items, and mainland users average about 24 GB a month. If you stream video daily, size up.

Is your model cleared for China eSIM use?

If it is unlocked, eSIM-capable, and covers LTE bands 1/3/5/8/38/39/40/41 plus n41 or n78 for 5G, yes. The classic exception is a phone bought in mainland China, where eSIM hardware may be disabled entirely. Verify your exact model on the eSIM compatibility checker before purchase.

Can I keep my home number active alongside a China eSIM?

Yes, and you should. Keep your home SIM active for calls and SMS verification codes, point mobile data at the eSIM line, and switch off data roaming on the home line. Data-only eSIMs cannot receive SMS, so bank codes still arrive on your home number.

What happens to my China eSIM if I cross into Hong Kong, China or Macao, China?

It stops providing data at the boundary unless the plan explicitly includes the destination, and it does not switch to local Hong Kong or Macao rates on its own. Buy a separate profile for those legs or pick a regional plan before you fly.

Does a Hong Kong eSIM work in Shenzhen?

Not as local service — Hong Kong profiles roam onto mainland networks at roaming rates, if at all, and coverage is not guaranteed across the border. Use a mainland-served plan in Shenzhen and a Hong Kong eSIM in Hong Kong, China, or one regional plan covering both.

Why will my profile not download inside China?

Because you tried to download it from inside mainland China. Provisioning servers are not reliably reachable on local networks. Install profiles before you fly, and if you must install on the ground, do it over airport or hotel Wi-Fi with patience for a retry.

Which is better for a city trip: China Unicom or China Mobile?

On a travel eSIM you rarely choose — the profile roams onto whichever network is strongest, and in megacities both are excellent. China Mobile’s edge is rural and western coverage; Unicom’s is the breadth of ID documents it accepts in official channels.
