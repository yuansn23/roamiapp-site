Norway is the clearest coverage paradox in Europe. Its networks are world-class: Telenor took the Best Mobile Network and Best 5G Network titles in the Ookla Speedtest Connectivity Report for Norway, 1H 2025 with a median 5G download of 255.4 Mbps, and Opensignal named both Telenor and ice Global Leaders for Reliability Experience in its 2025 awards. Yet data is expensive — Cable.co.uk puts 1 GB at about USD 4.07, among the priciest 15% of the 237 markets it tracks — and the geography is brutal: a single tunnel can black out your phone for twenty minutes, and the road between two covered towns can be uncovered for hours. A Norway eSIM therefore works best when you plan it like the trip itself: route by route, fjord by fjord.

Quick answer: Buy at home, install on the plane, and choose Telenor or the local networks based on whether your route is rural or urban — the full comparison below shows which is which. Most Norway visitors forget one step — the eSIM device checker covers it.

Two things to settle before comparing carriers: device compatibility and the install process. The device compatibility list answers the first, and how an eSIM profile installs answers the second — everything below stays on the carriers themselves.

Bottom line: City legs — Oslo, Trondheim, Stavanger — are effortless on all three carriers; Telenor is fastest (255.4 Mbps median 5G) and Telia holds 5G more often (84.6% availability). Fjord legs — Geiranger, Trollstigen, the Atlantic Ocean Road — alternate between full signal in towns and dead zones on the exposed stretches; download offline maps before each leg. The far north — Lofoten, Tromsø, the Hurtigruten coast — runs on Telenor’s and Telia’s rural grids with long gaps between towns. Buying locally means a passport, a store or kiosk visit, and Norwegian prices; most visitors take a Norway travel eSIM instead — the sample eSIM lets you test before you commit.

Three networks serve the country: Telenor (the incumbent, and the strongest overall per Ookla), Telia (the ex-NetCom network, now owned by Telia), and ice (the low-cost challenger). Before the route-by-route detail, the scoreboard — all figures from Ookla’s 1H 2025 Norway report:

Read the two availability-and-consistency numbers together, because they explain the whole country: Telenor’s 94.4% consistency means its signal where it exists rarely falls below usable speeds, and Telia’s 84.6% 5G availability means its users stay on 5G more of the time. Neither number promises coverage in the valleys — that is a geography problem no operator has solved.

The two datasets agree on the part that matters: Telenor leads on speed nationally and in every city Opensignal breaks out, while Telia’s advantage is real but narrower than a marketing page implies. The city numbers also show how much of Norway is not the country — Oslo’s 202.5 Mbps Telenor median is more than twice ice’s 88.6 Mbps in the same city, and all three fall to double digits the moment you leave.

The regulator supplies the other half of the picture. The practical output of Nkom’s market regulation is that Telenor, Telia and Ice between them hold around 90% of Norwegian mobile subscriptions, and that baseline household coverage at the end of 2025 was estimated at 100% for 4G and 99.8% for 5G. Hold that against this page’s central warning: 99.8% of households is a very different claim from 99.8% of a country whose households sit in valleys separated by rock.

Leg 1: Oslo and the southeast corridor

The easy part of the country. Oslo measured among the fastest mobile cities in Norway, and the E6/E18 corridors toward Göteborg and beyond hold signal nearly continuously — all three carriers run dense urban 5G, with Telia’s high availability making it the network you stay on 5G longest, and Telenor delivering the top speeds. Gardermoen airport arrivals, the Flytoget rail link, the metro: all covered. If your entire Norway trip is Oslo plus a day trip, your Norway eSIM choice barely matters; spend the decision time on the legs below.

Leg 2: The fjord country — Bergen, Geiranger and Trollstigen

This is where the paradox starts. Bergen itself is well covered — Telenor’s consistency shows in a city wrapped in mountains — but the moment you leave, the terrain takes over:

  • The Bergen Railway (Oslo–Bergen) crosses the Hardangervidda plateau: 4G in the stations and villages (Geilo, Eidfjord side), patches of nothing on the open tundra between. A seven-hour train ride is roughly half coverage, half dead time — plan downloads accordingly.
  • Geiranger and Trollstigen sit inside some of the steepest terrain in Scandinavia. The village of Geiranger has signal; the viewpoints above it often do not. Trollstigen’s eleven hairpins climb out of coverage quickly — take your photos at the top and upload them back in Åndalsnes or the Valldal valley, which carries signal better.
  • Ferry crossings on the fjords are generally covered close to shore and dead in the middle of the widest ones. Do not schedule a video call for the Hellesylt–Geiranger ferry.

The practical pattern every Norway eSIM user meets on a fjord leg: full signal in towns, 4G or less on the connecting roads, nothing on the exposed stretches. Carriers cover where people live and work — and almost nobody lives on the top of a waterfall road.

Leg 3: The Atlantic Ocean Road and Ålesund

Norway’s most photographed drive, the string of bridges between Averøy and the mainland, is also one of its least predictable coverage stretches: towns on either side (Molde, Kristiansund, the Bud–Kårvåg coast) hold solid 4G, while the exposed bridge sections over open sea can drop out entirely. It is a short leg, which makes it simple to plan: cache your offline map in Molde, enjoy the drive disconnected, upload from Ålesund, which has dependable 5G from Telenor and Telia.

Leg 4: Northbound — Trondheim, Bodø and the Lofoten E10

Trondheim is a genuine mobile bright spot — it recorded the fastest median mobile download of any Norwegian city in Ookla’s data at 303.56 Mbps — and the rail and road corridors north of it stay usable through Trøndelag. Then the country empties out. The E6 through Nordland runs between covered towns with long quiet stretches, and the Lofoten archipelago behaves exactly as the terrain suggests:

  • The E10 from Å to Svolvær holds signal in each fishing village (Reine, Hamnøy, Leknes) and thins on the connecting stretches, worst inside the longer tunnels.
  • Tunnels are the systematic dead zones of northern Norway — the country drives through hundreds of them, some many kilometers long, and your phone will drop and re-grab signal at each portal. Nothing is wrong with your eSIM; this is what rock does to radio.
  • Bodø, the ferry hub for the islands, measured among the fastest cities in the country (217.48 Mbps median fixed, strong mobile) — a good place to re-download anything you burned through in the islands.

Crossing into Finnish or Swedish Lapland from here is a common detour — Kirkenes, Tromsø, then east. Our Finland eSIM carrier guide and Sweden eSIM guide cover those networks, and if the trip spans all three, compare a Nordic multi-country plan against buying three singles.

Leg 5: The Hurtigruten coastal voyage

The classic Bergen–Kirkenes sailing (and its northbound twin) hops between more than thirty ports, and the coverage profile is exactly what you would guess: signal is good in and around each port call, patchy at sea, and absent in the deeper fjord sections and long channel stretches between towns. Telenor’s coastal grid — the same network that serves the fishing villages — is the one you will ride most of the way. Treat the ship as a moving series of towns rather than a floating Wi-Fi hotspot, and you will have the right expectations: sync, upload and navigate while docked, then let the phone sleep between calls.

The operator’s own numbers sharpen that picture. Hurtigruten’s Coastal Express calls at 34 ports between Bergen and Kirkenes on a sailing that has run since 1893, with up to six port stops in a day — some as short as fifteen to thirty minutes. Wi-Fi is free throughout the ship, in cabins as well as public areas, which changes the arithmetic: the ship’s connection handles the open-water legs and the land networks handle the ports. The trap is between them. Your phone will try to attach to a maritime satellite network over open sea, and those networks are not covered by EU roaming price caps — the same exclusion that applies to planes. Turn data roaming off when the coast drops out of sight and back on when the next port appears.

Leg 6: The far north — Tromsø, the North Cape and Kirkenes

Above the Arctic Circle the logic of Leg 4 holds and intensifies: coverage is a property of settlements and main roads, and everything else is a gap.

Svalbard coverage: its own connectivity world

Svalbard is under Norwegian sovereignty and uses the Norwegian kroner, but it is not simply “Norway, further north”. Longyearbyen runs on a Telenor-operated network fed by a fibre cable from the mainland, with good coverage in the town and parts of Isfjorden — and effectively nothing once you leave that footprint. Three consequences for a connected trip:

  • A Norway travel eSIM may exclude Svalbard entirely, because the archipelago is routinely carved out of standard Norwegian coverage. Check explicitly rather than assuming.
  • 3G is gone from the archipelago, so the handset you need is 4G-capable — the same requirement as the mainland.
  • Anything beyond Longyearbyen and the inner fjord is satellite-communicator territory, not eSIM territory. That is the same conclusion this guide reaches about the Finnmark interior, and it is not a limitation you can buy your way out of.

Two habits follow. First, never rely on live navigation inside a long tunnel — cache the route before the portal. Second, schedule every call and upload around port calls and village stops rather than around the crossing, because the dock is where the network actually is.

Will my phone work on Norwegian networks?

Norway’s 5G runs on n78 (3.5 GHz) in cities and n28 (700 MHz) for rural reach; 4G uses bands 1/3/7/20. Any unlocked 4G-or-later phone from the last five years works, and Norwegian-sold phones are unlocked by law — the lock risk comes from phones bought on contract elsewhere, so check Carrier Lock in settings or run your model through the eSIM compatibility tool before buying anything.

How Nkom regulates Norwegian mobile — and why a visitor should care

Nkom, the Norwegian Communications Authority, is the reason this country has three networks rather than one. Its April 2024 decision keeps Telenor designated as having significant market power in wholesale mobile access and call origination, which obliges Telenor to meet reasonable requests for national roaming and MVNO access on non-discriminatory terms. That single ruling explains two things you notice on the ground: ice exists as a national proposition at all, and ice’s customers have been moving onto Telenor’s network for national roaming since January 2025 under a three-year agreement, having previously roamed on Telia.

The same regulator tracks the technology withdrawal, and the timetable differs by operator in a way that catches out anyone travelling with an old backup handset:

Operator 2G status What it means for an old phone
ice Completed its 2G shutdown on 27 May 2025 A 2G-only device has no service on ice at all
Telia Phased regional decommissioning from August to December 2025 Coverage of legacy devices is disappearing region by region
Telenor 2G sunset postponed to 31 December 2027 to protect critical services An old phone may still work — on Telenor only

Winter driving rules that shape your connectivity plan

Norway’s winter rules are legal rather than advisory, and they interact with your data plan in a way that is easy to miss.

Rule The detail
Studded tyres Permitted from 1 November to the first Sunday after Easter across most of the country, and from 16 October to 30 April in Nordland, Troms and Finnmark
Tread depth 3 mm minimum in winter against 1.6 mm in summer, with studs fitted to all four wheels
The fine 8,500 NOK for inadequate tyres, and studded tyres also carry a seasonal fee in the largest cities
Chains Vehicles over 3,500 kg must carry chains from 1 November, and the road authority can close a pass to anyone without them
Convoy driving Some passes run kolonnekjøring — you wait at a gate and then follow a snowplough. Waits can run to hours

Several of the roads this guide describes close outright for the season: Trollstigen typically shuts in October and reopens in June, and Rv55 over Sognefjellet closes from October to late May. Statens vegvesen’s traffic service — with live mountain-pass, tunnel, bridge and ferry layers, and a status line on 175 — is the only source worth trusting on the morning of a drive, and Entur is the equivalent for ferries and public transport, including the seasonal lines.

This is also where a second network stops being a luxury. A closed pass, a convoy queue, a ferry delay and a warning on a screen you cannot read converge on the same need: a phone that works on more than one carrier’s masts. A multi-network profile in Norway is not about speed. It is about having a fallback when the network you chose is the one that is down — or the one that does not reach the valley you have just turned into.

What to prepare before your Norway trip

Do this at home, not at Gardermoen. A Norwegian itinerary strings ferries, tunnels and one or two very long drives together, and the phone running your Norway eSIM has to be self-sufficient between stops.

  1. Confirm the weak link is the handset, not the network. Run *#06# for an EID or check the model against the device list, then read Carrier Lock under Settings → General → About. Norwegian phones are unlocked by law; a contract handset from home may not be.

  2. Install the profile on your own Wi-Fi. Scan the QR code, name the line something you will recognise at 1 a.m. on a ferry deck, and leave it dormant. It should attach by itself when you land.

  3. Point mobile data at the eSIM and leave the home SIM alone. Roaming ON for the eSIM line, OFF for the home SIM — inside the EEA this is the most frequent activation miss there is.

  4. Switch on Wi-Fi calling if your carrier offers it. Much of the coast sits outside the mobile grid but inside somebody’s café network, and calls then ride whatever Wi-Fi turns up.

  5. Cache the maps for each leg before the last town. Download in Bergen before the fjord drive, in Bodø before the E10, in Kirkenes before the plateau. Doing it at a tunnel portal is a wasted gesture.

  6. Assume one network is not enough. The winter-driving and mountain-pass material below is the reason: redundancy is a thing you plan for here, not a setting you switch on later.

How to install and activate your eSIM for Norway

  • Telenor: prepaid is a physical starter from a Telenor store or a 7-Eleven or Narvesen kiosk, with passport registration at the counter. There is no clean self-serve eSIM route for a visitor.
  • Telia: the one carrier with a genuine direct eSIM path for tourists — a prepaid eSIM around 99 NOK for 1 GB over 14 days, activated through Telia, with passport ID at purchase.
  • ice: prepaid eSIM available in stores and online, running on its own network in the cities and on Telenor’s for national roaming everywhere else.
  • Travel eSIMs: the profile arrives by QR code and attaches to Telenor or Telia, re-selecting as you move. No registration, no counter, no SIM tray.
  • Home SIMs with EU/EEA roaming: nothing to install — Norway is in the EEA, so roam-like-at-home applies. Switch data roaming off until you land, and check that your plan names Norway rather than “Europe”.

When your eSIM loses signal — and when it is actually broken

Install the profile before you fly (the full sequence is in the activation how-to), then diagnose in this order:

  1. In a tunnel or valley: not a fault. Signal returns at the portal or the next town. Enable Wi-Fi calling before the trip if your carrier supports it, so calls ride any Wi-Fi you find.
  2. No signal anywhere, even in Oslo: check the eSIM is enabled and set as your data line, data roaming is ON for that line, then try manual network selection — Settings → Cellular → Network Selection — and pick Telenor or Telia instead of automatic.
  3. Signal but no data: confirm the correct APN (table above) if this was a direct-carrier purchase, and check whether your allowance is spent — Norwegian prepaid top-ups are not cheap, which is one more reason to size the plan generously upfront.
  4. Still stuck: the general fixes — lock status, profile reinstall, network reset — are in eSIM activation error guide. In genuinely remote terrain, no eSIM is the right tool for safety; that is satellite-communicator territory, and Norway’s mountain rescue assumes you know it.

Norway fjord dead zones: what the terrain does and the fixes

It worked in Oslo and then died in the fjords for hours

  1. First check whether this is simply the geography: look for a settlement or an E-road before you change anything
  2. Try manual network selection — Settings → Cellular → Network Selection — and test Telenor and Telia in turn
  3. On a single-carrier plan this is the expected outcome rather than a fault. A multi-network profile is the fix, not a setting

Bars on the screen and nothing loading

  1. Assign mobile data to the eSIM and confirm that roaming is ON for it
  2. Check the APN if this was a direct-carrier purchase
  3. Check the allowance — Norwegian prepaid top-ups are expensive and it is easy to have quietly run out
  4. Reboot, then toggle airplane mode to force a fresh attach

The phone was working, then shut down in the cold Not a network fault. Cold drains lithium-ion cells fast, and a phone reporting 60% can die within minutes outdoors. Keep it against your body, carry the power bank warm, and treat the handset as a navigation tool with a short duty cycle rather than an always-on device.

Questions travelers ask about eSIMs in Norway

Telenor vs Telia Norway: coverage compared

Telenor, on the combined evidence: Best Mobile Network and Best 5G Network in Ookla’s 1H 2025 report, the highest consistency (94.4%), and a Global Leaders Reliability award from Opensignal. Telia is the stronger pick for staying on 5G in cities (84.6% availability), and ice is a budget option that thins out fast outside urban areas.

Will my eSIM work in the Lofoten Islands?

In the villages along the E10 — Svolvær, Leknes, Reine — yes, on Telenor’s and Telia’s coastal grids. Between them and inside the longer tunnels, expect dropouts on every carrier. Download offline maps in Svolvær or Bodø and you will not be caught out.

How do you size a Norway data plan?

Among the most expensive in the world: about USD 4.07 per gigabyte, 205th of 237 markets in Cable.co.uk’s global survey, against a USD 2.59 average. Prepaid tourist bundles are correspondingly priced, which is why most short-stay visitors buy a travel eSIM instead.

Can you buy a Norway eSIM from Telenor?

Yes. Will your eSIM work on the Hurtigruten ferry?

Partially. Ports and their approaches are covered well; open sea stretches and deep-fjord passages are not. The ship’s own Wi-Fi may help but is rarely fast — plan uploads for port calls, not the crossing.

Whose network reaches furthest across Norway?

Telenor, on reach. It runs long-range rural sites on headlands and has the widest reach into the plateaus, which is why the coastal grid and the northern routes ride on it; Telia is the stronger pick in and around the cities, and ice thins out fast outside urban areas.

Telenor vs Telia Norway 5G: which is better in Norway?

In all major cities — Bergen, Trondheim (the fastest measured city at 303.56 Mbps median mobile), Stavanger, Bodø — and spreading along main corridors. Outside the cities, 4G is the realistic service on every carrier, and it is good 4G.

Does my Norway eSIM work in Svalbard?

Treat Svalbard as its own network rather than an extension of Norway. Longyearbyen runs on a Telenor-operated system fed by a fibre cable from the mainland, with good signal in the town and parts of Isfjorden — and effectively nothing beyond that. Some Norway plans include the archipelago and some exclude it, so check explicitly before you rely on it. Outside the settled area the right tool is a satellite communicator, not a better eSIM.

Do I need winter tyres to drive in Norway?

Yes, in practice and often by law. Studded tyres are permitted from 1 November to the first Sunday after Easter in most of the country, and from 16 October to 30 April in Nordland, Troms and Finnmark. Winter tread must be at least 3 mm and studs must be on all four wheels, with an 8,500 NOK fine for inadequate tyres. Rental cars arrive correctly shod — confirm it at collection, along with whether your agreement permits border crossings.

What is coverage like outside Norway’s cities?

Outside the cities it follows settlements and main roads, and everything else is a gap. Solid rock kills radio signal outright — the Lærdal Tunnel alone is 24.51 km, or roughly twenty minutes of no service on every carrier. Cache the route before the portal and let the phone re-acquire at the exit; if it does not, toggle airplane mode.
