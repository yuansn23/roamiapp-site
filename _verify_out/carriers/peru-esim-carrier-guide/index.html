A Peru eSIM handles the coast and the highlands well and the Amazon only partially, and this guide is built around knowing where the map goes dark. Peru compresses three very different countries into one itinerary: a coastal strip with fast urban networks, an Andean highland where signal follows the river valleys, and an Amazon basin where towns are islands of coverage in a green sea. A travel profile delivers the first two reliably and the third only around population centers. What separates a smooth trip from a frustrating one is not really the operator you land on — it is knowing where the coverage ends, and installing your profile before the altitude starts working on you. Picture the briefing a knowledgeable friend would hand you for Peru; that is this page, minus the jargon.

If you read nothing else: Claro is Peru’s fastest network — a median 29.01 Mbps overall and 57.79 Mbps on 5G, per Ookla’s H2 2024 report. Entel runs the best 5G availability, and Bitel is the network users themselves rate highest. But the decision that shapes a Peru trip most is timing. Since late 2025, buying a local SIM in person means biometric registration against the immigration database, and Lima airport counters charge a steep premium for the privilege. The better play: install a Peru eSIM online before departure, download offline maps for every day on the road, and treat connectivity in towns as the reward rather than the default. Where does coverage actually work in Peru?

Ask which network is “best in Peru” and you get a city answer to a country question. On Ookla’s most recent per-city data, Lima recorded the fastest median mobile download among populous cities at 23.46 Mbps, while Iquitos sat at the bottom with 16.3 Mbps and a startling 98 ms latency. Between those two poles lies most of what travelers actually visit: mountain towns, valley roads, high plains and the rainforest.

Three realities follow from that geography. First, no operator covers the Andes in any meaningful sense — coverage clings to towns, highways and valley floors, and vanishes a few hundred meters above them. Second, the coast is genuinely easy: from Lima down the Pan-American corridor, you can expect service nearly everywhere people live. Third, the Amazon is a different country for connectivity; plan as if you will be offline and be pleasantly surprised in town.

That is why the single most useful prep step costs nothing: download offline map regions for Cusco, the Sacred Valley, your route, and any Amazon leg before you leave home. A prepaid profile installed in advance then becomes a bonus where it exists, not a dependency everywhere. A Peru eSIM purchased at home inherits whatever the local networks deliver — nothing more, but crucially nothing less.

What Claro, Entel, Bitel and Movistar offer visitors

Peru’s four operators divide the country unevenly, and each has a distinct commercial personality. Understanding them matters less for picking a Peru eSIM — a managed profile roams across partners automatically — and more for setting expectations about where your bars will come from.

Which Peru network is speediest?

Claro holds the fastest measured network in the country on every headline metric Ookla published for H2 2024, and it is the operator most travelers end up on for good reason: coverage is strong along the entire Pan-American corridor and in every city a visitor is likely to sleep in. It also sells eSIM service locally, describing the profile as a way to activate a line digitally without a plastic chip. The local friction is procedural rather than technical — the counter process can take well over an hour at a busy Miraflores store.

Which carrier’s 5G is top in Peru?

Entel runs the country’s most visible 5G push and wins the experience metrics rather than the raw-speed ones: highest 5G availability at 22.9 percent, best 5G video experience at 83.38 and best 5G gaming experience at 72.92. It supports eSIM too, and allows an in-store swap from a physical SIM to a profile at any branch — with one quirk worth knowing, since Entel’s activation QR code can be reused up to four times on the same device. Entel’s footprint thins outside Lima, Arequipa and the other major cities.

Bitel eSIM: the Peru budget brand with the top user rating

Bitel is Viettel’s Peruvian operation, and it expanded in a direction the incumbents ignored: small Andean towns, Amazon communities and mining districts that the bigger networks left unserved. Its pricing is the most aggressive in the market, and it holds the highest Speedtest user rating of any operator at 3.51 out of 5. One important caveat for visitors: Bitel’s dedicated tourist SIM is documented as being for foreign nationals holding a Carné de Extranjería, Peru’s foreign-resident card — so a short-stay visitor may not be eligible for that specific product.

Movistar eSIM: Peru’s incumbent with the deepest populated reach

Movistar leads on the coverage measure that is hardest to buy your way into: OSIPTEL’s operator-level figures put it first for mobile coverage in populated areas at 70.65 percent, ahead of Bitel at 41.41, Claro at 20.91 and Entel at 3.21 percent, as Peru connectivity guidance for foreign residents reports. Its architecture is deepest, partly because it has been building since the 1990s. On modern data performance it trails Claro and Entel. Its prepaid base tariff is metered rather than bundled, at roughly PEN 1.00 per 10 MB of data and PEN 0.49 per minute of national voice, so a top-up without a bundle burns fast.

Buying options: Peru eSIM

  1. Decide whether you need a local line at all. If you have a travel eSIM, the biometric registration process applies to neither the profile nor your trip, and you skip everything below.

  2. If you do want one, go to an authorized operator store or punto de atención — a Claro, Movistar, Entel or Bitel branch, usually in a mall or a main shopping street. Street kiosks and informal resellers generally cannot complete the required identity check.

  3. Bring your passport, and be ready for biometric verification against the Migraciones database. Since late 2025 this is mandatory for foreign nationals, and the process has no announced end date.

  4. Expect the transaction to take time. Reports of an hour or more for two lines at a Lima store are common, and the biometric step adds queueing.

  5. Pay for the chip and buy your bundle in the same visit. Base pay-as-you-go rates are punishing — a metered Movistar top-up can vanish on a single map session.

  6. Confirm the line is registered in your name and keep a copy of the activation contract. Registrations whose identity data does not match official records are notified, then suspended, then cancelled if not corrected.

  7. Top up with a recarga at kiosks, supermarkets or pharmacies. They can sell you credit for an existing number without any registration step of their own.

Peru’s biometric SIM rules changed how tourists connect

Here is the development that most rewritten Peru guides still miss. Following an OSIPTEL resolution, Peru introduced mandatory biometric verification for new SIM registrations, enforced from November 2025: buyers must complete a fingerprint check validated against the immigration database, with a passport as the visitor’s identity document. The measure was published as Resolución N.° 000116-2025-CD/OSIPTEL, adopted as a temporary security measure tied to a state of emergency, and it has no announced end date. The regulator’s position is fraud control; the traveler’s reality is friction.

The practical consequences for visitors are specific. Physical SIMs must be bought in person at official carrier centers of sale — convenience stores that display a carrier logo can sell recharge, but not new lines. City stores are inconsistent about serving short-term tourists, and some turn them away for lacking local residency documents. Airport counters will process travelers with passport and biometric check, but at prices several times what residents pay for the same data. Regulator detail and consumer guidance live at OSIPTEL, and the bottom line for trip planning is simple: the in-person route now costs either time, money, or both. That is precisely why the Peru eSIM route has become the default answer for arrivals from anywhere.

Two smaller rules in the same package are worth knowing if you do buy locally. Registrations with identity data that does not reconcile are handled on an accelerated clock — notification, then suspension, then cancellation if the mismatch is not corrected within days. And Peruvian prepaid lines with no traffic are subject to deactivation, so a line bought for a two-week trip and left untouched can go dormant.

Will your eSIM work on the Inca Trail?

Tour operators will tell you plainly: on the classic four-day Inca Trail, expect no phone signal for the majority of the route. Coverage flickers near the start outside Ollantaytambo territory and returns only near the end, particularly after Wiñay Wayna, close to the Sun Gate approach. Campsites along the way have no power outlets either, so a fully charged phone in airplane mode and a 10,000–20,000 mAh power bank are standard packing-list items.

The Salkantay route is similarly intermittent — service improves in valley settlements like Collpapampa and Lucmabamba and deteriorates badly at the high pass. The Lares route behaves the same way. Guidance from Cusco-based operators is consistent: assume you will be disconnected for most of each day out there, and plan your check-ins around known coverage pockets rather than expecting live signal. Carrying an eSIM is for the towns at either end, not for the passes between them.

None of this argues against buying connectivity — you will pass through towns daily on most routes, and guides themselves post photos from the endpoint towns. It argues for the right setup: a profile installed and tested before you fly, offline maps for the full route, and a family notification plan that does not depend on being reachable. Buying at home also sidesteps Peru’s new in-person registration hurdles, covered below.

Staying connected in Cusco and the Sacred Valley

Cusco itself is well served — its median mobile download measured 17.7 Mbps in Ookla’s city data, enough for maps, messaging and video calls from a hotel lobby. Coverage in the Sacred Valley towns of Pisac, Urubamba, Ollantaytambo and Chinchero is solid in their centers and drops off quickly outside them. The pattern repeats everywhere in the highlands: a town is a coverage bubble, and the bus ride between two bubbles is dead air.

Aguas Calientes, the railhead town below Machu Picchu, has dependable service thanks to its tourist economy. The citadel itself is more limited — signal at the site is spotty and congested at peak hours, so treat any upload from the terraces as a bonus. If your itinerary includes Rainbow Mountain or Ausangate, expect the same: nothing on the approach roads, occasional signal at village waypoints.

One practical tip: top up your data allowance in Cusco before heading into the valley, not after. A travel eSIM makes that trivial — the profile is already live, so a top-up is an app tap, not a counter conversation. Town kiosks sell recharge codes, but you do not want to be troubleshooting a purchase with two bars of signal and a bus to catch.

Lima and the Pacific coast coverage

If your trip never leaves the coastal strip, you will barely notice Peru’s coverage geography. Lima is the country’s fastest city, and the capital’s 5G is concentrated exactly where visitors stay: Miraflores, Barranco, San Isidro and the airport corridor. Arequipa, Peru’s second tourist hub, measured a 20.18 Mbps median download with Claro the fastest provider there.

North and south along the Pan-American Highway, towns chain together nicely — Trujillo, Peru’s second-fastest city at 23.27 Mbps, Chiclayo, Piura, Nazca and the Paracas peninsula all sit on or near the covered corridor. The gaps appear when you turn inland: Huacachina and the viewpoints around Nazca can lose signal within minutes of leaving the highway, and the marine reserves are, predictably, dead zones.

For this kind of itinerary, almost any modern data plan works. Speeds comfortably handle navigation, rideshare apps, streaming and video calls. The only genuine decision is buying before you arrive at Jorge Chávez airport, so you can skip the arrivals-hall queue entirely — more on that below. A Peru eSIM installed at home simply appears as a second line in your settings the day you land.

eSIM coverage in the Amazon and the altiplano

Iquitos is the largest city on Earth unreachable by road — you arrive by air or by river — and its connectivity matches its isolation. It posted Peru’s slowest median mobile download at 16.3 Mbps with 98 ms latency in Ookla’s measurement, and once you push out to jungle lodges, coverage thins to nothing within an hour’s boat ride. Puerto Maldonado and the Tambopata lodges follow the same pattern: town coverage, river silence.

The altiplano has a different problem. Puno and Lake Titicaca sit above 3,800 meters, where cold nights quietly drain phone batteries and make every percent of charge matter. Signal in Puno town and on the nearshore islands is workable; the remote reaches of the lake and the deep Colca Canyon to the south are not. Travelers heading to either region should carry a power bank, keep the phone warm overnight, and set expectations with anyone tracking their trip.

For the Amazon specifically, no amount of operator choice changes the physics of tower density. A data plan earns its keep in Iquitos town, at the airport, and in Puerto Maldonado — everywhere else, downloaded maps and offline entertainment carry the day. An eSIM helps at the edges of the region, not in the middle of it.

Movistar, the longtime incumbent, remains strong in big cities but trails on modern data performance. Entel has built the country’s most visible 5G push, and Bitel, Viettel’s budget brand, carries real weight in provincial towns.

Skip the Lima airport SIM counter and arrive connected

A travel eSIM for Peru runs on the same national networks — Claro, Entel and Bitel among them — but arrives as a downloadable profile rather than a plastic card, and none of the biometric registration machinery applies to it. You buy from home, scan a QR code or activate in an app, and the data line wakes when you switch off airplane mode in Peru. No counters, no fingerprints, no arrivals-hall markup — the eSIM arrives as code, and the paperwork stays with the plastic cards.

The comparison is stark. A Jorge Chávez arrivals purchase can involve a queue, a marked-up tourist bundle, and a registration process with your fingerprint in the system. A pre-departure purchase takes five minutes on your sofa. Local SIM vs Travel eSIM: The Cost Math

For all the friction in local registration, Peru is one of the cheapest countries in the region for mobile data. Cable.co.uk’s worldwide pricing survey puts the average cost of 1 GB there at about USD 0.45 — 40th of 237 markets, against a global average of USD 2.59. Only Chile and a handful of Caribbean-priced outliers undercut it in South America. That cheap local baseline is why even generous travel eSIM plans stay affordable.

Speeds are mid-tier globally. Ookla’s Peru page in the Speedtest Global Index put the country’s median mobile download at 61.93 Mbps in August 2026, ranking it 76th worldwide — below the global median, and a useful corrective to marketing that promises “blazing 5G” across the Andes. Fixed broadband is much stronger, which is why hotels in Lima can feel faster than the street outside.

Scale context from DataReportal’s Digital 2025 report: 27.3 million Peruvians were online at the start of 2025 (79.5% penetration), supported by 39.4 million mobile connections — 115% of the population, a sign of how many people carry more than one line. A market that saturated buys data cheaply and uses it constantly; visitors inherit both habits.

Leave the APN menus alone. Peru is a market where handset support varies, but travel profiles arrive pre-configured, so the field should never need filling. Buy from Claro, Movistar, Entel or Bitel instead, and those networks push settings over the air to capable handsets. If something still refuses to connect, start with the activation troubleshooting guide before touching anything else.

When your Peru eSIM goes quiet on the road

Four failure patterns cover almost everything a Peru trip produces, and only one of them is a settings problem.

A. Bars, but nothing loads. Confirm the travel profile, not your home SIM, is the selected data line — dual-SIM phones reassign this silently after a reboot or an OS update. Then check that data roaming is enabled on that specific line. This is the single most common first-day failure, and it has nothing to do with Peru.

B. Signal that disappears as you climb. This is geography, not a fault. Coverage in the Andes clings to valley floors and town centers, and a few hundred metres of altitude takes you out of range on every network. Move back toward the village or the main road and the line re-registers; an airplane-mode cycle speeds it up. Away from the towns, treat the phone as an offline device and rely on downloaded maps.

C. A local prepaid line that stops working after a few idle days. Peru’s registration rules include deactivation of prepaid lines that show no traffic, so a SIM bought for a fortnight and left in a bag can go dormant. If you buy locally, send a message or load a page every couple of days, and keep the activation contract in case you need to re-register.

D. Stuck on a weak network in a remote town. In provincial towns the phone sometimes holds a faint signal from the operator it first registered with rather than switching to a stronger one. Switch network selection from automatic to manual, pick the alternative network, then revert to automatic.

Anything beyond these belongs in the full eSIM activation troubleshooting guide, which catalogues error messages by cause.

Peru eSIM borders: crossing into Bolivia, Chile, Ecuador and Colombia

Peru sits in the middle of the Andes, and almost every long itinerary eventually tests a border. What happens depends on the kind of plan you carry, not on the country you are entering.

A locally bought Peruvian line roams — within limits. Peru’s operators include Andean roaming in many postpaid plans, but prepaid bundles are domestic products first. Do not assume a cheap local pack follows you across the Desaguadero crossing to Copacabana.

A Peru-only travel eSIM stops at the frontier. This is the most common surprise on the circuit, because the profile has worked perfectly in Lima, Cusco and Puno for two weeks and then simply ends. If your route reaches Lake Titicaca and crosses into Bolivia, the plan must name Bolivia.

A multi-country Andean or Latin America plan keeps working. If your itinerary strings together Cusco, Copacabana, La Paz and the Chilean lake district, compare the country lists on Bolivia eSIM plans and Chile eSIM plans against one regional profile — the regional buy usually wins on both price and convenience.

Ecuador and Colombia, for anyone continuing north, sit on the same logic: separate countries, separate country lists. Where a route runs through several of them, buying each country’s coverage explicitly beats hoping one profile roams everywhere. The one exception is the Amazon itself, where borders are irrelevant — the coverage gap is identical on both sides of any frontier you can reach by boat.

Peru eSIM questions travelers ask before booking

Will my eSIM work on the Inca Trail and Salkantay?

No, not for most of the route — and neither does anyone else’s. The Inca Trail has no signal for the bulk of its four days, with coverage returning near Wiñay Wayna, and Salkantay is intermittent with dead stretches at the high pass. Download offline maps, carry a power bank, and let your eSIM shine in Cusco, Aguas Calientes and the towns along the way.

Can I buy a SIM at Lima airport?

You can, and counters in the arrivals hall will register you with passport and biometric check. Expect marked-up tourist pricing and a queue at peak arrival hours. Since Peru’s November 2025 biometric registration rules, the online route has become the standard recommendation for short-stay visitors.

Do Claro, Movistar, Entel and Bitel all support eSIM?

Claro and Entel both officially support eSIM in Peru — Claro describes it as activating a line digitally without a plastic chip, and Entel allows an in-store swap with a QR code that can be reused up to four times on the same device. Movistar offers eSIM service locally too, though as a visitor you would normally need a store visit. Bitel’s eSIM availability for short-stay tourists is the least established of the four.

How much data costs in Peru

Claro’s bundles are the easiest reference point: PEN 20 buys 6 GB plus 500 national minutes, PEN 30 buys 10 GB with unlimited national calls, and PEN 40 buys 20 GB. Entel’s equivalent sits near PEN 29 plus a chip charge, and the SIM itself costs PEN 5 to 20. That is well under USD 12 a month for a heavy allowance — but only if you can complete the registration.

Can I use my home SIM and a Peru eSIM together?

Yes, if you use dual-SIM correctly. Keep your physical home SIM active for calls and SMS verification, run mobile data on the eSIM profile, and disable roaming on the home line. One caution: most travel data plans are data-only, so bank confirmation codes still arrive on your home number.

Does it work in Bolivia or Chile afterward?

A Peru-only plan stops at the border. If your itinerary crosses into neighboring countries, look at our Bolivia eSIM guide for the Altiplano side, or Chile plans for the southern lake districts.

Taking the northern route instead? Colombia coverage is covered in the same depth — buying each country’s coverage explicitly beats hoping one profile roams everywhere.

Is 5G worth paying for in Peru?

Only if your trip is city-bound. Claro’s 5G-capable plans start at PEN 69.90, and 5G is available in Lima, Arequipa, Trujillo, Chiclayo, Piura and Cusco rather than nationally. 5G connections were roughly 7.5 percent of the market in early 2026. If your route runs through the Sacred Valley, Colca or the Amazon, the 5G premium buys you nothing.

Why do some stores refuse to sell me a SIM?

Because the biometric check against the Migraciones database cannot be completed everywhere. Only authorized operator stores and puntos de atención have the terminals and training for it, and some branches turn short-stay visitors away for lacking residency documentation. Kiosks and street resellers generally cannot complete the verification at all, and a line sold without it can be suspended shortly afterwards.

When does the validity clock start?

On most travel eSIM plans, the window begins when the profile first connects to a local network, not at purchase — which is why installing at home does not burn days of your allowance. Confirm the exact start rule on the plan you buy, since providers differ on this detail.

When should I install my Peru eSIM?

Before, and on Wi-Fi. Installing in Cusco means troubleshooting at altitude with tired Wi-Fi and a QR code you may no longer be able to open. Ten minutes at home removes every variable, and the profile simply waits until it sees a Peruvian network.

Can I rely on Wi-Fi instead of buying a plan?

Partly. Hotels, hostels and cafés in Lima, Cusco and Arequipa usually offer free Wi-Fi, and it is fine for uploading photos at the end of the day. It is not available on the trail, at Rainbow Mountain, on the buses between valley towns, or in the jungle lodges — which is exactly when you want a working map. Treat Wi-Fi as a bonus and the data line as the plan.

What if my Peru eSIM stops working?

Work the four patterns above in order — data line, geography, dormancy if you bought locally, then manual network selection — and if the line still refuses to register, the wider error catalogue in the eSIM troubleshooting guide covers the rest. Before you message support, dig out the EID and order number.
