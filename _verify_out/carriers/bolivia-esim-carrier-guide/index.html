Bolivia is the one country in South America where the carrier question is not about speed at all — it is about where the signal reaches. The national median mobile download sits at just 20.65 Mbps (Ookla, August 2026, 99th in the world), there is no commercial 5G yet, and the landscape does the rest: the altiplano sits at 3,600–4,000 m, the salt flats have no towers at all, and the Amazon basin eats every radio wave. Which Bolivia eSIM serves you well is therefore a coverage decision first and a price decision second.

Quick answer: Entel is the network most visitors should shortlist first in Bolivia; Tigo is the city-speed alternative to weigh against it. If you would sooner test than read, Roami’s free starter eSIM is the cost-free way to start in Bolivia.

This guide compares the three carriers an eSIM for Bolivia can ride on — state-owned Entel, Millicom-owned Tigo and the budget Viva network — then walks through what tourists can realistically buy locally, what a travel eSIM changes, and the two Bolivia-specific failure modes that catch visitors out. Whether the handset is eSIM-capable at all is a separate question, settled once on the compatibility page.

Fast take: Spend any real time outside the cities? Entel is the pick — its rural footprint is the widest in Bolivia, and it is the only operator with a usable signal in Uyuni town and much of the altiplano. Staying in La Paz, Santa Cruz or Cochabamba? Tigo usually posts the fastest urban 4G. Want the cheapest possible data for messaging and maps in the main cities? Viva does that job. And whoever you pick, remember the ground truth: the Salar de Uyuni itself has little to no signal for any carrier — download offline maps before you leave town.

Entel, Tigo and Viva: the measured numbers

Ookla’s Speedtest data puts Bolivia near the bottom of the global speed table, but the three carriers are not interchangeable — their footprints barely overlap outside the big cities.

Which network covers rural Bolivia best?

Entel is state-owned and carries the widest 4G/LTE footprint in the country, reaching roughly 70% of the population and — more importantly for travellers — a disproportionate share of the small towns the highway network connects. It holds the largest 700 MHz allocation, which is exactly the band that travels well across open altiplano and thin mountain air. Speeds in remote zones are modest, and Ookla’s consistency figures show Entel trading peak speed for presence: the signal is more likely to be there at all.

Where it matters: Uyuni town, Potosí, the Cordillera Real viewpoints, the Rurrenabaque side of the Amazon gateway, and the long La Paz–Santa Cruz bus corridor.

What’s the fastest carrier in Bolivia?

Tigo, owned by Luxembourg’s Millicom, is the largest private operator and concentrates its capacity where the people are: La Paz, El Alto, Santa Cruz and Cochabamba. In urban tests it typically posts the highest median download of the three, and its 1900 MHz and AWS spectrum is tuned for city capacity rather than rural reach. If your Bolivia time is split between hotels, offices and cafés in the big four cities, Tigo is the network your eSIM will enjoy most.

Bolivia mobile carriers

Viva (operated by NuevaTel, part of Trilogy International) runs 850 MHz and 1900 MHz, keeps costs low, and covers the main towns and trunk highways. It is entirely adequate for maps, messaging and the occasional video call, and it is the carrier local budget travellers default to. Its weakness is the same as its strength: it built the least, so the map thins out fastest once you leave the cities.

Bolivia has no 5G — what that means for your eSIM

Entel has been running 5G trial “experience zones” in departmental capitals using 3.5 GHz spectrum allocated by the regulator ATT, and an official commercial rollout has been on the government’s agenda since 2024. As of late 2026, though, no Bolivian operator sells commercial 5G service — the country’s population at large is on 4G and 3G.

Practically, that means two things. First, ignore any marketing that promises 5G speeds on a Bolivia eSIM; a realistic urban experience is 15–50 Mbps on 4G, which handles navigation, WhatsApp video and HD streaming without drama. Second, band anxiety is mostly unnecessary here: Bolivia’s 4G runs on bands (including 700 MHz and 850 MHz) that even modest international handsets support. The compatibility question that matters is whether your phone supports eSIM at all, not whether it has this year’s flagship radio. Check it once against the eSIM compatibility page before you buy anything.

Buying a local SIM in Bolivia versus a travel eSIM

All three carriers sell prepaid SIMs, and all three require in-person registration with your passport at a branded store — Bolivia has no fully online tourist SIM route. Expect a shop visit in La Paz or Santa Cruz, a photocopy-or-scan of your passport, and a waiting period before the line activates. Local prepaid data is cheap, but the process is built for residents with time to spare.

A travel eSIM for Bolivia skips all of it: the profile is issued abroad, installs at home on Wi-Fi, and attaches to Entel or Tigo when you land. You keep your home number alive for SMS verification on the physical SIM and switch mobile data to the eSIM line — the same dual-SIM setup we describe in how eSIM activation works. Your travel data plan lives on that second line, and if the Altiplano week stretches, a top-up extends it without a second install. If your itinerary continues into Peru or Chile, that matters more than it sounds, because a Bolivia-registered SIM is worthless the moment you cross the border.

Leave username and password empty unless the activation pack that came with the line states otherwise, and treat the carrier’s own settings page as the authority if a value is ever rejected.

  • iPhone: Settings → Cellular → the profile you just added → Cellular Data Network → APN. Leave user name and password untouched.

Save, then restart the handset. If data still fails, confirm the eSIM — not your home line — is the one selected for mobile data before changing anything else. On a dual-SIM phone a Bolivian profile that never activates is more often a data-line mistake than a network problem.

When a Bolivia eSIM will not connect: four checks in order

Work down this list before you touch a support chat, because three of the four causes are local to your phone.

1. Nothing installs. Nine times out of ten the handset is carrier-locked, or the QR code has already been consumed. Check Settings → General → About → Carrier Lock (iOS) or the SIM-lock status on Android, and confirm with your provider whether the profile is still pending.

2. It installs but shows no bars. The altiplano is a hard place to make a first attach. Run the radio through a full off-and-on cycle, then give the scan a minute — forcing a manual choice immediately often lands the phone on a rural cell that hands over badly. Two failed scans in a row justify a restart before anything more exotic.

3. Bars, but no data. Verify the eSIM is set as the mobile-data line, confirm data roaming is on for that line only, and check the APN against the table above if you bought locally. A prepaid line that has not been topped up looks exactly like a network fault.

4. “SOS” or “Emergency calls only”. In practice this is a second live profile competing for the radio. Delete it, reboot, and reset network settings as the third step. Reinstalling should come last, because a single-use QR code cannot be issued twice.

Anything outside those four patterns belongs to the general catalogue in our eSIM activation error guide. New customers can also run the whole flow with a test at no charge before committing to a paid plan.

Questions travelers ask about Bolivian eSIMs

Does any eSIM work on the Salar de Uyuni?

Realistically, no. Uyuni town has Entel-based 4G good enough for maps and messages, but once you are out on the salt crust, or up at the coloured lagoons and geysers of the Eduardo Avaroa reserve, the signal drops to nothing for every carrier. Guides on multi-day salt-flat tours expect you to be offline — download maps, accommodation details and entertainment in town before you depart.

Is your device on the Bolivia eSIM list?

Yes, provided the phone is eSIM-capable and carrier-unlocked: iPhone XS and newer, Google Pixel 3 and newer, and recent Samsung Galaxy models all install a Bolivian profile without issue. Because Bolivia runs 4G, you do not need a 5G handset. The one hard exception is mainland-China-model iPhones, which have eSIM disabled at the hardware level. Which operator offers top 5G in Bolivia?

Not commercially. Entel operates trial 5G zones in some capitals, but no Bolivian operator sells 5G service to the public, so every Bolivia eSIM — ours included — runs on 4G and 3G. That is why the realistic speed ceiling is around 20 Mbps nationally rather than the three-digit figures you see for Chile or Brazil.

Roaming with a Bolivia eSIM

No roaming fees apply — the profile connects to Bolivian networks at local rates, so your home carrier never bills you for the data you use in Bolivia. Keep data roaming switched off on your home SIM and switched on for the eSIM line, and background traffic from your home number cannot run up a bill.

Is hotspot allowed on Bolivia eSIMs?

Yes — most plans allow personal hotspot, which is useful when a laptop needs the connection and the hotel Wi-Fi does not. Check the allowance on the specific plan page, since a few unlimited-style products cap tethering separately.

Is WhatsApp calling usable in Bolivia?

Yes. Bolivia’s travel ecosystem — buses, hostels, tour operators, restaurants — runs on WhatsApp, and WhatsApp works fine on a data-only eSIM using your existing number. You will only miss a local number if you need to receive domestic Bolivian SMS verification codes, which almost no tourist does.
