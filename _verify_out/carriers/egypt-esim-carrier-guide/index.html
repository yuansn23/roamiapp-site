Egypt is one of the cheapest countries in the world for mobile data — and one of the most bureaucratic to buy it in. An Egypt eSIM is how you get the first without the second: local networks priced at roughly USD 0.65 per GB, no branch visit, no passport photocopy, no signing a paper contract at a counter in Cairo. Quick answer: Buy your Egypt eSIM at home, install it on the plane, and lean on Vodafone for the classic Nile-and-Red-Sea circuit, or WE for city speed — the full comparison below shows which is which. Most Egypt visitors forget one step — the compatibility checker covers it.

The 30-second version: Egypt has four national carriers: Vodafone Egypt (the market leader with the widest tourist-route coverage), e& Egypt / Etisalat Misr, Orange Egypt and WE (Telecom Egypt). On Ookla’s H2 2024 report WE was the fastest provider at 32.93 Mbps median download, Orange followed at 26.59 Mbps and Etisalat at 22.67 Mbps — but the more important fact is that 5G launched nationwide on 4 June 2025, roughly doubling measured median speeds in its first week. Getting a local SIM as a foreigner requires an in-person branch visit with your original passport; a prepaid Egypt eSIM skips that entirely and keeps your own number alive for WhatsApp and banking codes.

Why does Egypt make SIM buyers register in person?

Start here, because it is the single biggest difference between Egypt and most tourist destinations. The National Telecom Regulatory Authority (NTRA) — Egypt’s telecom regulator — requires foreign visitors to register a local line in person. You must visit a mobile operator branch, present your original valid passport plus proof of lawful entry, let the agent keep a color copy, and sign a physical contract on the spot. There is no online path and no airport-counter shortcut around the identity check; at Cairo International’s arrival halls, queues at the operator kiosks routinely stretch past 45 minutes on late-night bank arrivals, and activation of a freshly registered line can lag by hours.

Separately, Egypt registers the phones themselves. Since 2025 the country has run an IMEI-based device registration system through the official “Telephony” app: your handset works on local networks for 90 days without registration, and the duty-free allowance that once covered the first imported phone was withdrawn on 21 January 2026 (NTRA announcement). Tourists and Egyptians living abroad kept a 90-day exemption instead, and a device running on a foreign SIM with active roaming is exempt from the customs charge altogether. For a normal one-to-three-week trip this costs you nothing — but it explains why a local SIM ties your device into a government registry while an Egypt eSIM bought online — start with a free data trial if you want proof first — connects you as an international visitor, so no IMEI paperwork and no branch queue ever applies.

One more quirk worth knowing before you choose: on locally registered Egyptian lines, voice-over-IP calls — WhatsApp voice and video in particular — are frequently throttled or blocked, a policy that has been reported on Egyptian networks for years. Travel eSIM traffic routes through foreign roaming gateways, so WhatsApp calls behave normally — and in a country where WhatsApp is how you message your guide, driver and hotel, that matters.

Airport counter or city store?

Both charge much the same headline prices; what differs is time and friction.

  • Cairo International (CAI). The carrier counters sit in arrivals before the taxi area. Registration happens at the desk — passport handed over, a fingerprint taken on the scanner, plan chosen — and the SIM is inserted and confirmed before you leave. Expect a small premium over city prices and the longest queues after a large overnight arrival bank.

  • Hurghada (HRG) and Sharm El Sheikh (SSH) arrivals. Both have carrier kiosks in international arrivals, which is the pragmatic choice if your holiday begins at a Red Sea resort rather than in Cairo.

  • City stores. Vodafone has stores in City Stars mall in Nasr City and in Maadi Grand Mall; Luxor has a Vodafone store on Corniche el Nile. City branches are calmer, carry the full plan range, and can troubleshoot activation on the spot — but they cost you a taxi and an afternoon.

  • What not to do. Buy from a street vendor or a souvenir kiosk. Fake and resold SIMs circulate around tourist sites, and a recycled line can be deactivated mid-trip.

Vodafone vs Etisalat: which is better in Egypt?

Vodafone is the operator to default to if your itinerary is the classic one — Cairo, Giza, Luxor, Aswan, and a Red Sea resort at the end. It is the widest-reaching network on that circuit and, critically for travelers, the one that holds up on the Nile and in the resort towns rather than only in the cities.

  • Best at: reach along the tourist spine. Vodafone is the carrier with documented coverage through the Luxor–Aswan waterway, at the Luxor temples, at Aswan and Philae, and in Sharm El Sheikh and Hurghada.

  • Visitor purchase route: the carrier’s tourist product is sold at Cairo, Hurghada and Sharm airports and in Vodafone shops. It is restricted to foreign visitors and its validity is tied to your visa stay.

  • What it costs: reported 2026 tourist plans run around 350 EGP for 12 GB and 550 EGP for 25 GB, both on 30-day validity — and the country’s overall per-gigabyte cost sits near USD 0.65, among the cheapest on earth.

  • What goes wrong: the same registration queue everyone else faces, plus the airport kiosk’s premium and its late-night staffing gaps.

  • Scenario: you sail Luxor to Aswan with a Vodafone-based profile, and the connection returns every time the boat moors at Kom Ombo or Edfu — which is exactly when you want to upload photographs and confirm the next day’s guide.

Orange Egypt: the Nile-valley network

Orange is the second operator by subscribers and took the runner-up position in Ookla’s H2 2024 measurement at 26.59 Mbps median download. It is strong exactly where a Nile itinerary spends its time — Cairo, Luxor and Aswan — and it often prices its tourist bundles a little under Vodafone’s.

  • Best at: urban and Nile-valley throughput. Orange has long been the network that measures well in the temple towns, and its prepaid lines are sold through a dense store network with an English store locator.

  • Visitor purchase route: Orange stores and airport counters, on the same passport-registration terms as everyone else.

  • What it costs: reported tourist plans land near 300 EGP for 10 GB on 30-day validity, making it the value pick of the major three.

  • What goes wrong: rural consistency. Orange’s footprint outside cities and resorts is thinner than Vodafone’s, so a bundle bought for a Nile cruise and then used on a long intercity road is the wrong tool.

  • Scenario: four nights in Luxor and Aswan with heavy photo uploads and video calls home — the valley is where Orange’s city medians apply.

e& Egypt (Etisalat Misr): the urban and canal-zone alternative

Etisalat Misr, now trading under the UAE’s e& brand, was the slowest of the three measured operators in H2 2024 at 22.67 Mbps — a gap that matters less than it sounds, because all three networks are fast in the places tourists actually stand. Its footprint is oriented toward Cairo, Alexandria and the Suez Canal zone.

  • Best at: city coverage and long-validity bundles. Its tourist products include plans valid for up to 90 days, which suits slow travel and extended stays better than the 30-day norm.

  • Visitor purchase route: e& branches and airport counters, with the same in-person passport registration.

  • What it costs: the cheapest of the three major operators at the entry point — reported tourist plans from around 280 EGP for 10 GB.

  • What goes wrong: remote-area signal, and the smallest English-language support footprint of the three when something needs explaining at a counter.

  • Scenario: a two-month Arabic course in Cairo with weekend trips to the canal cities — the long validity window matters more here than peak speed.

WE (Telecom): fastest in tests, thin at the resorts

WE is the mobile arm of state-owned Telecom Egypt, and it is the paradox of this market: the fastest operator in Ookla’s H2 2024 report at 32.93 Mbps, with the best consistency (88.6%), video (68.50) and gaming (74.02) scores and the highest Speedtest user rating (4.20/5) — and still not the operator most travel eSIMs route onto, because its resort presence is thinner.

  • Best at: nationwide measurement performance and price. WE is consistently the cheapest way to buy data in Egypt.

  • Visitor purchase route: WE branches and airport counters, with passport registration.

  • What it costs: reported tourist plans run from roughly 100 EGP for small bundles to 300 EGP for 20 GB, the lowest range of the four.

  • What goes wrong: the coverage pattern. WE is excellent where it is and less generous at Red Sea resorts and on rural routes, which is precisely where a holiday spends its days.

  • Scenario: a Cairo-based business trip where video-call quality is the priority — WE’s consistency score is the metric that predicts a call which does not freeze.

Will I actually get 5G in Egypt?

Every speed figure from 2024 and earlier is now historical context, because Egypt’s commercial 5G went live on 4 June 2025, launched by the NTRA in an event at the pyramids. Telecom Egypt (WE) secured its 15-year 5G license first, in January 2024, and Vodafone, Orange and e& followed in October 2024; together the four operators invested around USD 2.7 billion in spectrum and infrastructure, per the Egyptian State Information Service.

Early results were dramatic: the national median jumped from 46.42 Mbps on 4G to 82.49 Mbps across all technologies within days, with 5G medians above 100 Mbps. Vodafone’s initial rollout spanned more than 2,000 sites from Cairo into Upper Egypt; Orange concentrated on high-traffic areas. In practice for a visitor: Cairo, Alexandria, Giza and the main tourist hubs now show 5G on a capable phone, and the rest of the country runs on widely available 4G — with Egypt’s per-GB pricing, one of the better places in the region to burn through map data and video calls. Further spectrum awards followed in a USD 3.5 billion deal to double national mobile spectrum, so the trend line points up.

How good is Egypt’s 5G, really?

Ookla’s follow-up analysis of North Africa’s first 5G year is the most useful document on this market, and its verdict on Egypt is nuanced rather than triumphant. Egypt’s launch-day 5G median was 110.5 Mbps — a 3.5× improvement over a 32.8 Mbps 4G baseline, and the smallest uplift of the four North African launches covered. The reason is spectrum, not engineering: Egypt refarmed 20–30 MHz of 2.6 GHz, where Algeria and Tunisia launched with at least 100 MHz of 3.5 GHz (Ookla, 5G in North Africa 2026).

Egypt’s 5G was the most consistent trajectory in the region: the network was already close to its capacity ceiling on day one, so it had little to lose as adoption climbed. That cuts two ways. The good news is that what you measure is close to what you get — Egypt’s 5G is not a peak that collapses the moment a second phone joins the cell. The less good news is that the ceiling itself is modest by regional standards, and Egypt’s 4G layer measured 17% slower after the 5G launch as traffic migrated onto shared capacity.

The next move is already funded. In February 2026 the four operators signed a USD 3.5 billion allocation covering 410 MHz, including first-time access to C-band 3.5 GHz spectrum, phased through 2032, with a stated target of 40,000 towers by 2028 and Vodafone Egypt committing around EGP 20 billion for FY2026/27. For a visitor in 2026 the honest expectation is: 5G in Cairo, Alexandria, Giza and the main tourist hubs, 4G everywhere that matters, and no reason to pay a premium for the 5G badge.

Egypt eSIM coverage along the classic itinerary

The classic Egypt itinerary — Cairo and Giza, a Nile cruise to Luxor and Aswan, maybe a detour to Abu Simbel — tracks the river, and so does the coverage. The NTRA field-tests network quality monthly across exactly the tourist circuit: Cairo, Giza, Alexandria, the Delta, the Canal cities, the Red Sea coast and Upper Egypt.

What that means stop by stop:

  • Cairo and Giza. Dense multi-operator 5G and 4G; the problem is congestion, not coverage — speeds sag at rush hour, and the stone walls of museums and tombs kill any signal.

  • Luxor and Aswan. Town coverage is good on all four networks — Luxor was Egypt’s fastest city in Ookla’s H2 2024 city table. The West Bank monuments and the side roads to the Valley of the Kings thin out quickly.

  • The Nile cruise itself. Between Edfu and Kom Ombo, and along the rural stretches between temples, signal comes and goes; travelers regularly report stretches on 3G or nothing for some kilometers. Cruise-ship Wi-Fi is typically slow, paid and saturated in the evening, so a working Egypt eSIM is the difference between being reachable all week and disappearing between locks.

  • Abu Simbel and Lake Nasser. Coverage exists in the town and thins along the lake road. Download offline maps in Aswan.

On a moving itinerary like this, a profile that re-selects the strongest Egyptian network in each town beats a single-carrier SIM — which is how Roami’s Egypt eSIM behaves. Red Sea resorts and the roads between them

Hurghada and Sharm El Sheikh are the parts of Egypt most visited by Europeans, and both are well covered: Vodafone’s resort coverage along the Red Sea coast is the strongest, with Orange and Etisalat close behind in the towns, and the roads immediately behind the resort belts are where signal stops. Marsa Alam and Dahab sit further from the backbone but stay usable in town.

The honest boundary is the empty interior. Beyond the resort belts and the Nile corridor, coverage exists only in the towns themselves and along the main highways. Plan the long transfer legs as offline legs: download Google Maps areas and any dive-site files before leaving Cairo or Hurghada, and treat live navigation as a bonus rather than an expectation. An Egypt eSIM cannot conjure towers that were never built — it just makes sure that everywhere Egypt has coverage, you have it too.

Hurghada coverage

The most developed of the Red Sea resorts and the one with the most forgiving coverage: strong 4G through the resort strip and the marina, and along the coast road back toward Safaga and Luxor. Two things still fail. The diving and snorkelling boats leave the coverage envelope within about 20 minutes of the marina, so a day trip to a reef site is an offline day once the harbour walls are behind you. And the excursion and quad routes behind the resort belt lose signal as soon as the road turns to track.

Sharm El Sheikh coverage

Strong 4G across Naama Bay, the Old Market and the resort hotels, and coverage holds on the coastal road north toward Dahab and Nuweiba in the towns. Two caveats. First, Ras Mohammed National Park: the visitor centre and main beach areas have service, but the offshore reef sites are reached by boat and are off-grid, so confirm the dive plan with your operator while you are still in the marina. Second, the interior Sinai, including the St Catherine’s Monastery area and the Mount Sinai sunrise hike, has very limited signal on any network.

Marsa Alam and Dolphin House coverage

Marsa Alam is quieter, more exclusive, and the furthest of the resorts from the network backbone — town coverage is usable rather than fast. The signature excursion is Dolphin House (Shaab Samadai), a semicircular reef an hour or more offshore where snorkellers meet resident spinner dolphins. Practical arithmetic for that day: it is a full-day boat trip with a marine reserve fee payable locally, there is no pier — boarding happens by dinghy — and the reef sits far outside any tower’s reach. Book it through the hotel or a licensed operator, confirm the pick-up time in person, and expect to be unreachable for the middle six hours.

Why Red Sea hotel Wi-Fi is not a substitute for an Egypt eSIM

Resort Wi-Fi on the Red Sea coast is built for the same purpose as resort Wi-Fi anywhere: guests checking email by the pool. Three structural problems make it a poor substitute for a working line.

  • Evening saturation. Every guest returns from dinner at once. Access points that were fine at 4 p.m. are unusable at 9 p.m., which is exactly when people call home.

  • Geographic limits. A resort router reaches the room, the lobby and the pool bar. It does not reach the dive boat, the felucca or the taxi.

  • Payment and verification flows. Booking a dive, confirming a transfer, or approving a card transaction often needs an SMS or a live app. Those flows break on a captive-portal Wi-Fi network.

A Red Sea week is a good place to be pragmatic: use the hotel network for bulk downloads, and keep your eSIM for everything that has to work the first time.

Cairo and Alexandria: coverage and the apps that use it

Egypt’s two biggest cities have the best and the most demanding networks in the country — best because the coverage is genuinely dense, demanding because congestion is constant and every app you rely on needs live data the moment you open it.

Cairo coverage

  • Downtown and Islamic Cairo. Dense 4G from all four operators, with 5G on a capable phone. The failure mode is congestion rather than absence: speeds fall at rush hour, and the alley walls of Khan el-Khalili defeat signal in the middle of a souk.

  • City Stars and Nasr City. The City Stars mall area is a modern commercial district with strong coverage and the city’s handiest concentration of carrier stores — Vodafone has a branch here, which makes it a practical stop if a line needs troubleshooting in person.

  • Maadi. A residential and expat district with a Vodafone store in Maadi Grand Mall, good coverage, and quieter streets than downtown — a useful base if you need reliable calls rather than reliable sightseeing.

  • The Giza plateau. Strong 4G at the entrance and across the visitor areas, and 5G in the wider Giza area. Coverage thins inside the monuments themselves, and — worth saying plainly — no network helps you inside a tomb.

  • The metro. Cairo’s three-line metro is the fastest way across the city and the cheapest. Fares are distance-banded and paid in cash at the booth, and the first two carriages of each train are reserved for women during peak hours, marked with pink signs. Signal is usable in most stations, which makes the metro a reasonable place to send a message you were about to send above ground.

Alexandria coverage

Egypt’s second city is the calmer counterpart to Cairo: excellent coverage along the Corniche, at the Bibliotheca Alexandrina, around Qaitbay Citadel and along the Mediterranean beaches, with noticeably less traffic between you and wherever you are going. Orange and Etisalat both perform well here. If your itinerary includes a couple of days in Alexandria, treat it as the easiest connectivity of the trip — and the best place to catch up on uploads.

Two habits make ride-hailing work in Egypt. First, confirm the price and the destination before you get in: app pricing is upfront, but drivers sometimes try to renegotiate after accepting, and holding the app screen up is the simplest way to close that conversation. Second, carry small notes, because cash clears faster than a card in a city where every driver has a story about a delayed electronic payout.

One more reason these apps matter: they translate. Egyptian drivers largely do not speak English, and the app’s Arabic destination label removes the single most common source of tourist overcharging.

Will WhatsApp calls work in Egypt?

This is the single most common connectivity surprise in Egypt, and it catches people on their first evening.

  • On a locally registered Egyptian line, WhatsApp voice and video calls, FaceTime, Skype and Messenger calling are frequently throttled or blocked. Text messages, photos and voice notes generally work; the call button is what fails. The policy is applied inconsistently and has been reported on Egyptian networks for years.

  • On a travel eSIM, traffic leaves the country through a foreign roaming gateway, so WhatsApp calls and FaceTime behave the way they do at home. This is a routing difference, not a settings difference — there is nothing to configure.

  • On hotel Wi-Fi, behaviour follows the local network, so a blocked call on Wi-Fi is the same block, not a broken router.

  • On a VPN, calls generally work — but you must install and sign in to the VPN before you fly, because VPN provider sites can be unreachable once you are in the country, and that is a bad time to discover you never set it up.

For a trip where WhatsApp is how you reach your driver, guide and hotel — which is every trip to Egypt — the difference between a local line and a roaming profile is the difference between messaging and calling.

What happens to my data at Egypt’s borders?

Egypt’s land and sea borders have their own rules, and none of them are country-plan friendly.

Crossing at Taba: visas, cash and data

The Taba crossing links Eilat to the Sinai Peninsula and operates around the clock. Two visa outcomes matter for your data planning:

  • A 14-day, visa-free entry stamp if you stay inside the Sinai Peninsula, which includes Sharm El Sheikh. Fine for a Red Sea holiday; useless if you want to continue to Cairo.

  • A 30-day visa on arrival, paid in cash in US dollars, if you will travel outside Sinai — including a transit through Cairo.

Visa fees at Taba are cash-only and denomination-specific; travellers have reported being asked for US-dollar amounts in the 60–100 range depending on the route taken. Canada’s travel advisory sets out the same structure: 14 days visa-free within Sinai, or a 30-day visa on arrival at the crossing — check the current figure against the Government of Canada’s Egypt advice or your own foreign ministry before you travel, and carry US dollars in small notes.

Connectivity note: the border facility is a queue, not a lounge. Buy your data before you reach it, because there is nothing worse than standing in a visa line while trying to load a bus timetable.

Jordan by ferry: the Nuweiba–Aqaba crossing and your data

There is no direct Egypt–Jordan land border — Israel sits between the two — so the Nuweiba–Aqaba ferry is the standard crossing, run by Arab Bridge Maritime across roughly 34 nautical miles in about two to three hours. Practical points:

  • Schedules shift. Confirm the day’s sailing locally through your hotel or an agent rather than relying on a ticket bought weeks ahead.

  • Allow a full half-day. Port formalities at Nuweiba are slow, and the timetable is notional rather than punctual.

  • A day trip is not realistic. The crossing is too long to return the same day, so plan to stay overnight in Aqaba.

  • Data: your Egypt profile ends at the port. An eSIM for Jordan bought before you leave Sinai keeps you online from the Aqaba side without hunting for a shop at the ferry terminal.

Saudi Arabia overland: the Sinai land routes and roaming

Saudi Arabia is reachable overland only by routing through Jordan, since the Gulf of Aqaba sits between Egypt and the Kingdom with no direct road link from Sinai. A Sinai-to-Saudi plan is therefore really a Sinai-to-Aqaba-to-Haqal plan, and it needs a Saudi Arabia eSIM profile rather than an Egyptian one. If your itinerary is a Red Sea circuit rather than a single country, a regional Middle East eSIM or a multi-country Africa plan is usually cheaper than stacking four single-country plans.

Sudan border: proceed with care

The historic route south is the Lake Nasser ferry between Aswan and Wadi Halfa, which ran weekly for years but has been disrupted by Sudan’s internal conflict. Treat it as closed unless a current official advisory says otherwise, and check your government’s travel advice before planning anything around it. No connectivity advice substitutes for that check.

Egypt eSIM failure patterns and fixes

General activation errors — a profile that will not download, a scan that fails, an eSIM that installs and never registers — are covered in the full error catalogue. The Egypt-specific patterns:

A. The eSIM will not install

  1. Confirm the handset is unlocked before anything else — an Egyptian profile will not install on a locked phone.

  2. Make sure the QR has not already been scanned — most codes work once.

  3. Ask support whether the profile is still pending; reissuing the QR normally clears it.

B. Installed, but no signal bars

  1. Re-check the lock status.

  2. Settings → Cellular → Network Selection → pick Vodafone Egypt, Orange, e& or WE manually instead of automatic.

  3. Purge saved network settings, then power-cycle.

C. Signal bars, but no data

  1. Check that the eSIM, not your home SIM, is carrying the data.

  2. Switch data roaming on for the eSIM line, and only that line.

  3. Check the allowance — a spent local bundle leaves bars with nothing behind them, and a fresh local line can show bars before registration completes.

D. WhatsApp calls fail but messages send

  1. This is the Egyptian VoIP policy, not a fault. Messaging and media work; calling is throttled on local lines.

  2. If you are on a travel eSIM, confirm the profile is the active data line — if your home SIM is carrying the data, the call is still leaving through a local gateway.

  3. If you rely on calling, sign in to a VPN that you installed before you arrived.

E. “SOS” or “Emergency calls only”

  1. Check that the eSIM is switched on in the SIM manager.

  2. Remove or disable other profiles so the Egyptian line is the only candidate.

  3. Reboot and reset network settings.

  4. Last resort: delete the profile and reinstall it from a new QR code.

One pattern is not an error at all: bars that vanish between Edfu and Kom Ombo, on the long transfer roads, or on a dive boat are the network being honest. No profile fixes geography.

Egypt eSIM FAQ: registration, WhatsApp and coverage

Can you buy an Egypt eSIM from Vodafone?

Yes, but only in person: the NTRA requires an operator branch visit with your original passport and a signed paper contract before a line is issued to a foreigner. That applies to a local eSIM just as it does to a physical SIM.

Which carrier should you pick: Vodafone vs Etisalat?

For a full Cairo–Nile–Red Sea circuit, Vodafone Egypt is the most common choice because of its resort and river coverage; WE posted the best Ookla speed and consistency numbers in H2 2024; Orange is strong in Cairo, Luxor and Aswan; Etisalat is a solid urban network. A multi-network travel eSIM re-selects among them automatically.

Is 5G available in Egypt for visitors?

Yes — commercial 5G launched on 4 June 2025 after the NTRA awarded licenses to Vodafone, Orange, e& and Telecom Egypt. It is live in Cairo, Alexandria, Giza and other major cities, with 4G carrying the rest of the country. Egypt’s launch-day 5G median was 110.5 Mbps, and the network settled near 89 Mbps by month six — modest by regional standards but unusually stable.

Should you plan an Egypt trip around 5G?

No. Egypt launched on refarmed 2.6 GHz spectrum with only 20–30 MHz, which caps what 5G can deliver, and coverage is limited to major cities and tourist hubs. Plan for excellent 4G and treat 5G as a bonus in Cairo, Alexandria and Giza. Choose your plan on coverage where you are going, not on a 5G label.

Will my eSIM hold up on a Nile cruise?

In the towns, yes on all four networks. On the river between Edfu and Kom Ombo and along rural stretches, expect gaps — sometimes hours. Ship Wi-Fi exists but is usually slow and paid, so tell your contacts ahead of the cruise that replies may wait for the next port.

Does my phone need IMEI registration in Egypt?

Not for trips under 90 days. Egypt’s Telephony app registration applies to devices staying longer; short-stay tourists keep a 90-day exemption, and a phone using a foreign SIM with roaming active is exempt from the customs charge altogether. The duty-free allowance for an imported handset was withdrawn on 21 January 2026, so a second device bought locally — or a long stay on a local line — is where the registry starts to matter.

Is WhatsApp calling usable in Egypt?

On a travel eSIM, normally yes — the traffic leaves the country as roaming data. On locally registered Egyptian lines, WhatsApp voice and video are frequently throttled or blocked, which is one of the most common complaints about local SIMs.

Sizing your Egypt data pack

For maps, ride-hailing, WhatsApp and photo uploads, 5 GB comfortably covers a week; a two-week Cairo-plus-cruise-plus-Red-Sea trip runs closer to 10 GB. Live coverage-checking and hotspot use multiply that quickly.

Will my eSIM work at a Red Sea dive site?

Not offshore. Hurghada, Sharm El Sheikh and Marsa Alam have strong coverage in the resorts and along the promenades, but dive boats leave the coverage envelope within about 20 minutes of the marina. Do your boat-day admin in the harbour — booking, payment, confirming the pick-up time — and accept a phone-free day at sea.

Out of data in Egypt: what happens

The line stops passing traffic until you top up or buy a new plan — no surprise overage bills. Top-ups apply to the stored profile without reinstalling anything.

Does an Egypt-only eSIM work in Jordan or Saudi Arabia?

No — country profiles die at the border, so buy a second profile or a regional plan before you cross. There is also no direct Egypt–Jordan land border, so most travellers cross on the Nuweiba–Aqaba ferry by sea, and Sinai-to-Saudi routes run through Jordan rather than directly.

Buying options: Egypt eSIM

At Cairo International arrivals, at the Hurghada and Sharm El Sheikh arrival kiosks, or at a carrier-owned store in the city. Airport counters carry a small premium and longer queues; city branches stock the full plan range and can fix a problem on the spot. Avoid street vendors and souvenir kiosks, where fake and resold SIMs circulate.
