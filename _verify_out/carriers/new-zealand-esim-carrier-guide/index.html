Bands, locks and the 3G shutdown

Three things decide whether your phone works on a New Zealand carrier: its bands, its lock status, and the 3G shutdown that changed the voice rules in 2026. All three are covered below.

Which bands does my phone need for New Zealand?

New Zealand carriers build their 4G on Band 1 at 2100 MHz, Band 3 at 1800 MHz, and Band 28 at 700 MHz for the low-band coverage that matters outside towns. Their 5G sits on n78 at 3.5 GHz, with some n40 at 2300 MHz in dense areas. The band that visitors most often miss is Band 28, because it is the one that carries signal into valleys and along the South Island’s alpine roads.

Band tables are not part of the packing list. Look up the model number rather than the shop name in device compatibility list; that is the version that decides. Worth knowing before a long-haul flight: what eSIM activation is and how it works describes the download that turns the chip into a working line.

Does my phone need to be unlocked for a New Zealand eSIM?

A locked phone is the most common reason a New Zealand eSIM install simply fails, and New Zealand has an unusually light rule here: there is no SIM registration law, so a prepaid line activates without a passport, and the only lock that blocks you is the one your home carrier put on the handset.

Check it: iPhone goes to Settings, then General, then About, then Carrier Lock. “No SIM restrictions” is exactly what you want to see before buying. If it reads “SIM locked,” no outside eSIM will install until that changes.

Fix it: the unlocking request goes to the store or network that originally sold the device. A phone off contract is generally unlocked within hours, and normally for nothing. Only then try installing your eSIM.

If you bought a used phone, or a phone from a market outside New Zealand, assume it may be locked and check before you fly. New Zealand also ended 3G in 2026, which means a phone that only does 4G data without VoLTE loses the ability to make calls, so the lock check is only half the story.

How to reach the APN screen on your phone

  • iPhone: Settings, then Cellular, tap the eSIM line, then Cellular Data Network, then enter the APN
  • Android: Settings, then Connections, then Mobile Networks, then Access Point Names, then add a new APN

When auto-APN fails in New Zealand

Four fixes for New Zealand eSIM problems

General activation errors, a profile that will not download, a scan that fails, an eSIM that installs but never registers, are covered in our activation troubleshooting guide. The four patterns below are the New Zealand-specific ones.

A. The eSIM will not install

  1. In About settings, confirm Carrier Lock says No SIM restrictions
  2. The QR code usually works exactly once, so scan it carefully the first time
  3. A quick message to the network can confirm the profile is not stuck in a pending state

B. Installed, but no signal bars

  1. Re-check the lock status
  2. Settings, Cellular, Network Selection, pick One NZ, Spark or 2degrees manually instead of automatic
  3. Purge saved network settings, then power-cycle

C. Signal bars, but no internet

  1. Re-read the table above and make sure every field matches
  2. Check that the data-line slot belongs to the eSIM rather than your home SIM
  3. Remember a data-only travel eSIM cannot receive SMS, so bank codes still arrive on your home number

D. You are in a Fiordland or alpine blackout

  1. This is geography, not a fault; no plan creates signal where there is none
  2. Switch to Spark if you are on another network, since Spark uses Band 28 most heavily for rural reach
  3. Open the offline maps you downloaded before leaving Te Anau or the last town

Buying direct from Spark, One NZ or 2degrees

Can I buy a Spark eSIM as a visitor?

Yes, and it is the main visitor route. Spark sells eSIM-only Travel Packs online with no passport and no local address; the entry tier is $29 NZD for 2GB over 90 days, and the packs run up to $129 NZD for 120GB. The profile arrives by email as a QR code and connects when you land.

Does One NZ sell a tourist eSIM for short stays?

Yes. One NZ, the network once called Vodafone, sells a prepaid Travel SIM that an international visitor can order online and install by QR before departure, with tiers from $29 NZD for 2GB over 30 days to $69 NZD for 40GB over 90 days. In practice the 30-day entry is shorter than Spark’s 90-day pack, so match the validity to your trip.

Is the 2degrees Unlimited Travel SIM a real eSIM option?

It is, and it is built only for visitors. The Unlimited Travel SIM gives unlimited data, calls and texts to New Zealand and Australian numbers for three to thirty days, priced from $24 NZD for three days to $95 NZD for thirty days, delivered by QR through the travel-sim portal. It cannot be moved to a standard prepay plan, so treat it as a clean short-stay tool.

What identification do visitors need for a New Zealand eSIM?

Almost none. New Zealand has no SIM registration law, so a prepaid eSIM activates without a passport, and the main thing that blocks you is a phone still locked to your home carrier. You do need an unlocked handset and a payment card that the checkout accepts, and some resellers may ask for a name, but the carriers themselves do not require passport registration for prepaid lines.

Will my phone make calls after New Zealand’s 3G shutdown?

Only if it supports VoLTE. New Zealand retired 3G between late 2025 and 31 March 2026, and once 3G is gone a handset that does 4G data but not voice over LTE loses the ability to make calls, including 111 emergency calls. Text 3G to 550 to check your device, and on iPhone confirm Settings, Mobile, Mobile Data Options, Voice and Data shows 4G or 5G with VoLTE on.

Rural coverage in New Zealand: One NZ vs Spark

Spark, on the evidence, because it uses Band 28 at 700 MHz most heavily for low-band reach, and travellers consistently report the widest signal outside cities. One NZ won the Best Mobile Network award and leads on 5G availability at 60.9%, which helps in covered towns, but Spark is the network most campervanners and road-trippers lean on for the South Island. No carrier covers the Milford Road blackout or the Great Walks, so download offline maps regardless.

Is there mobile coverage on the Milford Road?

Almost none for long stretches. The roughly 120 km between Te Anau Downs and Milford Sound is the longest continuous highway blackout in New Zealand; signal vanishes after Te Anau Downs, returns briefly for a few kilometres in the Eglinton Valley, then disappears until the Milford Sound visitor terminal. The 1.2 km Homer Tunnel is a hard dead spot, so cache your maps and road alerts before you leave Te Anau.

Do the Great Walks have mobile coverage?

No, the famous ones are not. The Milford, Routeburn and Kepler tracks run with no usable cell coverage, and most Department of Conservation huts have no reception at all, so a phone is a poor safety device on its own. Pair a travel eSIM with offline maps and, on the remote tracks, a personal locator beacon hired from DOC in Te Anau or Queenstown.

Checking your handset’s bands before you rely on rural signal

Check your exact model, not the shop name, against eSIM compatibility checker, and confirm it has Band 28 at 700 MHz for rural reach plus n78 for 5G. If the band list is intimidating, the compatibility checker is the fast path; most iPhone XS-and-later, Pixel 3-and-later and Galaxy S20-and-later handsets are fine in the cities and weak only in the valleys.

Should you buy from a New Zealand carrier or use a travel eSIM?

Direct means one network, carrier-issued provisioning, and often a local number and unlimited calling; in exchange you do the activation step and sometimes a payment-card hurdle. A travel eSIM means instant delivery, no paperwork, automatic switching between One NZ, Spark and 2degrees, and a fixed upfront price. Short trip: travel eSIM. Months in New Zealand, or you need a local number: direct.

What if my New Zealand eSIM still will not work?

Work the New Zealand-specific patterns above in order, lock status, network selection, APN and data line, then profile reinstall, and if it still fails the wider error catalogue in eSIM error troubleshooting guide covers the rest. What does the eSIM inside your phone actually do?

An eSIM does away with the plastic card: a chip inside the phone holds the carrier profile instead. Your operator hands over a profile, the phone stores it on the built-in chip, and from that moment it works like a regular SIM. how eSIM installation works covers the plumbing, SM-DP+ servers included, plus the single-use rule behind the code.
