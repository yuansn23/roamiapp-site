Algeria’s SIM registration rules and what they mean for visitors

Algerian telecom regulations — overseen by the regulator ARPT (Autorité de Régulation de la Poste et des Télécommunications) — require every mobile connection to be registered to a real, identified person.

For visitors that means a passport, photocopies of it, and an Algerian address on the registration form. Travellers routinely give their hotel’s address, which is accepted practice, but the paperwork is not optional: an unregistered line can be disconnected without warning.

The system is built for residents and long-stay workers, not for a two-week holiday. That is why the fastest-growing answer for visitors is an Algeria eSIM installed before departure.

When you buy a travel data plan for Algeria, identity verification happens digitally at checkout, and the eSIM profile sits dormant on your phone until it touches an Algerian network.

Is SIM registration mandatory in Algeria?

Yes. ARPT rules require every mobile connection in Algeria to be registered to a real, identified person, and an unregistered line can be disconnected without warning — the paperwork is an identity check rather than a fee or a contract. What you hand over at the counter is listed in the airport-buying section below.

Physical cards and profiles issued inside Algeria land in the same national register, so the local eSIM route carries identical paperwork.

The Ministry of Post and Telecommunications said in late 2025 that it was preparing to fold eSIM into operator packages alongside the 5G rollout. A local product is genuinely coming; it is simply not a walk-up service for a short-stay visitor yet, as Ecofin Agency reported.

Why a travel eSIM never joins that queue

None of the registration machinery applies to data you bought abroad and carried in with you.

  • Identity checks happen online at the seller’s checkout, days before departure

  • There are no photocopies, no forms in Arabic or French, and no counter visit

  • The eSIM sleeps on your handset until it attaches to an Algerian network

  • Traffic rides an existing roaming agreement, so no Algerian subscription is created

Buying a local SIM at Houari Boumediene Airport

Suppose you still want a local line — for a long stay, or because you need an Algerian phone number. Here is the sequence at Houari Boumediene Airport.

Past customs and baggage claim, the arrivals hall holds shops for all three carriers side by side: Djezzy, Mobilis and Ooredoo. Expect the transaction to take 15 to 25 minutes, longer if the queue ahead of you is deep.

What ID do you need to buy a SIM in Algeria?

The clerk copies your passport, has you fill in a registration form, and asks for an address.

  • Bring the passport you travelled on, not a photocopy or a photo on your phone

  • Bring a spare paper copy so a busy desk does not have to leave the counter

  • Write the address legibly — a hotel booking is accepted practice

  • Nothing is anonymous — a mismatched registration is the fastest route to a dead line

Register the line with the passport you used at immigration, and keep the registration slip afterwards.

Proving ownership matters if the SIM stops working mid-trip, and the slip is what re-links it at a carrier shop. If the phone is lost or stolen, report it quickly — a registered line is an identified line.

Is there a walk-up tourist eSIM in Algeria?

No. There is no walk-up product that sidesteps the register.

  • No walk-up tourist eSIM. Ooredoo has the most advanced local rollout, and even that is a gradual, mainly-Algiers affair where the profile is issued in the shop by QR code. Djezzy and Mobilis sell nothing comparable to Morocco or Tunisia.

  • Prices assume residents. Market reports in 2025 had one operator selling 100 GB for around 2,500 dinars, roughly USD 11 — but the cheapest tiers and top-up menus assume you read French or Arabic.

  • Top-ups live in the physical economy. Recharging means scratch cards and kiosks, or the operator’s app with a local payment method.

  • Cash is king. Contactless payment is not widely established, and the airport counters take dinars only.

For a trip of a couple of weeks the math rarely favors the counter. For a stay of months, a registered local line makes sense alongside your Algeria eSIM — keep your home number for banking codes and run mobile data on the travel plan.

Ooredoo, Mobilis and Djezzy: how Algeria’s carriers compare

Algeria has three national networks, and the gap between them matters more here than in most markets.

Ookla’s Speedtest Connectivity Report for Algeria, H2 2024 (collection period July–December 2024) is the clearest operator-level dataset available.

What’s the fastest carrier in Algeria?

Ooredoo was the fastest mobile provider with a Speed Score of 40.40, a median download of 31.22 Mbps, a median upload of 12.37 Mbps, and the market’s lowest latency at 46 ms.

Ooredoo award, H2 2024 Score
Fastest Mobile Network (Speed Score) 40.40
Best Mobile Video Experience 68.53
Best Mobile Gaming Experience 77.12
Most Consistent Network (5 Mbps down / 1 Mbps up threshold) 87.8%
Top Rated by Speedtest users 3.76 / 5

Nearly 88 percent of Ooredoo’s samples cleared the 5/1 Mbps threshold — the difference between a video call that holds and one that collapses. It was also the fastest provider in Oran and Annaba, which makes it the sensible default for itineraries that move between cities.

The weakness is reach rather than speed. Ooredoo’s advantage sits in built-up areas and along the populated coast, and it is not the operator that won the coverage award.

Which operator has the widest coverage in Algeria?

Mobilis, the state-owned carrier, is the network Algeria builds with geography in mind.

In the same Ookla report it took the Best Mobile Coverage award, with a Coverage Score of 6.20 percent — the largest verified geographic footprint of the three. Its median download of 19.95 Mbps trails Ooredoo, but speed is not the reason anyone picks Mobilis.

The counterintuitive detail: Mobilis is the fastest provider in Algiers itself, with a city median of 26.61 Mbps, the lowest city latency in the report (38.1 ms), and 84.5 percent consistency. Downtown at rush hour, the state network often outperforms the faster-on-paper rival.

Where Mobilis earns its place is away from the coast. Its footprint reaches further into the southern provinces, which is why itineraries beyond the coast — Tamanrasset, Djanet, the oil-belt towns — are usually advised to land on its towers.

The trade-off is the national average: more than 11 Mbps below Ooredoo, so it feels slower exactly where both are strong.

Djezzy: the backup network

Djezzy, owned by the Veon group, carries the largest subscriber base of the three and is the network most ordinary Algerians hold.

For visitors its story is simpler: in Ookla’s H2 2024 data it recorded a median download of 16.61 Mbps, the slowest of the three, and it did not win an award category.

What it offers is depth in the north. Djezzy’s urban 4G density is solid and its prepaid pricing culture is aggressive, so in the northern cities an Algeria eSIM that includes Djezzy in its network list has one more fallback when Ooredoo and Mobilis are congested.

The honest summary for a short trip: Djezzy is a backup network, not a destination network. If your itinerary is Algiers, Oran and the coast, either of the other two will serve you better most of the time.

The northern circuit: which network wins which city

The northern circuit is where all three networks overlap, so the decision is about peak-hour comfort rather than basic availability.

  • Algiers belongs to Mobilis on the measured numbers, with the country’s lowest latency

  • Oran and Annaba belong to Ooredoo, which posted the fastest downloads in both

  • Constantine and the eastern interior are served well by all three in built-up areas

  • A switching eSIM removes the choice entirely, which matters on a mixed route

Between cities, the East-West Highway corridor holds a signal for most of its length, so navigation rarely fails. The 7-to-10 p.m. window softens speeds in the big cities rather than cutting them.

Heading south: footprint beats throughput

Go south and the ranking changes, because footprint beats throughput.

  • Tamanrasset and Djanet sit deeper inside Mobilis’s verified map than either rival’s

  • Southern towns may offer only 2G or 3G — enough for messages, not for video

  • Trunk roads carry a usable signal; the space between them carries nothing

  • Automatic selection earns its keep here, because the strongest network changes with the province

Do not read a national median as a promise for the south. The measured figures come from towns and roads, where people actually are.

Any Algeria eSIM you install before departure roams on precisely this infrastructure. One caveat: Algeria has in past years throttled internet access during national exam periods as an anti-cheating measure. It rarely affects tourist use, but early-summer slowdowns are not a fault in your plan.

Will my eSIM work in Algeria’s far south?

Algeria’s tourism divide runs along the Tell Atlas, and connectivity divides the same way.

The classic north-coast itinerary

The north — Algiers, Tipaza’s Roman ruins, Oran, Constantine, the Kabylie coast around Béjaïa and Tizi Ouzou — behaves like any Mediterranean destination.

Cities are strong, the East-West Highway corridor stays connected, and a standard Algeria eSIM covers everything a first-time visitor does. Two to three weeks here is the classic itinerary, and 5 to 8 GB is realistic budgeting for it.

The far south: Tamanrasset, Djanet and the long gaps

The deep south is a different category. The main sights around Tamanrasset and Djanet are reached by domestic flight or on guided 4x4 tours with local operators.

Connectivity along the way is confined to the southern towns and trunk routes, with hundreds of kilometers of nothing between them. In the towns you may find only 2G or 3G; away from the main routes there is no consumer network at all.

This is remote travel, where a satellite communicator — rentable in Tamanrasset or Djanet through local operators — is safety equipment rather than an upgrade. Download your maps while you still have signal, and never navigate the far south on the assumption that your phone will work.

A note on borders, because Algeria is ringed by five of them. Local lines and travel eSIMs alike go dark at the frontier: Algeria’s networks do not extend into neighbouring countries on standard plans.

A Maghreb loop — Algeria into Tunisia, or south toward Niger or Mali — means arranging connectivity country by country. Price each leg separately and check whether a regional plan genuinely covers both before assuming one exists.

Keep your registration slip filed with your passport at the frontier.

Do you need to change APN settings for Algeria?

An access point name is the last thing most visitors touch and the first thing worth ruling out when a data line refuses to work. Here is what the setting does, when you genuinely need to type it, and where the menus live.

What an APN is and why it matters

The APN is the label your phone hands the network so the operator knows which gateway to route your traffic through. It is plumbing rather than a password, and it is usually invisible.

When it is wrong, the symptom is specific: full bars, a registered network, and still no internet. Nothing on the status bar flags the APN as the culprit, which is why it earns an early check.

The eSIM must also be the line selected for mobile data, and roaming must be switched on for it — a correct APN on the wrong line still produces no connection.

When an eSIM needs manual entry

Managed profiles normally ship with their gateway already configured, which is why most visitors never open this menu.

  • A handset old enough that carrier settings never arrive on their own

  • A plan set up with a typed activation code instead of a QR scan

  • A locally bought SIM or eSIM where automatic configuration did not run

  • A handset that has been factory reset, or a plan reinstalled after an error

Never invent the values. Check the installation email your eSIM arrived with, the operator’s own support pages, or the paperwork handed to you at a counter. If the field already contains text, leave it alone — a duplicated entry breaks a working line.

Entering the APN on an iPhone

Open Settings, tap Cellular, tap the line carrying your Algeria data, then Cellular Data Network. Enter the value in the APN field and leave username and password blank unless the operator’s instructions say otherwise.

Reboot once you save, so the phone picks up the new gateway. If nothing changes, verify that the line you edited is the one set for mobile data.

Finding the APN screen on Android

Head into Settings and work down through Mobile Networks to Access Point Names. Add a new entry, save it, and select it so the phone stops using the previous one.

Menu wording shifts between manufacturers — older Samsung builds put the same screen under More Networks, and some ROMs label it “APN” directly. Reboot afterwards and check the data line selection once more.

Algeria eSIM plan sizes and what the data really costs

Data in Algeria is cheap at the source, which changes the maths in a visitor’s favour: the risk is buying too much, not spending too much.

What data allowance fits your trip

  • A long weekend in Algiers — 3 to 5 GB is plenty for navigation, messaging and photo uploads

  • Two weeks of coast — 5 to 8 GB is the comfortable middle

  • Heavy video or laptop tethering — plan for 10 to 15 GB instead

  • Voice and messaging — WhatsApp carries both over data, so no separate allowance is needed

Because an Algeria eSIM is data-only, your calls and verification codes keep running on your home line.

Longer stays: validity beats volume

Longer stays change the shape of the problem rather than the volume. The southern leg is largely offline, so the days between towns consume almost nothing.

  • Three weeks or more — pick a plan with a longer validity window rather than a bigger one-off bundle

  • A southern week in the middle — the connected days bookend the trip, so the allowance stretches further than the calendar suggests

  • Top-ups — buy an extra bundle from the same account or app rather than hunting for a scratch card

  • Local comparison — Algeria’s USD 0.73 per gigabyte against a USD 2.59 global average is what makes a modest eSIM feel inexpensive

The cheapest option is rarely a local counter purchase for a short stay. The 15 to 25 minutes of paperwork and the cash-only payment are real costs.

Getting an Algeria eSIM switched on

Installing is a five-minute job done at home, and doing it before you fly avoids a 25-minute queue in Algeria. The platform-by-platform walkthrough lives in our install-and-activate walkthrough.

Installing an Algeria eSIM

  1. Confirm your phone is unlocked and eSIM-capable — check the model against the compatibility list if you are unsure.

  2. Buy the plan, receive the QR code by email, and screenshot it somewhere besides the handset you are installing on.

  3. Install over Wi-Fi, label the line “Algeria,” and leave it disabled until landing.

  4. On arrival, enable the eSIM, set it as your data line, switch on data roaming, and pick the network manually if automatic selection hesitates.

Do step three at home. The arrivals hall Wi-Fi at Houari Boumediene is as congested as any airport’s, and an eSIM installed on the ground competes with everybody else’s.

eSIM troubleshooting, in order

Work these in order, because each one rules out a larger problem than the last.

  • No bars at all — scan for networks manually and choose Ooredoo, Mobilis or Djezzy by name

  • Stuck on a weak 2G signal — the Algeria-specific wrinkle in remote zones, where the phone holds a faint legacy signal instead of moving to a stronger network; toggling airplane mode forces a fresh scan

Algeria eSIM questions travelers keep asking

Does a travel eSIM need registration in Algeria?

No. The registration law applies to lines issued inside Algeria by the local carriers. An eSIM sold internationally has already been verified through the provider’s own checkout, so there is nothing to present at a counter and no address to invent.

Can you buy a local eSIM in Algeria?

Not in a convenient way, currently. Ooredoo has the furthest progressed local rollout — an in-shop process using QR codes, concentrated in Algiers — while Mobilis and Djezzy have no walk-up tourist product. Verify with the operator before counting on it.

Can you call on WhatsApp in Algeria?

In the north, yes — city medians above 20 Mbps and 22 ms national latency are more than enough for navigation, messaging and video calls. WhatsApp is the default messaging app everywhere in the country. In the far south, plan around gaps: towns are covered, the spaces between them are not.

How much data does a two-week trip really need?

For a standard north-coast itinerary, 5 to 8 GB covers maps, social sharing and streaming. Heavy video use or hotspot sharing for a laptop pushes that toward 10 to 15 GB. Algerian data is cheap at the source, so a modest eSIM is usually the right starting point.

Does your handset support eSIM in Algeria?

It needs to be unlocked, eSIM-capable, and ideally friendly with LTE band 3 (1800 MHz) and band 7 (2600 MHz), which carry the bulk of the 4G traffic; band 20 (800 MHz) fills in rural coverage. Almost every international flagship from the last several years qualifies.

Does Algeria’s network slow down at peak hours?

It can, in the big cities. Central Algiers and Oran carry heavy residential traffic in the 7-to-10 p.m. window. The effect is a modest softening of speeds rather than an outage, and it rarely matters for messaging or navigation.

Can a local Algerian line and a travel eSIM run together?

Yes, and long-stay visitors often do exactly that: a local line for a genuine Algerian number, an eSIM for data. Point mobile data at the eSIM, keep data roaming off on the local line, and the two stay out of each other’s way.

Taking your Algeria eSIM overseas

It stops. Algeria’s networks do not extend into neighbouring countries on standard consumer plans, so a Maghreb loop means arranging connectivity country by country. Crossing into Tunisia, the Tunisia data plans apply from the moment you cross; heading west instead, so do Morocco’s.

Is a local Algerian SIM ever worth it?

Yes, in one scenario: a stay measured in months, where you need an Algerian number for a landlord, an employer or a local bank. In that case a registered line is worth the paperwork. For a holiday of two to three weeks, the queue and the cash-only counter outweigh the savings.

Which carrier should my eSIM pick for travel beyond the coast?

Mobilis, on the evidence — its verified footprint reaches furthest into the southern provinces, which is the only metric that matters once you leave the coast. A profile that selects networks automatically attaches to Mobilis exactly when you are remote.

How do I keep my home number working in Algeria?

Leave your home SIM enabled for calls and SMS, and route mobile data to the eSIM. Switch off data roaming on the home line so background traffic cannot be billed, and remember that most travel profiles are data-only, so bank codes keep arriving on your own number.
