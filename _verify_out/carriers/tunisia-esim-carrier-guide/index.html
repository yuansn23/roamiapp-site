A week that stretches southwest — Tozeur, Kebili and the southern towns — is a routing problem, where knowing which operator holds the fringe signal matters.

Which Tunisia eSIM fits your trip?

Travellers to Tunisia overwhelmingly do one of two weeks, and the connectivity calculus differs sharply between them.

The coast week is a resort-and-medina circuit along the Mediterranean — dense towns, motorway corridors, reliable signal everywhere a tourist sleeps. The southern week runs southwest from the coast, where the towns spread out and the consumer networks thin out fast.

Does the operator choice matter in Tunisia?

Choosing an eSIM for Tunisia is easy on the coast and consequential in the south, because the two trips reward opposite things.

  • On the coast, every network works, so the decision is about eSIM plan length and price rather than operator

  • In the south, the decision is about reach, and a Tunisia eSIM that can fall back between carriers is worth more than a cheaper bundle

  • Between them, the motorway corridors behave like the coast, which is why a coast-only traveller never notices the difference

If you are still deciding whether your phone supports eSIM at all, start with the device compatibility checker and return here with a confirmed handset.

How do I avoid buying the wrong Tunisia data plan?

The most common mistake is buying a data plan sized and chosen for the wrong trip.

A coast visitor who buys the biggest package wastes money, because streaming, maps and photo uploads fit comfortably in a small allowance. A driver in the south who assumes constant coverage is running a genuine safety risk rather than a billing one.

Work out which trip you are taking before you spend a dinar, and the rest of this guide falls into place.

Tunisia coast week coverage: Tunis, Sidi Bou Said and Djerba

The classic northern circuit runs Tunis to the coast and back, and it is the trip 80 percent of visitors take.

In the capital, the medina, the Ville Nouvelle and the Carthage peninsula are all densely covered.

Farther south along the motorway, the resort belt takes over.

Tunis coverage: Sidi Bou Said and the Carthage peninsula

  • The medina and Avenue Habib Bourguiba sit in the densest coverage in the country

  • Sidi Bou Said, twenty minutes up the coast road, never drops signal

  • Carthage and the TGM light rail line are covered end to end

  • Day trips to Dougga, Cap Bon and Kairouan keep working on the same eSIM

Hammamet, Sousse, El Jem and Djerba coverage

Hammamet and Sousse are resort towns where hotel Wi-Fi is mediocre at best, and your own Tunisia eSIM is the dependable option.

Two stops deserve special mention.

El Jem’s Roman amphitheater sits in a mid-sized inland town that is fully covered despite being off the main coastal strip.

And Djerba, the island resort in the southeast, is reachable by the causeway at El Kantara or by air into Djerba-Zarzis airport — both arrival routes are covered, and the island’s resort zones hold strong 4G year-round.

Sizing the eSIM for the coast week is simple: 5 GB is comfortable, 10 GB is generous.

Between towns, the A1 motorway corridor stays connected for nearly its whole length, so navigation rarely goes dark.

Southern Tunisia coverage: Tozeur, Douz and the south

Southwest of Gafsa the network map changes. Tozeur, the palm-grove town on the southern circuit, is a proper town with full coverage.

From there, the itineraries fragment: the causeway toward Kebili, the old Berber villages around Tataouine and Medenine in the southeast, and the oasis town of Douz.

Data speeds in Tozeur, Douz and Kebili

Here is the useful surprise buried in Ookla’s regional data: the measured medians in the deep-south governorates are not bad at all.

Tozeur recorded 30.51 Mbps, Medenine 29.26, Kebili 27.58 and Tataouine 25.91 — respectable figures, because the measurements happen where people live, on roads and in towns.

What the medians cannot tell you is that those towns are islands.

Between them — on the long rural roads that link the southern towns — there is no signal of any kind, from any operator, at any price. No Tunisia eSIM changes that: the limitation is geographic, not commercial.

Mobile signal at Matmata and the southern day trips

The film locations are the reason many visitors push this far south, and they sit in two different connectivity situations.

  • Tozeur’s palm groves and the old town, reached by road, keep a signal for most of the day

  • Matmata and its underground hotels sit in a covered village

  • The roads beyond the last towns are the point where coverage stops entirely

  • Tour operators running deep-southern tours carry satellite phones for emergencies — rely on theirs, not yours

The rule for the week: treat every town as a connectivity island, download the entire south as an offline map area before leaving the coast, and treat any excursion beyond a marked road as a fully offline activity.

How Ooredoo, Orange and Tunisie Telecom differ for a visitor

Tunisia has three national operators, and unlike most markets in the region, the top-line speed race ended in a draw. In Ookla’s Speedtest Connectivity Report for Tunisia, H2 2024, there was no fastest mobile provider — no statistical difference between the three in download speed or video experience.

Ooredoo Tunisia: the strongest visitor-facing product

Ooredoo is the operator a visitor is most likely to end up on, and not only because of the numbers.

  • What it offers — the best mobile gaming score in the market at 80.02, which reflects low latency and therefore smoother video calls, plus the only visitor-oriented digital purchase flow in the country

  • Where it is strongest — Tunis and the capital region, where Ookla named it the fastest provider, and the coastal cities that carry most tourist traffic

  • The weakness — its edge is urban and latency-led rather than geographic; it is not the operator that holds signal longest on the desert fringe

The purchasing angle matters as much as the network.

Ooredoo runs an online eSIM pre-order: you request the profile on its website, select your arrival airport, complete identity verification online, and receive a QR code by email or at airport pickup. It works, but it needs a few days of lead time — it is not an instant fix on the jet bridge.

Orange Tunisia: coverage in the far south

Orange’s differentiator is the hardest to measure and the easiest to notice in the wrong place.

  • What it offers — broad availability across the main corridors, and a presence that travellers consistently report at the last towns before the wide-open south

  • Where it is strongest — the transitional zone between settled Tunisia and the empty south, where its cells tend to hold on longest

  • The weakness — its own eSIM products are aimed at residents through its own channels, and it sells no walk-up tourist version at the airports

For a coast-only trip, Orange is interchangeable with the other two. For a route that pushes toward Douz, Ksar Ghilane or the Algerian border, a profile that can attach to Orange is worth keeping in the list.

Tunisie Telecom: the most consistent network

The state operator won the one metric that predicts how a trip actually feels.

  • What it offers — the country’s best mobile consistency, with 87.6 percent of samples clearing the 5 Mbps down / 1 Mbps up threshold, plus the fastest fixed broadband in the market

  • Where it is strongest — the interior and secondary towns, and it was the fastest provider across eight governorates in Ookla’s regional table

  • The weakness — its eSIM product is built for residents, so a visitor cannot simply buy in; the practical route to its network is a travel eSIM that includes it

Treat that consistency figure as the closest thing this market has to a “does it work everywhere” measure, and it matters most outside the big cities.

Buying a Tunisia SIM at Tunis-Carthage arrivals

Tunis-Carthage (TUN) is the reliable option. In the arrivals area near baggage claim, Ooredoo operates a kiosk with Orange immediately alongside, and a basic tourist SIM costs around 20 dinars.

Bring your physical passport — registration against it is a legal requirement for every SIM sold in Tunisia, not a formality — and a payment card, because the airport kiosks generally do not deal in cash. Ten minutes, and you have a working local line.

Monastir airport SIM: why staffing tracks the flight schedule

Monastir (MIR), the charter gateway for the Sousse coast, is less dependable. Counter staffing tracks flight schedules and staff availability, and arrivals on quiet days sometimes find no one there.

If Monastir is your entry point, have a backup plan: an operator shop in town later the same day, or a travel eSIM already installed on the flight.

One rule applies at both airports.

Never buy from informal street vendors — every SIM must be registered to a passport, and unregistered cards get disconnected by the network, sometimes within days.

What are Tunisia’s mobile networks actually like?

Pull the national numbers together and Tunisia profiles as a mid-tier, steadily improving market. Split them by governorate and a few assumptions about strong and weak zones get rewritten.

How fast is mobile data in Tunisia?

The Speedtest Global Index put the country’s median mobile download at 50.79 Mbps in August 2026, ranked 80th in the world at 21 ms latency. Fixed broadband measured 22.54 Mbps.

On price, Cable.co.uk’s survey puts a gigabyte of Tunisian data at USD 1.28 — cheaper than the world average, though noticeably above neighbors Morocco (USD 0.63) and Algeria (USD 0.73).

DataReportal’s Digital 2025: Tunisia report counts 15.7 million mobile connections (128 percent of the population) and 10.5 million internet users (84.9 percent penetration), with median mobile speeds of 26.56 Mbps as of early 2025.

It is a connected country; the variability is geographic, not infrastructural.

What APN settings does my Tunisia eSIM need?

An access point name is the setting nobody thinks about until a data line goes quiet. Here is what it controls, when you actually need to type it, and where the menus sit on each platform.

What the APN (access point name) actually controls

The APN is the label your phone passes to the network so the operator knows which gateway should carry your traffic. It is routing information rather than a credential, and on a working eSIM it is completely invisible.

Get it wrong and the symptom is unmistakable: signal bars on screen, a live network registration, and zero connectivity. Nothing visible tells you the gateway is misconfigured, which is why it belongs near the top of any troubleshooting list.

Tunisia APN: when you must type the values yourself

Because a travel profile carries its configuration with it, nothing in Tunisia normally requires you to type access-point values. Ooredoo, Orange and Tunisie Telecom also push settings automatically to compatible handsets.

  • Manual entry is reserved for older devices paired with a locally bought SIM

  • A plan written into the phone with a typed code instead of a QR scan can need it too

  • A factory-reset handset, or a plan reinstalled after an error, can lose the values

Never invent the strings.

Your plan’s installation email, the operator’s support pages and any counter paperwork all carry the values, so take them from there and nowhere else. If a field is already filled in, leave it.

How do I enter the APN on an iPhone?

Open Settings, tap Cellular, tap the Tunisia line, then Cellular Data Network. Type the value into the APN field and leave username and password empty unless you were told otherwise.

Save the entry and reboot so the phone re-reads the gateway. If nothing improves, check that the edited line is the one handling mobile data.

How do I enter the APN on Android?

Open Settings, then Connections, then Mobile Networks, then Access Point Names. Add an entry, save it, and select it so the handset stops using the old one.

Labels move between manufacturers — some builds file the screen under More Networks, and others name it “APN” outright.

Reboot afterwards and re-check the data-line selection.

If the profile installs but still refuses to connect, work through the troubleshooting walkthrough before re-buying anything.

Local SIM vs travel eSIM costs

Two small things smooth out a Tunisian trip: knowing what a local line actually costs against a travel plan, and knowing how the money side works once you are there.

What does data cost in Tunisia?

A basic tourist SIM at Tunis-Carthage runs around 20 dinars, against a travel eSIM’s fixed upfront price — and the hidden costs are the passport registration, the queue and the single-network lock-in.

  • A local line gives you a Tunisian number, one network, and a recharge cycle you have to manage in cash

  • A travel eSIM gives you a fixed upfront price, automatic network selection and no shop visit

  • Per gigabyte, Tunisia sits at USD 1.28 against a USD 2.59 global average, so neither route is expensive by international standards

  • The difference is convenience, not headline price, for any trip under a month

Top-ups differ too.

A travel eSIM is renewed from the same account or app you bought it from, while a local prepaid line needs a recharge card or a kiosk visit.

What about cash and language in Tunisia?

The Tunisian dinar is a managed currency you obtain inside the country, and daily life runs on cash far more than card. Airport kiosks take cards, but town-side top-ups, taxis and most cafés want banknotes — keep a reserve.

Language runs the same way.

French and Arabic operate everything, and operator menus, registration forms and shop signage assume at least one of them. English is spoken in resort zones and by younger staff in the cities, but offline translation is worth having for anything bureaucratic.

Keeping your home number live while you travel

Keep your home line active for calls and banking SMS codes, and point mobile data at the Tunisia eSIM. Two settings prevent the classic mistakes.

  • The Tunisian line selected as the data line in your phone’s SIM settings

  • Verification codes keep arriving on your home number, because most travel eSIMs are data-only — which is exactly what you want

Tunisia eSIM questions and quick answers

Is your model approved for Tunisia eSIMs?

Almost certainly not as a blocker. Tunisia’s 4G runs on the common international bands — 1800 MHz (B3) and 2600 MHz (B7) in towns, 800 MHz (B20) for reach — which virtually every recent international handset covers. The 5G deployment uses 700 MHz and 3.5 GHz in city zones.

Any phone released in the last five years for the European market will work on a Tunisian eSIM.

Can I buy an eSIM directly from a Tunisian operator?

Ooredoo runs an online pre-order: request the profile on its site, pick your arrival airport, verify your identity, and collect the QR code by email or at pickup — allow several days. Orange and Tunisie Telecom aim their own eSIM products at residents, and neither offers a walk-up airport tourist version.

For instant delivery, the international travel route is simpler.

Can I make phone calls on a Tunisia eSIM?

No — travel eSIMs are data-only. Calls and SMS verification codes continue on your home SIM, and voice calls to Tunisia-based services run over data through WhatsApp, which is how most local businesses actually communicate anyway.

Which Tunisia eSIM suits your trip?

Coast-only: 5 to 8 GB. Coast plus southern circuit: the same 5 to 8 GB, because the southern days are largely offline — the constraint there is maps, not megabytes, and your eSIM allowance stretches further than you would expect. Add headroom if you tether a laptop or stream nightly.

Does an eSIM work on Chott el Djerid and the Kebili road?

On the paved causeway and in Kebili town, yes — the region’s 27.58 Mbps median reflects real service along the corridor, and an eSIM holds it without drama.

On the salt flat itself and any track leaving the road, assume none. Download offline maps for the whole southwest before leaving Tozeur or Douz.

What if my eSIM will not connect after landing?

Enable the line, confirm it is the selected data line, toggle data roaming on, then re-scan for networks manually. If it still fails, work through the ordered fixes in the troubleshooting guide and contact support with your order number and a screenshot, so a replacement profile can be issued if the original is faulty.

Which operator should I choose for the south?

If your plan lets you choose, treat reach as the priority rather than speed. Orange is the operator travellers most often report holding signal at the last towns before the wide-open south, and Tunisie Telecom is the consistency leader across the interior governorates. On a managed eSIM you do not choose at all — it attaches to whichever network is strongest where you stand.

How do I buy a SIM at Tunis-Carthage airport?

Around 20 dinars for a basic tourist SIM at the Ooredoo kiosk in the arrivals area, with Orange alongside.

Bring a payment card, because the airport counters generally do not take cash, and bring your physical passport, because registration against it is mandatory for every SIM sold in the country.

Do you need a passport to buy a SIM in Tunisia?

Yes, and since January 2025 European visitors need a passport specifically — national identity cards are no longer accepted at the border. That is convenient rather than annoying, because the document you need for any local SIM registration is the one you are carrying anyway.

Can I top up a Tunisia eSIM without a shop visit?

Yes. A travel eSIM is renewed from the same account or app you bought it from, which is the simplest route if you are moving between cities. A locally bought prepaid line instead needs a recharge card or a kiosk visit, paid in dinar cash.

Will my eSIM work on Djerba and at the beach resorts?

Yes. Djerba is reachable by the El Kantara causeway or by air into Djerba-Zarzis airport, both routes are covered, and the island’s resort zones hold strong 4G year-round.

The same applies along the Hammamet and Sousse belt, where your own data line is usually more dependable than hotel Wi-Fi.
