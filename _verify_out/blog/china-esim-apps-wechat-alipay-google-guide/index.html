The Great Firewall blocks Google, WhatsApp, Instagram, YouTube, Facebook, and hundreds of other services inside China. An international-routing eSIM keeps your usual apps working, but individual apps behave differently: Google Maps and WhatsApp work normally with routing; YouTube needs a plan with international routing; WeChat and Alipay work with any connection. This guide covers the essential apps every traveler needs installed before departure — exactly what works with Roami, what does not, and the alternative apps to use for each.

Google Maps with a China eSIM: Works on International Routing

Google Maps is the app most travelers miss first. With international routing through Hong Kong, navigation works exactly as it does at home. You land, open Maps to find your hotel, and the map tiles never load. On a local Chinese SIM or WiFi network, Google Maps is completely blocked. The network filtering blocks the API endpoints and tile servers that the app connects to.

With an international China eSIM: Google Maps works normally. Because your data routes through Hong Kong or Singapore before reaching the internet, the firewall never sees your Google Maps traffic. You can search addresses, get directions, check transit routes, and view street view — exactly as you would at home. A China eSIM with automatic carrier switching between China Mobile, Unicom, and Telecom maintains this consistent routing even as you move between buildings or cities where the strongest available network changes.

Practical limitations to know:

  • Address search in English works for major landmarks, hotels, and tourist attractions. For smaller venues, you’ll get better results using the Chinese name. Keep a translation app handy.
  • Transit directions work for subway and bus routes in major cities including Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, and Chengdu. However, Google Maps transit data for China is less detailed than what Apple Maps or local apps like Baidu Maps provide.
  • Navigation is usable but turn-by-turn voice guidance routes through Google’s servers, which adds a small latency penalty over the international routing path. It works, but it’s not as snappy as using Gaode Maps (AutoNavi) natively.
  • Offline maps — download these before you depart. Google Maps allows you to download city-level maps for offline use. Do this for every city you plan to visit. Even with a working eSIM, offline maps save data and provide a backup if the connection gets slow.

What about Apple Maps? Apple Maps is a strong alternative in China because Apple partners with Gaode (AutoNavi), a licensed Chinese mapping provider — Apple explains the partnership on its support site. Apple Maps works on Chinese networks without any special configuration. Transit directions, business listings, and navigation are all better than Google Maps within China. For most travelers, the combination of Google Maps on eSIM for familiar use and Apple Maps as a local backup is the best setup.

For most travelers, Google Maps on an international eSIM is the simplest navigation solution. The maps load instantly, turn-by-turn works reliably, and you never need to configure anything.

Summary: Google Maps works with international-routing eSIM. Does not work on local Chinese SIMs. Download offline maps before you go. Apple Maps works on any connection and is more accurate for Chinese addresses.

Gmail with a China eSIM: Works Reliably on International Routing

Gmail is accessible with an international eSIM. The connection to Google’s mail servers routes outside China, so authentication, message sending and receiving, and attachments all function normally.

Caveats:

  • Push notifications may be delayed by 30 seconds to a few minutes depending on the eSIM provider’s routing latency. This is noticeable but not debilitating for most travelers.
  • Two-factor authentication SMS won’t arrive on a data-only eSIM. If you use SMS-based 2FA for your Google account, keep your home SIM active for SMS reception (dual SIM mode) or switch to an authenticator app before traveling.
  • The Gmail app itself should be installed before you arrive in China. While Google Play is also blocked, the Gmail app will continue to function once installed — it doesn’t need Play Services for core email functionality.

Summary: Gmail works fully with China eSIM. Install the app before arrival. Set up authenticator-based 2FA before leaving.

Google Translate with a China eSIM: Download Offline Packs First

Can I use Google Translate with China eSIM? Yes, and you should. Google Translate is one of the most useful tools for China travel, and it works with an international-routing eSIM because its queries go through Google’s servers outside the firewall.

Before you arrive:

  1. Open Google Translate on WiFi at home.
  2. Tap the language indicator (shows “English” or whatever your source language is).
  3. Scroll down and tap “Offline Translation.”
  4. Download Chinese (Simplified) — this is a ~400 MB pack covering Mandarin.
  5. Also download Chinese (Traditional) if you plan to visit Hong Kong or Macau.
  6. Download your home language’s offline pack too.

With offline packs installed, Google Translate works even without any data connection. The camera translation feature (point your phone at a Chinese menu or sign) works offline for text within downloaded language packs — invaluable for reading restaurant menus, street signs, and product labels.

Why offline packs matter in China: Even with a working eSIM, data speeds through international routing can vary. In crowded areas, subway tunnels, or remote locations, the connection may be slow or drop entirely. Offline translation packs mean the app keeps working when the network does not.

Alternative: Baidu Translate and Pleco are excellent Chinese-focused translation apps that work on any network (they’re Chinese services, not blocked). Pleco is particularly good for character-by-character dictionary lookup and handwriting recognition. More on these in the alternative apps section.

Summary: Google Translate works with China eSIM. Download offline Chinese language pack before departure. Camera translation works offline for downloaded languages.

YouTube with a China eSIM: Needs International Routing

YouTube is fully blocked on Chinese networks. Google’s video platform is one of the most aggressively targeted services by mainland network filtering. On a local SIM or Chinese WiFi, the app won’t load videos, the website redirects to an error page, and embedded YouTube players on other sites also fail.

With an international-routing eSIM: YouTube works. Streaming video at 720p and 1080p is generally smooth on 4G/LTE connections through Hong Kong-routed eSIMs. 4K streaming may buffer due to the additional hop latency.

What you should know:

  • YouTube Music also works on international routing. Download your playlists for offline playback before departure to save data.
  • YouTube Premium offline downloads — download videos before you leave. This is the most reliable way to watch YouTube in China because offline playback doesn’t depend on your connection being routed correctly at all.
  • YouTube Kids works the same as the main app on international routing.

Alternatives: Bilibili is the closest Chinese equivalent to YouTube, with a massive library of user-generated content, educational videos, and anime. Douyin (the Chinese version of TikTok) fills the short-video niche. Both are covered in the alternative apps section.

Summary: YouTube works with international-routing eSIM. Blocked on local networks. Download videos offline before departure as a backup.

Google Search with a China eSIM: Use Bing.cn as a Replacement

Google Search (google.com and all regional domains) is blocked in China. This includes Google Images, Google News, Google Scholar, and Google Flights. On a local Chinese network or SIM, all of these return connection errors.

With an international-routing eSIM: Google Search works, but with a practical caveat — every search query makes a round trip outside China, which adds 200-600ms of latency. Simple text searches are fine, but image-heavy results can load slowly.

The practical approach for most travelers: Use Bing.com/cn (Bing China) as your primary search engine while in China. Bing is not blocked and provides excellent results in both English and Chinese. Bing Image Search and Bing Maps are also available. Microsoft has a partnership with Baidu that gives Bing decent coverage of Chinese-language content.

Other alternatives:

  • Baidu (baidu.com) — China’s dominant search engine. Essential for finding Chinese-language content, business listings, and local information. The English-language results are poor; use Bing for English queries and Baidu for Chinese ones.
  • DuckDuckGo — intermittently blocked. It works occasionally but should not be relied upon.

Summary: Google Search blocked on all networks. Works with international routing but slow. Use Bing.com/cn as primary search. Use Baidu for Chinese-language queries.

WhatsApp with a China eSIM: Voice, Video, and Messages

WhatsApp compatibility is one of the top concerns for travelers heading to China, and WhatsApp is one of the most reliable Western apps in China when you have the right eSIM setup. Because WhatsApp uses end-to-end encryption and routes messages through Meta’s servers, an international-routing eSIM keeps the entire communication pathway outside mainland network filtering.

What works:

  • Text messages — send and receive instantly.
  • Voice calls — clear and reliable on 4G/LTE. Call quality depends on your eSIM provider’s bandwidth. Roami’s automatic carrier switching helps maintain stable call quality by connecting to the strongest available Chinese network.
  • Video calls — work well on international routing. Expect 720p quality on mobile data. WiFi calling through your eSIM also works.
  • Media sharing — photos, videos, and documents all send and receive normally.
  • Group chats — function perfectly.
  • WhatsApp Web — works when your phone maintains the connection.

What does not work:

  • WhatsApp on local Chinese WiFi — if you connect to hotel WiFi or a cafe network, WhatsApp will be blocked unless you also have a VPN active on that connection. Your eSIM data is the reliable path.
  • WhatsApp backups to Google Drive — require Google services which may be blocked. Back up manually before you leave China.

Setup note: Install WhatsApp before you arrive. While the app continues to work if installed beforehand, downloading it fresh from the app store is impossible once you are inside China (the app stores themselves are accessible, but WhatsApp’s download pages are blocked). If you need to reinstall mid-trip, use an eSIM plan with international routing to download it.

Summary: WhatsApp works fully with China eSIM. Voice and video calls are reliable. Switch to eSIM data when using public WiFi.

Instagram with a China eSIM: Works on International Routing

Instagram loads, refreshes, and posts normally when connected through an international-routing eSIM. The Feed, Stories, Reels, DMs, and Explore tab all function.

Practical notes:

  • Reels may buffer on slower connections. Instagram video compression is heavy, so this is usually fine even at 3G speeds.
  • Story uploads go through smoothly but may take 10-20 seconds for video stories.
  • Direct Messages work in real time.
  • Instagram on Chinese WiFi — blocked. Same dynamic as WhatsApp: your eSIM data path works, but hotel and cafe WiFi will block Instagram.

Summary: Instagram works fully with international-routing China eSIM. Blocked on local WiFi. No special setup needed.

Facebook and Messenger with a China eSIM

Facebook’s core app and Messenger both function normally on an international-routing eSIM. News Feed, notifications, Marketplace, and Groups all load.

Messenger-specific notes:

  • Voice and video calls through Messenger work well on international routing. Call quality is comparable to WhatsApp.
  • Messenger SMS integration — if you use Messenger as your default SMS app, those messages route through your phone’s cellular connection (your home SIM), not through the eSIM. Standard SMS does not pass through eSIM data.

Facebook Lite is worth installing for China travel. It uses less data and loads faster on connections that may have higher latency due to international routing.

Summary: Facebook and Messenger work with China eSIM. Install before arrival. Blocked on Chinese WiFi.

WeChat: Setting Up and Using China’s Essential App with a Foreign Number

WeChat (Weixin in Chinese) isn’t optional for China travel. WeChat users can register with a foreign phone number without needing a Chinese SIM. It’s the primary communication tool for locals, the payment method for almost all merchants, and the gateway to restaurant reservations, train tickets, museum bookings, and taxi rides. If you only install one app for China, WeChat is it.

Setting Up WeChat with a Foreign Phone Number WeChat registration with a non-Chinese number has become easier in recent years, but it still requires a verification step that trips up many travelers.

Step-by-step:

  1. Download WeChat from your app store before arriving in China. (WeChat’s official download page lists all supported platforms.) The app is available globally; download it at home so you don’t need to use data for the initial download.
  2. Open the app and tap “Register.”
  3. Enter your country code and mobile number (your home number, not your eSIM — WeChat needs a number that can receive SMS).
  4. WeChat sends a verification code via SMS. This code goes to your home SIM, not your eSIM. If you have dual SIM active (home SIM for SMS, eSIM for data), this works seamlessly.
  5. If the SMS does not arrive (a common issue with some non-Chinese carriers), tap “Call Me” instead. WeChat will place an automated voice call to your number and read the verification code.
  6. After SMS or voice verification, WeChat asks you to have an existing user scan a QR code to “help verify your account.” This is a anti-abuse measure. You can ask a friend who already has WeChat to scan the code, or you can skip this step on some newer versions — try tapping “Skip” or “Unable to Scan” first.
  7. Set your profile picture and name. You are in.

If the QR verification is required and you don’t know anyone with WeChat: Some travelers report success registering after arriving in China and connecting to a hotel WiFi or eSIM connection. The verification requirement appears to be IP-dependent. If you try at home and hit the QR roadblock, wait until you land and try again.

Critical note on phone number: Once registered, don’t switch your WeChat-linked phone number unless absolutely necessary. Changing the linked number later requires SMS verification to the new number, and WeChat support for foreign accounts is minimal. The safest approach is to register with your permanent home number. For a deeper breakdown of how phone number verification works for travel eSIMs in China, see the guide to China eSIM phone number registration.

WeChat Pay with a Foreign Credit Card WeChat Pay is where most travelers hit friction. WeChat Pay (the payment feature inside WeChat) historically required a Chinese bank account and a Chinese ID number. In 2024 and 2025, Tencent relaxed these requirements significantly under pressure from the Chinese government to make the platform more accessible to international visitors.

Current status (2026):

  • You can link a Visa, Mastercard, JCB, Diners Club, or Discover credit card to WeChat Pay.
  • The foreign card must be linked from within the WeChat app: Go to Me > Services > WeChat Pay > Wallet > Cards > Add a Card.
  • Fees: Foreign card transactions incur a roughly 3% fee plus a fixed per-transaction charge. This is higher than using Alipay with a foreign card, which is covered in the next section.
  • Limits: Single transaction limit for foreign cards varies but is typically around $830 USD. Daily and monthly caps also apply.
  • What you cannot do with a foreign card: Send money to other users (“red packets” or person-to-person transfers), top up your WeChat balance with a foreign card, or use WeChat Pay for certain regulated services like wealth management products.

Practical advice: Use WeChat Pay for small transactions — convenience stores, street food, taxi fares. Use Alipay for larger purchases and restaurant bills, as Alipay’s foreign card integration is more mature and charges lower fees.

Mini-Programs That Need a Chinese Number WeChat Mini-Programs are lightweight apps that run inside WeChat. Many essential China travel services operate as Mini-Programs, and a subset of them require a Chinese phone number for registration.

Mini-Programs that work with a foreign number:

  • Meituan (food delivery and restaurant booking) — mostly works with a foreign number.
  • Didi Chuxing (ride-hailing inside WeChat) — the Didi Mini-Program may ask for a Chinese number. Use the standalone DiDi Global Edition app instead (covered in the Uber/Didi section).
  • Ctrip/Trip.com (hotel and flight booking) — works with a foreign number.

Mini-Programs that require a Chinese number:

  • Railway 12306 (train ticket booking) — register with your passport and finish verification before you fly; Trip.com is a faster alternative if you want to skip the wait.
  • Some hospital registration Mini-Programs — require Chinese ID. Use your hotel concierge or the Trip.com app for medical appointment booking.

Verdict: WeChat is non-negotiable for China travel — you simply can’t function without it. Even if you only use it for communication and nothing else, it’s the app every local, restaurant, and service expects you to have. The setup friction (foreign number registration, QR verification) is annoying but worth pushing through before you depart.

Summary: WeChat is essential. Register with your home number before departure. Link a foreign credit card for payments (higher fees than Alipay). Some Mini-Programs require a Chinese number; use standalone apps as alternatives.

Alipay: Setup with Foreign Card and the Tour Card Feature

Alipay is the more foreigner-friendly of China’s two dominant payment apps. Travelers using Alipay benefit from the Tour Card feature which accepts foreign credit cards. Ant Group (Alipay’s parent company) has invested significantly in international visitor support, and in 2026, Alipay is the easiest way for travelers to pay for almost everything in China.

Setting Up Alipay with a Foreign Credit Card Alipay allows you to link international Visa, Mastercard, and American Express cards directly. The process is simpler than WeChat Pay and the fees are lower.

Step-by-step:

  1. Download Alipay from your app store. The international version (not the Chinese-only AlipayCN) is available globally. The official Alipay site lets you verify which version is correct for your market. The icon is blue with a white Chinese character and the developer is “Alipay.com.”
  2. Open the app and tap “Create Account.” Enter your email address or mobile number (your home number works).
  3. You receive a verification code via SMS. Use your home SIM for this, just like with WeChat.
  4. After account creation, tap “Me” > “Bank Cards” > “Add Bank Card.”
  5. Enter your international Visa, Mastercard, or Amex details.
  6. Alipay makes a small temporary charge (typically $1-2 USD) to verify the card, then refunds it.

What works: Paying at stores by scanning the merchant’s QR code, or having the merchant scan your payment QR code. Online payments within apps like Trip.com, Fliggy (Alibaba’s travel platform), and Ele.me (food delivery).

What does not work: Sending money to other Alipay users, topping up your Alipay balance with a foreign card, and some government-related payments.

Fees: Foreign card transactions incur a 2-3% fee depending on the card network, capped at a lower per-transaction maximum than WeChat Pay. Some promotional periods waive the fee entirely.

The Alipay Tour Card Feature The Tour Card is Alipay’s best feature for international travelers. It is a prepaid digital card that lets you load money from your foreign card and spend it within China without per-transaction foreign card fees.

How it works:

  1. In the Alipay app, search for “Tour Card” or go to “Me” > “Tour Card.”
  2. You are issued a prepaid digital card (powered by Shanghai Bank, Mastercard network).
  3. Transfer money from your foreign card to the Tour Card. The exchange rate is set at the time of transfer and is close to the mid-market rate.
  4. Spend the Tour Card balance at any merchant that accepts Alipay. There are no per-transaction foreign fees because you are spending from the prepaid balance, not directly from your foreign card.
  5. Unused balance can be transferred back to your foreign card when you leave, minus a small fee (typically 2-5 USD).

Tour Card limits (2026): Minimum top-up is roughly $28. Maximum balance is about $1,380. You can top up multiple times.

Why use Tour Card over direct card linking: The Tour Card eliminates per-transaction foreign fees. If you plan to make many small purchases (coffees, street food, metro tickets), the savings add up quickly. For large purchases like hotel bills or train tickets, direct card linking is more convenient because you don’t need to pre-load funds.

Summary: Alipay is the most foreigner-friendly payment app. Link a foreign card directly for convenience. Use the Tour Card for fee-free small transactions. Alipay works on any internet connection — no special eSIM routing needed.

Uber and DiDi: Ride-Hailing in China

DiDi Global Edition (English UI) DiDi Chuxing is China’s dominant ride-hailing platform, operating in over 400 cities. The company acquired Uber’s China operations in 2016, so Uber-branded cars in China no longer exist. However, DiDi offers an “International” or “Global Edition” version of its app designed specifically for foreign travelers.

DiDi Global Edition features:

  • English-language interface throughout — every button, menu, and notification is in English.
  • Foreign credit card payment — link your Visa or Mastercard directly. No Chinese bank account or Alipay needed.
  • Driver communication — in-app translated chat lets you message your driver in English; they see it in Chinese and their replies translate back.
  • Estimated fares shown upfront before you confirm the ride.
  • Available in: Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Chengdu, Hangzhou, Nanjing, Chongqing, and dozens more cities.

How to set up DiDi Global Edition:

  1. Download the “DiDi” app from the App Store or Google Play. The icon is a black background with a white “Di” character. Make sure it is the Global Edition — the Chinese version (marked “DiDi Rider” in Chinese) doesn’t have the English interface.
  2. Register with your foreign phone number (your home SIM number). Verification SMS arrives on your home line.
  3. Link a credit card: Visa, Mastercard, or Amex supported.
  4. Set your pickup location and destination. The app uses Apple Maps or Google Maps depending on your OS.
  5. Confirm the fare and request the ride.
  6. When the driver accepts, you see their plate number, car model, and estimated arrival time.

Pricing: DiDi in China is significantly cheaper than Uber in most Western countries. A 15-minute ride in central Beijing typically costs $3-6 USD. During surge pricing (rain, rush hour, late night), prices can double but remain reasonable by Western standards.

Uber with International Routing The Uber app technically works in China with an international-routing eSIM. You can open the app, set a pickup location, and see available cars. However, because Uber China was absorbed into DiDi, there are very few Uber-branded cars actually active. In major cities like Shanghai, you might find a handful of Uber vehicles operating, but wait times are long and prices are higher than DiDi.

Practical advice: Install Uber as a backup if you already have an account, but use DiDi Global Edition as your primary ride-hailing app. The DiDi experience is smoother, cheaper, and more widely available.

Can I use Uber Didi with China eSIM? Yes, both work. DiDi Global Edition is the recommended primary app because it is designed for foreign travelers and has the widest coverage. Uber works but has limited car availability.

Summary: Use DiDi Global Edition (English UI, foreign card support). Uber works technically but has very few cars. Both function on any internet connection including China eSIM.

Google Drive, Google Photos, and Google Docs

Google’s productivity apps work with international routing but have practical limitations in China.

Google Drive: File uploads and downloads work. Syncing can be slow due to the latency overhead of international routing. Do not rely on real-time collaboration in Google Docs — the latency makes typing feel sluggish.

Google Photos: Backup works when connected via eSIM, but it will use significant data. A day of photos might consume 200-500 MB of upload. If your eSIM plan has a data cap (most do), either delay backups until you are on a connection outside mainland networks, or wait until you leave China.

Google Docs/Sheets/Slides: Work for editing. Offline mode (save documents for offline editing) is highly recommended. Enable offline access for all critical documents before departure.

Summary: Google productivity tools work but are slow on international routing. Enable offline mode before departure. Do data-heavy tasks on a connection outside mainland networks.

Alternative Apps to Download Before China

No matter how good your eSIM setup is, some services remain blocked or are simply better served by local alternatives. Download these apps before you leave — ideally on WiFi — so they’re ready when you need them.

Maps.me and Apple Maps (Google Maps Alternative) You already know Google Maps works with Roami, but having a backup mapping app is wise.

Maps.me is the best offline map app for China travel. Download city maps before departure for Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Chengdu, and any other cities you plan to visit. Maps.me provides turn-by-turn navigation, points of interest, and walking/driving directions — all without an internet connection. The map data is sourced from OpenStreetMap and is reasonably accurate for major Chinese cities.

Apple Maps works on any connection in China because Apple uses Gaode (AutoNavi) as its data provider. Transit directions are excellent. Business hours, phone numbers, and user reviews for Chinese businesses are more complete than what Google Maps offers. If you have an iPhone, Apple Maps should be your primary navigation app in China for day-to-day use.

Baidu Maps is the most detailed mapping app for China but has a Chinese-only interface. Download it if you read Chinese or are willing to navigate a non-English UI.

Bing (Google Search Alternative)

Bing.com/cn — as discussed in the Google Search section — is the best English-language search engine accessible on any Chinese network. Install the Bing app or just use the mobile website. Bing’s image search, news aggregation, and map features all work well on Chinese networks.

Baidu Translate and Pleco (Translation Alternatives)

Baidu Translate is a strong alternative to Google Translate. It handles Chinese text exceptionally well, supports camera translation, and works on any network since it’s a Chinese service. The English-to-Chinese translation quality is comparable to Google Translate, and for Chinese-to-English, it sometimes produces more natural results because the AI models are trained on Chinese language patterns.

Pleco is the essential app for anyone who wants to understand written Chinese. It is primarily a dictionary app with features that go far beyond Google Translate:

  • Character recognition — draw a character with your finger and Pleco identifies it.
  • Optical character recognition — point your camera at Chinese text and Pleco overlays translations.
  • Stroke order animations — shows how to write each character.
  • Flashcard system — for travelers who want to learn basic phrases.
  • Full offline mode — all dictionaries work without internet.

Install Pleco before departure. The free version includes the core dictionary and OCR features. The paid add-ons ($5-15 USD) add more dictionary sources and expanded OCR.

Bilibili and Douyin (YouTube Alternative)

Bilibili is the closest Chinese equivalent to YouTube. It hosts a massive library of content: educational videos, vlogs, music, gaming, anime, tech reviews, and documentaries. Some content creators upload the same videos to both YouTube and Bilibili. The iOS and Android apps are available on their respective app stores globally.

Douyin is the Chinese version of TikTok — separate from TikTok International, which is also available in China but has less local content. Douyin is focused on short-form video, live streaming, and e-commerce. It is immensely popular in China and provides a window into local culture and trends.

Both apps are fully accessible on any Chinese network. Neither requires a VPN or international routing.

Topping Up Your eSIM Plan

Can I top up China eSIM plan? Yes, and you should know how before you need to. Running out of data in a country where Google Maps is blocked on local networks and you can’t easily buy a top-up card at a convenience store isn’t a situation you want to find yourself in.

When to Top Up Most eSIM plans for China are data-only and have a fixed validity period (7 days, 15 days, 30 days). You can usually top up at any point during the active period. Here is when you should consider it:

  • Before you leave home — if your plan is 7 days and your trip is 10 days, buy a 15-day plan upfront. Top-ups are usually more expensive per GB than the initial plan.
  • Mid-trip — most eSIM providers send a warning at 20%, 10%, and 5% remaining data. Set a phone reminder at 50% to check your balance daily.
  • Hotspot sharing — if you plan to share your connection with a laptop or tablet, double your data estimate. Tethering through an eSIM uses the same data pool.

How Top-Ups Work The process varies slightly by provider, but the pattern is consistent:

  1. Open the provider’s app (or website) on your phone.
  2. Navigate to your active plan.
  3. Select “Top Up” or “Add Data.”
  4. Choose a data add-on (typically 1 GB, 3 GB, or 5 GB increments).
  5. Pay with your credit card or PayPal. The top-up data is added to your existing plan immediately.

Most providers require an internet connection to process the top-up. If your data is completely exhausted and you have no WiFi, you can’t top up. Avoid this by topping up before you hit zero. If you do get stuck, many hotels in China offer free WiFi — connect to that (apps like WhatsApp won’t work on it, but the top-up page will load) and process the top-up there.

Some providers now offer auto-top-up, which adds a pre-defined data package automatically when your balance drops below a threshold. Enable this before departure if you anticipate heavy data use. Why App Compatibility Differs by eSIM Provider

Not all China eSIMs are created equal when it comes to app compatibility. The key variable is whether your eSIM provider routes traffic internationally or hands you a local Chinese IP address.

International routing eSIMs — Your data travels from your phone to the Chinese carrier’s tower, then to the provider’s servers in Hong Kong, Singapore, or another location outside mainland China. From there, it reaches the open internet. The network filtering inspects traffic entering China, not traffic leaving it, so your outbound requests to Google, Meta, and other blocked services are not filtered. This is the type of eSIM you need for app compatibility.

Local IP eSIMs — Some eSIMs (particularly those sold as “local China eSIMs” from Chinese carriers directly) give you a Chinese IP address. Your traffic goes through mainland network filtering normally, and blocked apps remain blocked. These eSIMs are cheaper but require a separate VPN to access Western services.

The Roami difference: Roami eSIMs use automatic carrier switching that connects you to the best available Chinese network (China Mobile, China Unicom, or China Telecom) while maintaining international routing through Hong Kong. This means Google Maps, WhatsApp, Instagram, and YouTube work regardless of which carrier has the strongest signal in your current location. In practice, this matters most when moving between cities or entering buildings where different carriers have different coverage quality.
