Spain’s Four Mobile Networks at a Glance

Before diving into individual network strengths, it helps to understand how Spain’s telecom market is structured and what each operator offers at a fundamental level. The Comisión Nacional de los Mercados y la Competencia (CNMC) regulates all four networks and publishes quarterly reports on coverage obligations and subscriber counts. Spain’s mobile market has undergone significant consolidation over the past decade, with the acquisition of smaller players by the major groups and the emergence of dozens of MVNOs that ride on the infrastructure of the four main networks. According to the GSMA, Spain ranks among the top European countries for 4G availability and has seen rapid 5G adoption since the first spectrum auctions in 2021.

Network Coverage Overview

Operator Coverage Strength Speed Profile Best Known For

Movistar operates the most extensive physical infrastructure, having inherited Telefónica’s legacy copper and fibre network. Orange and Vodafone rely on their own infrastructure in major cities but negotiate roaming agreements with Movistar in rural zones. Yoigo began life in 2006 as an MVNO running on Movistar’s network but has since built out its own radio access network; today it sits within MASORANGE, the group created by the 2024 merger of Orange Spain and MásMóvil.

Each network uses different frequency bands that affect building penetration and range. Movistar’s 800 MHz band (4G) provides superior indoor and rural reach. Orange and Vodafone lean on the 1800 MHz and 2100 MHz bands for urban capacity, while Yoigo layers its own holdings with capacity from the wider MASORANGE portfolio. For 5G, all four operators deploy on the 3.5 GHz band in cities, and Movistar, Orange, and Vodafone also hold 700 MHz licences won in Spain’s July 2021 spectrum auction, which they use for broader 5G coverage — a band that Yoigo’s predecessor MásMóvil notably declined to bid for.

Key differences in network philosophy:

  • Movistar builds for maximum geographic coverage, prioritising rural and motorway connectivity.
  • Orange invests in urban capacity and speed, with dense small-cell deployment in city centres.
  • Vodafone balances coastal coverage with inland city performance, given its subscriber base along the Mediterranean arc.
  • Yoigo competes on price with “good enough” coverage in urban and suburban areas.

The practical takeaway is that no single network is the best choice for every traveller. Your destination, itinerary, and data usage patterns will determine which operator suits you. This is why multi-network Spain eSIM solutions that can switch between carriers are gaining popularity among international visitors. If you’re planning to drive through multiple regions, a Spain eSIM with auto-switching capabilities eliminates the need to choose a single carrier before your trip.

Understanding Spain’s MVNO Ecosystem

Beyond the four major operators, Spain has one of Europe’s most active MVNO markets with over 50 active virtual operators. Brands like Simyo, Jazztel, Amena, Pepephone, Lowi, and MásMóvil offer budget plans using the infrastructure of one of the big four. For travellers, the existence of these MVNOs means you can often find cheaper prepaid eSIM options than the main brand rates, but with an important caveat: MVNO subscribers receive lower data priority on congested cells. During peak hours in busy tourist areas, an MVNO plan on Orange’s network may deliver noticeably slower speeds than a direct Orange-branded plan, even though both use the same physical towers.

5G Rollout Status and What It Means for Travellers

Spain’s 5G rollout has progressed rapidly. All four operators had launched commercial 5G services by mid-2023, and the 700 MHz licences allocated in the July 2021 auction enabled the wider low-band coverage that followed. As of mid-2026, 5G reaches more than 99% of Spain’s population according to the government’s 2025 broadband coverage report, including all provincial capitals and most medium-sized cities. The 3.5 GHz band provides the high speeds associated with 5G but has limited range and poor building penetration, while the newer 700 MHz deployments offer broader coverage at lower speeds. For travellers, this means you will experience 5G in city centres, transport hubs, and major tourist zones, but you should expect to drop to 4G in suburban and rural areas on any network.

The practical impact of 5G on your travel experience is currently marginal. 4G in Spain already delivers speeds sufficient for HD video streaming, video calls, and navigation. 5G becomes relevant if you need to upload large files, participate in high-quality video conferences, or stream 4K content. For most tourists, the difference between a 4G and 5G connection is not noticeable in everyday use.

Movistar eSIM: Best Coverage in Rural Spain

Movistar, the commercial brand of Telefónica, is Spain’s longest-running telecommunications operator and the one with the most extensive physical network. If your travels take you beyond city limits into the Spanish countryside, national parks, or smaller inland pueblos, Movistar is consistently the most reliable option. Telefónica’s historical position as the former state monopoly gave it a head start in infrastructure deployment that none of its competitors have fully closed, particularly in low-population-density areas where the economics of building new towers are unfavourable.

Rural and Motorway Coverage

Movistar covers virtually the entire Spanish population with 4G, and its landmass coverage — the share of territory with some form of mobile data signal — leads every rival by a wide margin, based on the coverage data carriers report to CNMC. Orange and Vodafone concentrate their infrastructure where people live, so their geographic reach is noticeably thinner. The gap is most noticeable in regions such as:

  • Castilla y León: Sparse population density means Orange and Vodafone rely on Movistar roaming agreements. Outside Valladolid, Salamanca, and Burgos, Movistar provides the only consistent signal. On the A-62 between Burgos and the Portuguese border, Movistar holds a steady 4G signal along nearly the whole route, while Orange fades in and out across long stretches.
  • Extremadura: Rural routes between Cáceres and Mérida frequently drop to 3G or no signal on non-Movistar networks. The N-430 and N-432 secondary roads that connect Extremadura to Andalusia are Movistar territory. The A-66 between Mérida and Seville is better served by all operators, but depart from the main autovía and the gap widens immediately.
  • Aragón: The Pyrenean foothills and the Teruel province have patchy coverage from all operators, but Movistar maintains usable 4G in more locations. The A-23 motorway from Zaragoza to Teruel keeps Movistar 4G along most of the route, while Orange drops to 3G in multiple sections.
  • Galicia: Coastal towns are well covered by all networks, but inland routes near Lugo and Ourense favour Movistar. The A-6 motorway from Lugo to the Asturias border shows a consistent pattern: Movistar maintains 4G while Orange and Vodafone flicker between 4G and 3G, particularly in the mountainous sections near El Acebo.

On motorways and major roads (autovías and autopistas), Movistar’s coverage gap versus Orange narrows considerably. All four operators invest in corridor coverage because of competitive pressure and EU universal service obligations. However, Movistar maintains an edge on secondary roads (carreteras secundarias) where population density drops below 50 people per square kilometre.

Urban Performance

In cities, Movistar is rarely the fastest network. Independent speed tests from Madrid, Barcelona, Valencia, and Seville consistently place Orange ahead on average download speed, with the gap most pronounced in dense urban canyons and during peak hours in metro stations and shopping districts. Movistar prioritises coverage breadth over raw speed, which means you will almost always have a signal, but it may not be the fastest one available.

On Madrid’s Gran Vía during peak hours, congestion matters more than headline speed on any network. In Barcelona’s Gothic Quarter, where narrow streets and historic stone buildings create challenging propagation conditions, Movistar often holds a more reliable connection despite lower top speeds. This illustrates the trade-off: Movistar trades peak speed for consistency, which matters more in difficult coverage environments.

Orange eSIM: Balanced Performance Across Spanish Cities

Orange España, owned by the French Orange Group, operates Spain’s second-largest mobile infrastructure. Where Movistar leads in breadth, Orange leads in speed and urban density. The network consistently posts the fastest average download speeds in independent tests conducted by nPerf and Ookla. Orange’s investment strategy has focused on dense urban capacity rather than broad geographic coverage, which makes it the best choice for city-based travel.

City-by-City Performance

Orange’s dense small-cell deployment gives it a measurable speed advantage in Spain’s largest cities. Independent crowd-sourced measurements from Ookla and nPerf consistently put Orange at or near the top of median download rankings in the biggest metros, with Movistar and Vodafone typically within a modest margin and Yoigo trailing in every market. The typical pattern across major cities:

Málaga Vodafone strong on coastal capacity; Orange fast in the centre

Orange’s advantage is most noticeable in high-density environments: metro stations during rush hour, major shopping streets like Calle de Preciados in Madrid or Passeig de Gràcia in Barcelona, and large public events where thousands of users contend for bandwidth simultaneously. Orange deploys carrier aggregation and higher-order MIMO more aggressively than its competitors in city-centre cells, which translates into a visible throughput advantage in congested scenarios.

Speed Test Result Highlights:

  • Orange leads the median download rankings in most large Spanish cities, with the biggest margins in Valencia and Madrid.
  • The gap between Orange and Movistar narrows in Andalusian cities, where Vodafone’s coastal capacity keeps it competitive.
  • Vodafone’s speeds are remarkably consistent from city to city — rarely the fastest, rarely the slowest.
  • Yoigo trails the big three in every measured market, though the gap is smallest off-peak.

For travellers who need to upload photos, stream video, or participate in video calls, the speed advantage of Orange and Vodafone over Movistar and Yoigo becomes noticeable. For basic navigation and messaging, all four networks are adequate in city centres.

Indoor Coverage Considerations

Where Orange falls slightly behind Movistar is indoor penetration in buildings constructed with thick stone walls, which are common in older Spanish city centres. The 1800 MHz and 2100 MHz bands that Orange relies on for capacity do not penetrate thick masonry as effectively as Movistar’s 800 MHz spectrum. In ground-floor apartments, basement restaurants, and historic buildings with metre-thick walls, Orange can drop to 4G with reduced signal bars while Movistar holds a stronger connection. This is a niche concern for most tourists but worth noting if you plan to work remotely from older apartment buildings in cities like Toledo, Salamanca, or Granada’s Albaicín quarter.

In modern buildings and newer construction, the indoor performance gap between Orange and Movistar narrows to near-zero. If you are staying in a contemporary hotel, apartment complex, or co-working space with modern construction materials, you will not notice a difference. The issue is specific to old-town accommodation in historic city centres.

Orange’s MVNO Ecosystem

Orange operates several MVNO brands in Spain, including Simyo, Jazztel Móvil, and Amena, which use Orange’s infrastructure at lower price points. These brands offer cheaper prepaid plans but with reduced priority on congested cells. For a direct Orange Spain eSIM tourist experience, you want the primary Orange brand rather than one of its discount sub-brands, as full-speed data priority makes a measurable difference during peak network hours. Simyo plans, for example, are routinely cheaper than equivalent Orange-branded plans, but deprioritisation can make them noticeably slower than the main brand during evening peak periods in central Madrid.

Orange’s 5G Standalone Network

Orange has been among the most aggressive Spanish operators in deploying 5G standalone (SA), which brings lower latency and more stable performance in crowded areas. For travellers, 5G SA means faster reconnection after signal loss (such as emerging from a metro tunnel) and steadier speeds at large events. As of mid-2026, Orange’s SA coverage concentrates in central Madrid and Barcelona with expansion continuing, though for everyday travel tasks the difference between SA and non-standalone 5G is minor.

Vodafone Spain: Strong Along Coastal Routes

Vodafone Spain, now under new ownership following its 2024 acquisition by Zegona Communications, serves roughly a fifth of Spanish mobile subscribers. The Zegona acquisition marked a significant shift in Vodafone Spain’s strategy, moving from a group-owned subsidiary to a locally managed operator focused on profitability and network efficiency. The network’s historical strength lies along Spain’s Mediterranean coast, from the Costa Brava down to the Costa del Sol, as well as the Balearic and Canary Islands.

Coastal and Tourist Zone Performance

Vodafone invested heavily in 5G deployment along tourist corridors during the 2022–2025 period, and that investment is visible in real-world performance data. The result is excellent coverage in resort areas:

  • Costa del Sol: Marbella, Málaga city, Nerja, and Estepona all have strong Vodafone 4G/5G with consistently fast average speeds, and coverage along the coastal N-340 road is close to continuous — making Vodafone the best pick for driving this route.
  • Costa Blanca: Alicante, Benidorm, and Torrevieja are well-served with Vodafone’s coastal small cells. Benidorm, with its dense high-rise hotel district, benefits from Vodafone’s investment in vertical coverage solutions that provide strong signals on upper floors.
  • Costa Brava: Blanes, Lloret de Mar, and Tossa de Mar have Vodafone coverage that matches or slightly exceeds Orange in peak summer months when tourist numbers strain network capacity.
  • Balearic Islands: Mallorca, Menorca, Ibiza, and Formentera all have Vodafone 4G coverage on major beaches and resort areas. Vodafone’s 5G reaches Palma, Ibiza Town, and Mahón. On Menorca, Vodafone’s coverage extends to more of the remote calas than Orange or Movistar.
  • Canary Islands: Vodafone covers Tenerife, Gran Canaria, Lanzarote, and Fuerteventura with consistent 4G in tourist zones and 5G in major resort centres like Playa de las Américas, Maspalomas, and Puerto del Carmen. On Tenerife, Vodafone maintains coverage on the TF-21 road up to the Teide National Park visitor centre at 2,356 metres, which is a useful advantage for hikers.

Inland Urban Performance

Vodafone’s inland urban performance is competitive but not market-leading. In Madrid and Barcelona it typically posts average speeds between Movistar and Orange, and its 5G deployment reaches most large urban populations. In secondary cities like Zaragoza, Valladolid, and Pamplona, Vodafone’s performance is on par with the market average.

One area where Vodafone underperforms relative to Movistar and Orange is indoor penetration in inland cities. Vodafone’s reliance on the 1800 MHz band for its core 4G layer means thick-walled buildings in cities like Toledo, Salamanca, and Granada can experience signal degradation. The 700 MHz 5G band that Vodafone won in the 2021 auction is beginning to address this, but deployment is still ongoing in smaller inland cities.

Vodafone and EU Roaming

Vodafone Spain offers standard EU “roam like at home” on its domestic plans, which is relevant if you are entering Spain via France or planning day trips to Portugal, Andorra, or Gibraltar. The network’s integration with Vodafone group companies in other European countries means seamless handoff at borders — your phone connects to Vodafone France, Vodafone Portugal, or Vodafone Gibraltar without hunting for partner networks. This is a minor convenience for multi-country trips, but worth noting for travellers combining Spain with Portugal or France in a single itinerary.

Network Sharing with MásMóvil

As a condition attached to the Orange–MásMóvil merger that created MASORANGE — approved by the European Commission in February 2024 — Vodafone Spain secured a long-term network access agreement with the newly merged group. The arrangement lets both operators make use of each other’s radio access infrastructure in certain areas while maintaining separate core networks and brand identities. For consumers, the practical effect is that Vodafone and Yoigo coverage are increasingly similar in areas where the shared infrastructure is deployed. This blurs the line between the two networks and means you should check recent coverage maps rather than relying on historical reputation when evaluating Vodafone Spain eSIM coverage versus Yoigo.

Yoigo eSIM: The Budget Option with Growing Coverage

Yoigo operates differently from the three larger networks. Originally launched in 2006 as an MVNO running on Movistar’s network, Yoigo has since built out its own radio access network and now sits within MASORANGE, the group formed by the 2024 merger of Orange Spain and MásMóvil — a consolidation that created Spain’s largest operator group by customer numbers. Yoigo competes through aggressive pricing.

Yoigo’s primary advantage is price. Its prepaid ladder is blunt and cheap: EUR 10 buys 25 GB, EUR 15 buys 50 GB, and EUR 20 buys 100 GB, with 150 GB available for EUR 35 — all on 28-day recharges. For budget-conscious travellers who primarily need connectivity in cities and along major transport corridors, Yoigo delivers strong raw value; on price per gigabyte it undercuts every main-brand rival in Spain.

Where Yoigo Falls Short

Three limitations are important to understand:

  1. Rural coverage gaps: In provinces where Yoigo relies on roaming beyond the MASORANGE footprint, data speeds can be throttled, because roaming partners receive lower priority on congested cells. The Spain eSIM coverage situation varies considerably by region, and Yoigo is the most variable of the four networks. In Teruel, Soria, and Ávila provinces — among the least densely populated in Spain — its effective coverage falls well short of what its headline population figures suggest, because coverage is concentrated where people actually live.

  2. 5G is limited: Yoigo’s 5G footprint is the smallest of the four networks, concentrated in big-city centres. Outside major cities, Yoigo subscribers frequently drop to 4G while other networks maintain 5G. Access to the wider MASORANGE network should improve this over time, but as of mid-2026, the rollout benefits have not yet reached smaller cities.

  3. Customer service limitations: Yoigo’s primary support channels are in Spanish. While the self-service app has some English localisation, phone support is predominantly Spanish-only. This can be a hurdle for tourists who encounter activation or connectivity issues. The Mi Yoigo app provides basic functionality in English for account management, but troubleshooting support is more accessible if you have Spanish language skills.

Who Should Choose Yoigo

Yoigo makes sense for travellers staying exclusively in well-covered urban areas on a tight budget: backpackers basing themselves in Madrid or Barcelona, digital nomads who work from co-working spaces in city centres, and short-stay visitors who need light data for navigation and messaging rather than heavy streaming or video calls. For any itinerary that includes rural driving, mountain hiking, or extended periods outside major cities, the savings versus a Movistar or multi-network eSIM are unlikely to justify the coverage compromises.

  • Orange leads median download speeds in most major cities. Its strategic focus on urban capacity investment shows up clearly in dense districts and transit hubs.
  • Vodafone is consistently second or third, never the fastest but rarely the slowest. Its performance is the most uniform across cities, with the smallest gap between its best and worst markets.

Latency Comparison

Speed is only one dimension of network quality. Latency (ping time) affects how responsive your connection feels, particularly for real-time applications like video calls, online gaming, and live streaming. Orange’s 5G standalone deployments give it a measurable latency advantage in the cities where SA is live; Movistar and Vodafone sit close behind, and all three are more than responsive enough for video calls and navigation. Latency differences between the big three only become noticeable to power users running real-time collaboration tools or cloud gaming.

The more important differentiator is not raw speed or latency but coverage consistency in the specific locations you plan to visit. A network that is blisteringly fast in Madrid’s city centre but drops off in a rural pueblo is less useful for a traveller than a network that delivers a solid connection everywhere.

Which Spain eSIM Network Works Best on AVE Trains?

Spain’s high-speed AVE network operated by Renfe covers over 3,200 kilometres of track connecting most major cities. Mobile connectivity on AVE trains has been a historically weak point for all Spanish networks due to the combination of high speed (up to 310 km/h), tunnel sections, and the metal-bodied train carriages that attenuate signal. The problem is compounded by the frequent cell tower handoffs required at high speed — your phone may switch towers every 30–60 seconds on the fastest sections, and each handoff carries a risk of dropped packets or brief disconnection.

Network Performance on AVE Routes

Independent speed tests across the Madrid–Barcelona, Madrid–Valencia, and Madrid–Seville AVE corridors typically produce the following observations:

Orange performs best overall on AVE trains. Orange has deployed distributed antenna systems (DAS) inside several AVE train models on the Madrid–Barcelona line, and its network handles the frequent handoffs between cell towers at 300 km/h more smoothly than competitors. Onboard speeds on any network are modest — enough for browsing, messaging, and music streaming, but not seamless 4K video. Tunnel sections near Lleida, Zaragoza, and the Guadarrama mountain range cause complete signal drops for 1–3 minutes at a time regardless of network. However, Orange reconnects fastest after tunnels and holds a usable connection for the largest share of the journey, with Vodafone and Movistar trailing by a visible margin.

Vodafone performs best on coastal AVE routes such as Barcelona–Málaga (the Mediterranean corridor) and the connection to Alicante. Vodafone’s coastal network density minimises dropouts on these routes, where it holds a narrow edge over Orange across the journey.

Movistar’s AVE performance is adequate but unremarkable. The network reconnects somewhat slower after tunnels and experiences slightly more frequent handoff drops in the high-speed sections near Toledo and Cuenca. For travellers who primarily need messaging and occasional browsing during the journey, Movistar is sufficient. Spain eSIM Network Recommendations by Destination

Different Spanish destinations create different demands on mobile networks. Below are specific recommendations based on where you plan to visit.

Madrid and Barcelona

Orange is the strongest performer in both capitals, with Vodafone close behind. For a city break confined to urban areas, a single-network Orange eSIM or any multi-network solution that includes Orange will deliver fast, reliable data. In Madrid, specific areas where network choice matters: the metro system has good coverage from all operators, but Orange and Vodafone maintain connections more consistently in tunnel sections between stations. Andalusia (Seville, Granada, Cordoba, Malaga, Costa del Sol)

This region presents a split profile that makes it one of the most challenging for single-network eSIMs. The cities (Seville, Granada, Cordoba) are well-covered by all four networks, with Orange leading speeds. The coastal strip from Malaga to Tarifa is Vodafone’s home territory, with its strongest coverage. The inland white villages (Ronda, Arcos de la Frontera, Grazalema) are Movistar country, and the difference between Movistar and Orange or Vodafone here is significant — you may have 4G on Movistar while Orange and Vodafone drop to 3G or lose signal entirely.

If your Andalusian itinerary includes the classic route of Seville — Granada — Cordoba — Costa del Sol, you will cross three different network-optimal zones. A Spain eSIM that can switch between networks as you drive from Seville to the coast to the mountain pueblos will provide the most consistent experience. The Alhambra in Granada and the Alcazar in Seville are large stone complexes where indoor coverage varies noticeably by network.

Recommendation: Multi-network eSIM with automatic switching between Vodafone and Movistar.

Valencia and Costa Blanca

Valencia city is well-served by all four networks, with Orange leading. The Costa Blanca resorts (Benidorm, Alicante, Torrevieja) have strong Vodafone and Orange coverage, with Vodafone holding a slight edge in peak summer months when tourist numbers are highest. Inland areas of Valencia province favour Movistar, particularly the mountainous interior near Requena, Utiel, and the Sierra de Javalambre.

For visitors staying exclusively on the coast, Orange or Vodafone are both excellent choices. For anyone planning day trips into Valencia’s interior wine regions or the mountains of Alicante province, Movistar’s broader coverage is valuable.

Recommendation: Orange or Vodafone for coastal stays; multi-network if exploring inland towns.

Basque Country and Northern Coast (Bilbao, San Sebastian, Santander)

Orange and Vodafone perform comparably in Bilbao and San Sebastian. The coastal highway (A-8) between Bilbao and Santander has good coverage from all four networks. Inland routes through the Basque mountains favour Movistar, particularly on the winding roads through the Urkiola Natural Park and the routes from Bilbao to Vitoria-Gasteiz.

The Guggenheim Museum in Bilbao and the old town of San Sebastian (Parte Vieja) present different coverage challenges. In the Guggenheim, all networks work adequately for basic browsing. In San Sebastian’s dense old town, with its narrow streets and tall buildings, Orange and Movistar perform best.

Recommendation: Multi-network eSIM, or Orange for city-focused itineraries.

Balearic Islands (Mallorca, Menorca, Ibiza, Formentera)

Vodafone and Orange both provide strong coverage in resort areas and beaches. Movistar is unnecessary here unless you plan to explore the interior of Mallorca extensively, including the Serra de Tramuntana, where Movistar’s rural advantage matters. On Menorca, the remote northern coves (calas) are better served by Vodafone and Movistar than Orange.

Formentera, the smallest of the inhabited Balearic Islands, has limited coverage from all networks away from the main port area. Vodafone provides the most consistent signal across the island, but even Vodafone drops to 3G in the more remote beach areas.

Recommendation: Vodafone or multi-network eSIM for island road trips.

Canary Islands (Tenerife, Gran Canaria, Lanzarote, Fuerteventura)

Vodafone has the strongest coverage in tourist zones across all Canary Islands. Orange and Movistar are comparable in resort areas but both trail Vodafone in coverage breadth. The interior of Tenerife (Mount Teide, Anaga) and Gran Canaria (inland mountain villages) benefit from Movistar’s broader rural coverage. The Roque Nublo and Teide National Park trails are best covered by Movistar.

Each island has distinct coverage patterns. On Lanzarote, the Timanfaya National Park has limited coverage from all networks. On Fuerteventura, the northern beaches (Corralejo, El Cotillo) are well-covered by Vodafone but the interior roads are Movistar territory.

Recommendation: Multi-network eSIM with Movistar for hikers and road-trippers; Vodafone for resort-based stays.
