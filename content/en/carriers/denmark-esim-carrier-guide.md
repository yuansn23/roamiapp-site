## What EU roaming rules mean for your eSIM



Denmark joined the EU in 1973, which puts it inside the "roam like at home" regime. The practical upshot for anyone who buys a SIM or takes a subscription from a Danish operator: that plan keeps working in the other 26 EU member states, plus Norway, Iceland and Liechtenstein, without per-megabyte surcharges. Operators may apply a fair-use cap when you roam, and if you blow past it they can levy a small regulated fee — but for maps, messaging and a week of normal browsing, a Danish plan behaves the same in Rome as it does in Roskilde.



Two borders deserve special mention because they surprise travelers leaving Denmark.



**Norway is covered.** It sits in the European Economic Area, so EU roaming rights extend there. If your Copenhagen trip ends with a flight to Oslo, a Danish-issued plan keeps its home-rate treatment on Norwegian networks.



**The United Kingdom and Switzerland are not.** Both sit outside the EU regime, so Danish operators may charge whatever their international price lists say the moment you cross into either. The same applies in reverse for an UK or Swiss SIM used in Denmark. If your itinerary strings together London, Copenhagen and Zurich, budget for that gap — or buy a multi-country travel plan whose country list covers all three explicitly.



Now the clarification competitors get wrong most often: EU roaming rules apply to *subscriptions issued in an EU country*. A travel eSIM you buy from an online provider before flying to Copenhagen is not a Danish subscription. It is a roaming product, governed entirely by the country list attached to the plan you purchased. Those rules do not grant it any rights in the EU; they only describe where the provider has negotiated coverage. That is why a Denmark-only profile stops passing traffic the second you cross the Øresund Bridge, while a [Europe eSIM](/europe-esim/) keeps working from Copenhagen to Hamburg. Match the plan's footprint to your route, not to the rulebook.



## Market structure



Denmark runs on four physical mobile networks. YouSee is the consumer brand of TDC, the country's oldest and largest operator. Telenor and Telia operate a shared radio network, which means the two brands cover nearly identical ground — their customers attach to the same towers. And 3 (often written Tre) runs an independent network that punches above its weight in the big cities.



Because a travel eSIM attaches to whichever partner network the provider has contracted with, you rarely choose the network directly. What you should know is how each performs, since your profile will land on one of them. All figures below come from Ookla's [Speedtest Connectivity Report for Denmark, H1 2025](https://www.ookla.com/research/reports/denmark-speedtest-connectivity-report-h1-2025), collected January through June 2025.



| Network | Standout measurement in H1 2025 | What it means for a short trip |

|:---|:---|:---|

| Telia | Fastest overall: 212.6 Mbps median download, and the fastest 5G median at 286.6 Mbps | Top pick for speed-sensitive work: large uploads, video calls, cloud backups |

| YouSee | Best mobile gaming experience in the country, Game Score 91.02 | Lowest latency and jitter — the metric that matters on the train |

| 3 | Best video streaming score (86.2 mobile, 88.64 on 5G) | Strongest choice if streaming is your main use |

| Telenor | 182.38 Mbps median download overall, 272.28 Mbps on 5G | Deep coverage through the shared Telia/Telenor footprint |



One result in that report matters more than any single number: Ookla found **no statistical difference between the providers on its overall Connectivity Score, on consistency, or on 5G availability**. In most countries the carrier question answers itself. In Denmark, it mostly does not — the networks are close enough that where you are standing moves the needle more than which logo your profile attaches to. That is the strongest argument for buying a managed travel plan rather than agonizing over operator choice: automatic re-attachment to the strongest available partner network matters more in Denmark than any brand preference.



Sector oversight sits with the Danish Business Authority, which functions as the telecom regulator under Denmark's implementation of the EU telecommunications code — relevant mainly if you ever need to escalate a complaint about a local operator.



## Prepaid your eSIM plans, priced in kroner



The four network operators are not really in the tourist business. Their retail shelves are built around Danish residents: a monthly subscription, a CPR number and a Danish bank account set up for direct debit. What visitors actually buy sits one layer down, in the prepaid brands and MVNOs that resell Telia, Telenor and 3 capacity. Knowing which brand sits on which network is most of the decision.



### Lebara: the tourist default on the Telia/Telenor footprint



Lebara sells at 7-Eleven, Netto and most kiosks, and it is the simplest route to a working Danish line. The entry pack is tiny in price and decent in data, and the mid-tier pack is the one most visitors end up with. Note the split between Danish and EU allowances: the headline gigabyte count applies at home, and only a fraction of it follows you across a border.



### Denmark bargain data plans



Lycamobile competes on the same shelves with a slightly better roaming allocation at the entry level, and it is the brand most often recommended by long-term Denmark residents for multi-country trips. Its ALL-IN-ONE packs bundle international calling minutes to Denmark, the EU and the USA on the upper tiers, which suits anyone phoning home rather than just messaging.



### Carriers compared in Denmark



Oister is 3's prepaid brand and the best-value domestic pack in the country on a per-gigabyte basis. It also sells eSIMs. The catch is the sign-up: 3's digital flows lean on MitID, Denmark's national digital identity, which visitors do not have, so a prepaid purchase may mean a store visit or a SIM ordered to a Danish address.



### Telia, Telenor and YouSee: where the CPR wall starts



Buying direct from a network operator means a subscription, and a Danish subscription means a CPR number plus a Danish address. YouSee will sometimes open an account against a deposit for foreigners, but that is a resident's arrangement, not a holiday one. Treat direct operator plans as the right answer only for stays measured in months.

### Where you can buy Denmark eSIM



1. Decide whether your trip leaves Denmark. If it does, buy an EU-inclusive pack or a Europe-wide travel plan — the cheapest domestic pack will strand you at the bridge.

2. Buy at a counter you can return to: 7-Eleven, Netto, Føtex, a carrier flagship near Nørreport or on Strøget, or a Lebara/Lycamobile dealer. Airport kiosks stock Lebara and Lycamobile starter packs but thin out late at night.

3. Present your passport. Prepaid registration with photo ID has been mandatory since 2021, so the assistant scans the document and the line is activated on the spot — there is no waiting period, but there is no anonymous purchase either.

4. If you bought a supermarket starter pack rather than a counter sale, complete registration yourself on the provider's website or app by uploading a photo of your passport.

5. Configure the phone: insert or install the profile, then set the new line as the mobile-data line and enable data roaming on it if you plan to cross a border.

6. Top up before you need to. Voucher top-ups work at kiosks and supermarkets, but some online top-up flows reject non-Danish cards, so keep the balance ahead of your usage rather than reacting to it.



### Documents needed for a Denmark SIM



- **Passport or EU national ID** — the registration document, required at every prepaid sale

- **A Danish address**, if the operator is provisioning a subscription rather than prepaid — hotels and Airbnbs sometimes suffice, sometimes not

- **A card that works in Denmark** — Dankort and international Visa/Mastercard are accepted almost everywhere in person, but foreign billing addresses fail on some online flows

- **MitID** for any fully digital sign-up — a barrier visitors cannot clear, which is why counter purchases matter

- **Your IMEI and EID** — dial `*#06#`; the EID confirms your handset can hold a profile at all

- **Twenty minutes of Wi-Fi** — install and test before you fly, not in the arrivals hall

## Per-gigabyte pricing



Denmark is one of the harder places in Western Europe to walk in and buy prepaid service, for three stacked reasons.



First, registration is mandatory. Since 2021, anyone buying a prepaid SIM in Denmark must present a passport or national ID at the counter, every time, at every outlet — a rule documented in [Danish prepaid registration guidance](https://mobileoperator.dk/prepaid-sim-card-denmark/) and applied to eSIM purchases as well as plastic. No passport, no SIM — this is enforced at kiosks as much as carrier stores. Registration applies to eSIM purchases too, not just plastic.



Second, the prepaid range is thin. Danish operators build their retail business around monthly subscriptions tied to a national registry number (the CPR number) and a Danish address. Visitors cannot get either, which pushes them onto a narrow tourist-tier selection at prices built for convenience rather than value.



Third, distribution leans on convenience retail. Outside the carrier flagships on Strøget and near Nørreport, prepaid stock lives at 7-Eleven counters and supermarkets, where staff can scan a passport but are not equipped to troubleshoot an activation that did not go through.



None of this makes a local SIM a bad product for the right traveler. If you are staying several weeks and want a Danish phone number for bookings and deliveries, a registered prepaid line is worth the errand. For a trip of one to two weeks, the registration queue and limited selection are exactly the friction a travel eSIM removes — install from an email at home, and the line activates when you land.



## Cashless Denmark: top-ups, cards and the MobilePay effect



Denmark is about as close to a cashless economy as any country has come. Debit cards — Dankort and international Visa/Mastercard debit — work essentially everywhere, MobilePay is ubiquitous, and a meaningful share of cafés, kiosks and even some church collection points display signs stating that cash is not accepted. Legally, retailers in Denmark may refuse banknotes.



For a traveler, this cuts two ways. The good news: contactless payment works on the metro, in supermarkets, at museum counters and in most food kiosks, so a working card covers daily life. The catch lands when you try to top up a local prepaid line: voucher purchases at kiosks assume you can pay by card, and some online top-up flows reject foreign-issued cards outright. Travelers who arrive with a local SIM and a non-Danish wallet occasionally find themselves unable to reload it without a trip to a carrier store.



MobilePay is the other half of the story. Denmark's dominant payment app is tied to a Danish bank account and, in practice, to a CPR number — so it is not a traveller's tool, however ubiquitous it looks. Every sign you see reading "MobilePay only" is a sign that your contactless card is the fallback.



A prepaid travel eSIM sidesteps the entire loop, because you pay once, at checkout, with the card you already use. If your trip is short, that single-transaction model is worth more than the krone-per-gigabyte advantage of a local top-up.



## Arriving at Copenhagen Airport: the SIM queue without a Denmark eSIM



Copenhagen Airport (CPH) handles the overwhelming majority of international arrivals, and its SIM situation is workable but rarely fast. In Terminals 2 and 3 you will find a 7-Eleven selling Lebara and Lycamobile starter packs, a WHSmith with a smaller selection, and SIM vending machines near baggage reclaim. Carrier-branded stores with staff who can register a passport properly sit in the city, not the airport. At peak arrival waves the queues stretch, and every kiosk transaction includes the passport scan discussed above.



Billund and Aarhus airports offer convenience-store purchases only — fine for an immediate connection, useless for comparing bundles.



Compare that with arriving already provisioned. A travel eSIM installed at home costs you nothing at the airport: put the phone in airplane mode on descent, toggle it off, and the data line registers on a Danish network before you reach passport control. The metro from the airport to Kongens Nytorv takes fourteen minutes, and you will want transit directions working before you board it. ## A connected Denmark week: castles, coastlines and city data



Denmark rewards itineraries that radiate out from Copenhagen, and mobile data earns its keep differently on each leg.



**Copenhagen and North Zealand.** The capital needs no connectivity excuses — the metro runs driverless, the harbor buses take cards, and restaurant bookings happen in apps. The classic day trip runs north along the coast: Frederiksborg Castle in Hillerød, then Kronborg in Helsingør, the Renaissance fortress that Shakespeare borrowed for Elsinore. Trains leave every twenty minutes and hold signal the whole way. Two to three gigabytes covers a city week of maps, tickets and photo uploads.



**Aarhus.** Denmark's second city sits three hours from Copenhagen by train, or forty minutes by air. ARoS art museum, the open-air Den Gamle By, and a compact walkable center make it a two-night stop. Aarhus measured 266.96 Mbps median download in H1 2025 — second among populous cities — so remote work sessions from a café here are effortless.



**Odense.** Hans Christian Andersen's hometown sits mid-route between Copenhagen and Jutland and makes a natural half-day break. Funen's connectivity held at 263.18 Mbps in the city center, thinning on rural B-roads away from it.



**Bornholm.** The rock-and-smokehouse island in the Baltic is the itinerary outlier: a flight from Copenhagen, or a ferry from Ystad in Sweden. Round churches, herring smokehouses and the Dueodde dunes justify two nights. Coverage concentrates in the coastal towns and thins on the island's forested interior — download the trail maps before you set out. If your plan is Denmark-only, the ferry leg from Ystad is also the moment a single-country eSIM stops working; a [Sweden eSIM](/sweden-esim/) or a multi-country plan is the fix. The same logic applies if you continue south into the continent — see [eSIM options for Germany](/germany-esim/) for the Hamburg leg.



## Signal by Region



Denmark is flat, compact and dense with towers, but the edges of the map still soften. Region Zealand — the rural belt around Copenhagen, excluding the capital itself — recorded the slowest median download of any Danish region in H1 2025 at 153.02 Mbps, a reminder that the archipelago behind the capital is not uniformly urban.



The reliably thin spots for travelers:



- **The west Jutland coast.** The North Sea beaches around Klitmøller and Thyborøn sit far from the population centers, and signal weakens along exposed stretches of the dune coast.

- **The Wadden Sea islands.** Fanø, Mandø and Rømø are reachably close to Esbjerg, but Marsh-island coverage is coastal-town-deep only.

- **Bornholm's interior.** Almindingen forest and the paradise hills hold signal on the main roads, not always on the trails.

- **The small islands.** Læsø, Samsø, Langeland and the South Funen archipelago have solid town coverage and patchy surroundings; ferries between them are connectivity dead time.

- **Bridges and tunnels.** The Storebælt crossing holds signal; expect dips on the longer spans at peak traffic.

## Denmark eSIM borders: crossing into Sweden and Germany



Denmark's geography makes border crossings routine rather than exotic, and what happens to your connection depends on one distinction: was your line issued in Denmark, or by a provider abroad?



**The Øresund crossing.** Copenhagen to Malmö takes roughly forty minutes by train, and Danish-issued SIMs roam onto Swedish networks at no extra cost under roam like at home. A Denmark-only *travel* profile does not — it stops passing traffic somewhere over the bridge, usually with no warning banner. Travelers notice the failure in Malmö's central station, which is the worst possible place to be debugging a plan. If your itinerary includes any Swedish leg, buy a plan whose country list says Sweden.



**The German border.** Overland routes to Hamburg and Berlin cross a Schengen frontier with no visible change, which is exactly why the plan footprint matters here too. A Europe-wide profile transitions silently. A Denmark-only profile behaves as if you switched your phone off.



**Norway and Iceland.** Both sit inside the EEA, so a Danish subscription keeps working. A travel plan still needs both countries named in its coverage list.



**The Faroe Islands and Greenland.** Both are Danish territories and neither is inside the EU roaming regime. Coverage comes from local operators on separate arrangements, and no mainstream Danish prepaid pack extends there. Treat them as their own purchase.



**Poland and the Baltic states**, if your route runs south or east through the continent, are ordinary EU territory for a Danish-issued line and ordinary country-list territory for a travel plan — see [Poland eSIM options](/europe-esim/) among the Europe-wide comparisons if your route runs long.



## Getting your eSIM live



Everything about Danish arrival logistics — airport queues, passport registration, kiosk hours — argues for provisioning a travel eSIM at home. The sequence takes ten minutes:



1. **Confirm your handset.** An unlocked, eSIM-capable phone is the whole prerequisite. Run your model through the [handset compatibility checker](/compatibility/) rather than guessing; regional iPhone variants are the classic trap.

2. **Buy and receive the QR code.** Delivery is by email, typically within minutes. Screenshot it and store it in cloud storage, not just your camera roll.

3. **Install on Wi-Fi.** Add the profile in Settings while you still have home broadband. Label the line clearly — "Denmark" outlives "Secondary" in a pinch.

4. **Set the data line and roaming toggle.** Point mobile data at the new line and enable data roaming on it; without the toggle, the profile installs fine and passes no traffic.

5. **Defer activation until landing** if your plan supports delayed activation — otherwise it simply connects when it finds a Danish network.

## Sizing Your Data Plan



Most in-country failures resolve in under five minutes, in this order:



- **No internet despite signal bars.** Check that the travel line, not your home SIM, is selected for mobile data — dual-SIM phones flip this silently after a reboot. Then confirm data roaming is on for that specific line.

- **Working in Copenhagen, dead in Malmö.** This is the Øresund handover, not a fault. A Denmark-only travel profile has no rights on Swedish networks, so a Swedish leg needs a plan whose country list includes Sweden. A Danish-issued SIM would have roamed through without a flicker, which is why the same trip can behave differently on two different plans.

- **Attached but throttled.** Most travel plans slow down, rather than cut off, when the allowance runs out. Check remaining data in the provider app before blaming the network; Danish networks rarely throttle otherwise.

- **"No service" after a ferry or flight.** Airplane-mode cycle once. If the line still will not register, switch network selection from automatic to manual and pick the Danish carrier your plan partners with, then revert to automatic.

## Denmark eSIM questions travelers ask most, answered



### Does a Denmark eSIM work elsewhere?



No. The rules apply to plans issued by operators in EU countries. A travel eSIM purchased from a provider abroad is a roaming product governed by its own country list — so a Denmark-only plan will not roam in Sweden, and an EU-listed plan will. Buy to match your route.



### Can I use my Danish SIM in Malmö or Gothenburg?



If it was issued in Denmark, yes — Norway and the EU are included under roam like at home, with a fair-use allowance. If you are running a Denmark-only travel profile instead, the answer is no the moment you cross the bridge; that is what a multi-country European plan exists for.

On Ookla's H1 2025 data, YouSee delivered the best mobile gaming experience with a Game Score of 91.02, and 3 took best video streaming at 86.2. Telia was fastest outright. With no statistical gap on overall quality, none of this should drive a purchase decision for a normal trip.

Yes, for any locally purchased prepaid service — registration with photo ID has been mandatory since 2021 and is applied at kiosks and carrier stores alike, for eSIMs as well as plastic. A travel eSIM bought online before departure involves no ID check at all.



### Are Denmark data prices high?



Entry-level tourist packs from Lebara and Lycamobile start at about DKK 19 for a month, with mid-tier packs around DKK 49 and large domestic packs reaching DKK 299 for the unlimited Lycamobile tier. Yes, if you buy prepaid. Tourist-oriented packs from Lebara and Lycamobile register against a passport alone. A CPR number only becomes mandatory when you take a monthly subscription from a network operator such as YouSee, Telia or Telenor.



### Does signal hold across Denmark's smaller islands?



Yes in towns and along main roads. Expect thinner coverage in Bornholm's forested interior and on the smaller islands, and download offline maps before island-hopping days. Ferries are dead time on every network.



### Is a local Danish SIM cheaper than a travel eSIM?



Per gigabyte, usually yes — a DKK 49 pack can carry ten gigabytes, which no travel plan matches on price. Once you add the airport queue, the passport registration and the risk of an unusable top-up on a foreign card, the gap narrows sharply for trips of one to two weeks. Beyond a month, the local pack wins outright.

On a locally bought prepaid pack, the 30-day window starts the moment the SIM activates at the counter, which makes buying at the airport on arrival expensive in a subtle way.
