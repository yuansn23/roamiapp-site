**Antigua.** The cobblestone heart of Antigua tests at 62.86 Mbps with 88% consistency, and both networks treat it as a priority market. The moment you hike out toward Cerro de la Cruz and the surrounding hills, the signal relaxes — the department median is 20+ Mbps lower than the city's for a reason.



**Lake Atitlán.** This is Guatemala's real coverage trap. The Sololá department median of 36.48 Mbps is the second-lowest among populated regions, and the geography explains why: deep volcanic caldera, villages strung around the shoreline, boats as the only transport. Panajachel, San Marcos and San Pedro each have usable 4G in the towns; the lake itself and the trails between villages are intermittent. If you're working remotely from a lakeside rental, judge the property's Wi-Fi first and treat mobile data as backup.



**Tikal and Petén.** Petén posts 52.51 Mbps with 91.4% consistency — better than you'd expect for jungle lowlands, because Flores and the tourist corridor are priority coverage. Inside Tikal National Park, signal exists around the entrance and visitor area but does not follow you into the temple squares and jungle loops. Download the park map before you enter; guides coordinate by WhatsApp from the entrance, not from the canopy.



**Volcano hikes, including Acatenango.** The connectivity story is simple: the trailhead base camps on the lower slopes have signal on at least one network, and above the treeline you should assume none. No published report measures the mountain itself — treat anything above the trailhead as offline, tell your contacts before you ascend, and let your base-camp operator handle emergencies. The same logic applies to the other volcano trails of the western highlands.



**Guatemala City and the highway system.** If your route is airport → Antigua → Lake Atitlán by the main roads, you're in the best-covered corridor in the country; the highways hold 4G for most of their length, thinning in the eastern departments.



**Semuc Champey and the road to Lanquín.** This is the destination where the coverage map and the trip map disagree most sharply. Lanquín, the town that serves the pools, holds a working LTE pocket, and the town square is fine for messages and maps. Then you descend into the canyon and the limestone bluffs absorb the signal: the pools and the caves are, in practice, offline. The last leg from Lanquín is a rough pickup-truck ride to the trailhead, so combine the two facts and you get the rule — download everything while you are standing in Lanquín, and treat the pools as a genuinely disconnected afternoon.



**Chichicastenango and the western highlands.** The market town — market days are Thursday and Sunday — has usable 4G in the square and along the main approach, which is what you need for checking the exchange rate and messaging a driver. The gap is not at the destination but on the way: the road over the departmental ridges dips in and out of coverage between cells. If you are coming from Panajachel or Antigua by shuttle, that is a couple of hours of intermittent signal, and the right time to have your return pickup saved rather than to be searching for it on arrival.



## Guatemala coverage: shuttles, chicken buses and the roads between



Almost every Guatemala itinerary is a chain of shuttles, and the country's transport menu is unusually broad. Knowing which mode you are on tells you how much connectivity to expect.



**Tourist shuttles** run the visitor corridors — Guatemala City to Antigua, Antigua to Lake Atitlán, Antigua to Lanquín, Antigua to Flores — in minivans booked through hostels and agencies. They are what most visitors use, they run door to door, and their drivers coordinate by WhatsApp, which means you will be messaged rather than called. That single habit is an argument for having data on arrival: the pickup confirmation is the one message you cannot afford to miss.



**Chicken buses** are the local network: repainted former US school buses that reach almost every market town in the western highlands at a fraction of shuttle prices. They are also where the connectivity advice matters least and the safety advice matters most — they are routinely overloaded and the western-highland routes have a reputation for theft, which is why the usual guidance for visitors is a short daylight hop rather than a long haul.



**The Atitlán–Xela road** is the case that makes offline maps non-negotiable. The route between the lake and Quetzaltenango climbs through highland ridges where the signal alternates between a working cell and nothing at all, on a road of switchbacks with few towns to fall back on. Your phone's GPS works without a mobile signal, so a downloaded offline map of Sololá and Quetzaltenango departments gets you there; a phone relying on live map tiles does not. Download the area over hotel Wi-Fi before you leave Panajachel, and screenshot your lodging's location — rural Guatemalan addresses are descriptions, not coordinates.

**The passport rule is statutory.** Guatemala's mobile-terminal law, Decreto 8-2013 (Ley de Equipos Terminales Móviles), administered by [the SIT](https://www.sit.gob.gt/)) requires every SIM buyer to show identification, and for foreigners that document is specifically a **valid passport**. The seller keeps a copy on file for three years, and activation is legally conditional on that registration. No passport, no SIM — which is also why tourists cannot skip the counter by buying a physical SIM through a friend.



**Claro's route: self-serve eSIM.** Claro Guatemala sells an "eSIM Prepago" activated from a unique QR code, with a published device-compatibility list running from iPhone XS/XR and Samsung Galaxy S20 onward. In practice you buy the eSIM at a Claro store or authorized dealer, register your passport, and scan; data packages start around **Q55 for roughly 8 GB** with free social-media bundles (about USD 7), with the SIM itself Q5–Q25 depending on the channel.



**Tigo's route: in-store eSIM.** Tigo supports eSIM but provisions it at a Tienda Tigo — request it at the counter, present your passport, and the profile loads there. Tigo's published tourist package is **Q149 (about USD 19)**: 10 GB, free social media and 200 minutes over 30 days, plus the Q10 SIM. Tigo's rule worth knowing before you queue: a phone not bought from Tigo **must be carrier-unlocked** or the eSIM activation fails.

## Which devices accept eSIM



The band question is gentle here. Both networks run 4G on LTE bands 2, 4, 5, 7, 12 and 28, with 5G on n41 and n78 — the standard North-and-South-America spread, so almost any recent unlocked phone from the Americas works fully, and European or Asian handsets connect on 4G comfortably. The two real failure modes are both avoidable at home:



- **Carrier lock.** Tigo states it outright: a handset not bought from Tigo must be "liberado" — unlocked — before an eSIM activates. A locked phone is the single most common cause of a Guatemala eSIM refusing to register. Check Settings → General → About → Carrier Lock, or run your model through the [compatibility page](/compatibility/).

- **Dual-SIM setup.** Keep your home line active for bank verification codes and point mobile data at the eSIM — the standard two-line pattern. Detailed band behavior is in the [eSIM compatibility page](/compatibility/).



## APN values on a Guatemala eSIM, and when to touch them



On both Guatemalan carriers, and on every travel eSIM written for the country, the access point is pushed to the handset automatically when the profile attaches. **The narrow cases where a manual entry still helps:**



- An older handset that does not accept carrier settings over the air

- A Claro eSIM Prepago whose configuration never ran at the dealer

- A profile you installed from an activation code rather than a QR scan



In any of those, get the value from the counter or from your eSIM provider — a number typed from a forum thread is a guess.



**The two menu paths:**



- **iPhone:** Settings → Cellular → tap the eSIM line → Cellular Data Network.

- **Android:** Settings → Connections → Mobile Networks → Access Point Names.



Save, restart, then verify the eSIM — not your home SIM — is the line carrying mobile data.

## Guatemala eSIM borders: crossing into Mexico, Belize, El Salvador



Guatemala is the hinge of Central America, and three overland exits sit on the standard traveller route. Each has a different character and a different data plan.



**To Mexico — La Mesilla / Ciudad Cuauhtémoc.** The main crossing for the Chiapas–Quetzaltenango corridor, with a gap of a few kilometres between the two immigration buildings. Travellers who know the region tend to prefer it on comfort grounds — the highland road to San Cristóbal beats the heat and congestion of Tapachula — but it is also the crossing where arriving outside business hours is most likely to cost you a day, because the customs and migration windows do not match.



**To Belize — Melchor de Mencos / Benque Viejo del Carmen.** The natural exit if Petén is the end of your route, and the crossing the overland Cancún–Tikal traffic uses. It is straightforward, and the bus network on the Guatemalan side connects it to Flores and Santa Elena.



**To El Salvador — Pedro de Alvarado / La Hachadura.** On the CA-2 Oriente, this is the Salvadoran crossing with the widest published hours, and the one Guatemalan immigration names when it tells travellers to carry a passport rather than a simpler identity document. Under the CA-4 arrangement, Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras and Nicaragua share a single 90-day stamp, so your clock keeps running when you cross — you do not get a fresh window by stepping over the line.



Three rules for all of them:



- **Guatemala's own exit carries no fee.** What catches people out is the entry cost of the country they are entering, and the unofficial "guides" at busy posts. ## Guatemala on the trail: ride-hailing, hotel Wi-Fi and power



Three practical details decide how much of your eSIM allowance you actually spend.



### Getting around Guatemala with a ride-hailing app



Uber's Guatemala site is live, and its published city index covers **Guatemala City, Quetzaltenango, Escuintla and Guastatoya**; Antigua is widely reported as served in practice, but supply outside those listed cities should be checked in the app before you rely on it ([Uber Guatemala](https://www.uber.com/gt/en/)). **inDrive** is the other app visitors use, and it works differently: you propose a fare, drivers accept or counter, and cash is the normal payment method.

Wi-Fi quality tracks the same geography as mobile coverage. If you plan to work from a lakeside rental, judge the property's Wi-Fi before you book and treat your eSIM as backup rather than primary.



### Claro Guatemala vs Tigo Guatemala: coverage compared



The Acatenango overnight is the clearest case in the country of a trip where your eSIM stops being useful at a predictable point. The trailhead and lower slopes are within reach of at least one network; the base camp sits around **3,600 m** and has **no mains electricity and no running water**, so charging is on you. Reception up there is unreliable on every carrier — the operators' own descriptions say as much.



What to do instead:



- Charge everything and pack a power bank. You hike to the summit at 4 a.m. by headlamp, so your phone is also your torch, camera and clock.

- Tell someone your itinerary and expected return before the ascent, not during it.

- Treat the mountain as offline time and let your base-camp operator handle emergencies, which is what they are set up for.
