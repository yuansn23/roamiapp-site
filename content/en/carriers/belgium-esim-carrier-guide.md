> **Which Belgium carrier should you buy?**

> **Proximus, Orange Belgium and Telenet — what the latest network data actually shows**

**The short answer:** Belgium is one of the easiest connectivity markets in Europe, and that is exactly why picking a Belgium eSIM carrier is less dramatic than the comparisons suggest. On Ookla's H1 2025 Speedtest data, Proximus is the fastest mobile network (median download 93.33 Mbps), Orange runs the fastest 5G (median 156.37 Mbps), and Telenet/BASE puts the most users on 5G (19.6% availability) — but the top three networks finish within about 13 Mbps of each other overall, and Ookla found no statistical difference between them for overall network quality. Downtown Brussels, Antwerp, Ghent or Bruges, any of the three will feel identical. Where the real decisions live: the Ardennes (the one region where coverage genuinely falls off), the EU quarter at 9am, the trains between cities, and the fact that Belgian prepaid SIMs require in-person ID registration, which most visitors are better off skipping entirely with a [Belgium travel eSIM](/belgium-esim/).

Belgium is the densest mobile market most travelers will ever visit: 12 million mobile connections for 11.3 million people, 96.4% of the population online, according to DataReportal's 2025 figures. Three carriers — Proximus, Orange and Telenet/BASE — cover a country you can cross by train in under three hours. That combination of density and short distances means network choice matters far less here than in France, the Netherlands or almost anywhere else. If your phone's eSIM support is uncertain, settle that first with the [device compatibility list](/compatibility/) before worrying about which network to join.

The market runs on three mature networks and no fourth challenger with its own towers, so the practical questions are narrower than they look: what does each network actually win, what changes when you leave the city triangle, and how do you legally get a Belgian line if you want one. This page answers all three, in that order.

## The network owners

If you are choosing between the three rather than accepting whichever tower is nearest, this is where the measured differences land. Each entry covers what the network wins, how a visitor buys into it, what it costs, and what goes wrong.

### Proximus vs Orange: which Belgium carrier is faster?

Proximus is the former state incumbent and the only operator to lead Ookla's headline speed table outright in H1 2025: a **Speed Score of 65.87**, a **median download of 93.33 Mbps**, a median upload of 12.38 Mbps and the **market's lowest latency at 38 ms**. It also took the **best mobile gaming score (89.89)** and the best 5G gaming score (91.84), which is the clearest sign that it holds latency under load rather than merely peaking high.

**Buying in as a visitor:** Proximus Pay&Go prepaid is a shop product. The eSIM itself is normally attached to a MyProximus subscription account, which is built around Belgian identity and billing, so the realistic visitor route is a Proximus shop with your passport — and even then the staff member performs the legal identity registration for you at the counter.

**What it costs:** Pay&Go works on top-up credit rather than a flat tourist bundle, so the data allowance scales with how much you recharge. There is no visitor-priced tier.

**What goes wrong:** Proximus's distance-selling and self-service registration paths assume the Belgian eID, which a foreign passport cannot satisfy. In shops it is straightforward, but shops close early and the airport desks keep limited hours.

**Where it pays off:** a video call from a train seat on the Brussels–Liège intercity, or a 09:00 Teams meeting near Schuman, where the 38 ms latency is the asset you are actually buying.

### Proximus vs Orange 5G: which is better in Belgium?

Orange is the 5G specialist. It recorded the **fastest 5G network with a Speed Score of 48.39**, a **median 5G download of 156.37 Mbps** and a median 5G upload of 14.44 Mbps, and it took both the **best video streaming score (80.57)** and the **best 5G video score (85.46)**. Ookla also awarded it the **Best 5G Network**, with a 5G Speedtest Connectivity Score of 69.60. In the city table it was the only operator to lead in more than one place, winning Antwerp, Ghent, Leuven and Brussels.

**Buying in as a visitor:** Orange Belgium sells prepaid in its own shops and verifies your identity before the SIM leaves the counter — bring a passport or national ID card.

**What it costs:** Orange's prepaid is a credit-based product rather than a tourist plan; you buy the starter and then top up in steps.

**What goes wrong:** the paradox of this market. Orange's advantage is a 5G layer that most users only reach 9–19% of the time, so outside Antwerp and Ghent the 5G lead often does not translate into a visible difference. And its own stores are the same places that close by early evening.

**Where it pays off:** streaming or sharing from Antwerp, Ghent or Leuven, where Orange's published city medians are the highest of the three, and the 5G video score is the best in the country.

### Who leads 5G in Belgium?

Telenet and BASE are the same group and the same network. This is the network that took the **highest 5G availability in Belgium at 19.6%** — meaning its users are on 5G a majority of the time in more places than anyone else's — while finishing third on raw speed (80.54 Mbps median, 125.23 Mbps on 5G). On the numbers, this is the value position: more time on the newer layer, slightly less peak throughput.

**Buying in as a visitor:** BASE runs the most foreigner-friendly registration in the country. Its own identification page sets out four routes — itsme (Belgium's national digital ID), a photo of your Belgian ID, EU ID card **or international passport** uploaded to the My BASE app with a biometric selfie comparison, a card reader in a shop, or manual entry. The passport-plus-selfie route is the only one a non-resident can complete end to end without Belgian identity tools.

**What it costs:** BASE prepaid bundles combine data, minutes and SMS in a single top-up rather than selling data alone.

**What goes wrong:** availability is not speed. Being on 5G more often is worth something, but it will not rescue you in a valley, and the biometric check itself is the step visitors fail — the selfie has to match the document photo cleanly.

**Where it pays off:** a week-long stay where you genuinely want a +32 number and are willing to do the registration once, in the app, using your passport.

## Where a Belgium eSIM actually struggles: the Ardennes

Belgium's urban network is excellent; its southeast is the exception that proves the rule. Once you leave the city triangle and drive into Wallonia — the Condroz, the Famenne, and above all the Ardennes — coverage thins with the population. The deep river valleys are the problem: the Semois below Bouillon, the Ourthe and Amblève, the Lesse near Han-sur-Lesse all cut signal corridors where one cell serves a gorge. Villages and main roads are generally fine; minor forest roads, the High Fens plateau and hiking trails are not.

The named places matter, because the pattern is not random. Bastogne, La Roche-en-Ardenne, Durbuy, Dinant and Spa all sit in towns with working coverage; the gaps appear on the roads and trails between them. The Ardennes is also the region where the three networks diverge most, which is the opposite of the cities: whichever operator has a mast in the valley you are standing in wins, and that changes from one village to the next.

The practical rule for the Ardennes is the same as for any rural region: download offline maps before you leave Namur or Liège, and expect your phone to drop to 3G-speed 4G or no signal for stretches. If you are planning Dinant, Durbuy, La Roche or Bouillon, a travel eSIM that can re-select among Proximus, Orange and Telenet/BASE gives you the best odds, because the strongest carrier changes valley by valley.

The trains are a different story. IC trains between Brussels, Antwerp, Ghent, Bruges and Liège run through continuously populated corridors, and data generally holds well enough for maps, messaging and even video calls on Proximus and Orange. The lines that degrade first are the slower regional routes through the Ardennes and along the coast beyond Knokke — again, distance from the cities, not carrier brand, is the variable.

## Belgian rail and tram coverage: where congestion actually bites

Belgium's rail network is the reason this market punches above its size. SNCB/NMBS intercity services run Brussels–Antwerp in 35–50 minutes, Brussels–Ghent in 30–40, and Brussels–Bruges in 55–65, and the whole classic tourist loop sits inside continuously populated country. Coverage on those corridors is a genuine strength compared with neighbouring Germany, where rail dead zones are a running joke. The one regular blackout is the North–South junction under central Brussels, where the tunnels cut the connection for roughly a minute.

The coastal tram is the other showcase. The Kusttram runs 67 km from Knokke to De Panne — the longest tram line in the world — and holds signal for the full ride, with 5G in the larger resort towns. Above ground in the cities, trams and the pre-metro are well served too; the underground stretches of the Antwerp premetro are the variable part.

Where signal does not fail but speed does is congestion, and it has a calendar. The EU quarter around Schuman fills with commuters and roamers on weekday mornings; the Grand Place fills with tour groups on summer evenings; Brussels Airport fills in arrival waves; the busiest coastal resorts fill on hot weekends. On every one of those occasions the cell is the bottleneck, not the carrier, and switching networks will not help.

Two practical consequences follow. First, the apps that need data on a Belgian trip — STIB/MIVB for Brussels, De Lijn for Flanders, SNCB/NMBS for rail, plus Uber or Bolt for airport runs — all expect a live connection, and SNCB in particular issues tickets as in-app QR codes. Second, taking a taxi or ride-hailing app in a congested cell is the moment you notice a slow network most, because the driver is waiting on a map that will not load.

## Using the eSIM abroad

This is the section most visitors get wrong, and the error runs in one direction.

**Roam Like At Home (RLAH)** has existed since 2017 and was extended by **Regulation (EU) 2022/612 until 2032**. It means a mobile line issued in one participating country is charged at domestic rates in all the others — calls, SMS and data out of your normal allowance, with nothing to switch on. The zone covers all 27 EU member states plus Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway through the EEA, and was extended to Ukraine and Moldova on 1 January 2026.

The rule that matters is about **where the SIM was issued, not where you are standing**. Belgium is inside the zone, so a German, Dutch, French or Belgian subscriber roaming into Brussels pays domestic rates automatically. A visitor from the United States, Canada, the UK, Australia or anywhere else outside the EU/EEA gets nothing from the regulation: their carrier's roaming terms apply, whatever the map says. That is the single reason a US traveler's phone bill and a German traveler's phone bill look completely different in the same Antwerp café.

Two limits apply even inside the zone. RLAH is written for **periodic travel, not permanent residence**: if over a four-month window you spend more time and data abroad than at home, your operator may contact you and then apply a surcharge capped at the wholesale rate. And **Switzerland and the United Kingdom are not in the zone** — both sit outside the EU and EEA, so roaming there is an ordinary commercial matter.

Travel eSIMs sit outside all of this. They are not EU-issued lines, so RLAH does not govern them; their terms come from whoever sold you the plan. What that means in practice: an EU-issued SIM needs no purchase at all for Belgium, a non-EU visitor's home plan usually needs an alternative, and a travel eSIM is that alternative rather than a beneficiary of the regulation. Full detail: [Roaming — Questions & Answers](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/roaming-questions-answers) from the European Commission.

## Can tourists buy a Belgium eSIM directly from the carriers?

This is the question that most changes the answer for short trips, and the honest answer is: not easily.

Belgian law requires every prepaid SIM buyer to be identified — an anti-terrorism measure the carriers apply strictly. [BASE's own identification page](https://base.be/en/support/account-personal-data/your-personal-data/mandatory-identification-for-prepaid-sim-cards.html) states that as a telecom provider it is "legally required to identify you" when you buy a prepaid SIM card, with registration done via itsme (Belgium's national digital ID), a photo of your ID or international passport with a biometric selfie check in the My BASE app, or a card reader in shops. Of the three carriers, BASE/Telenet has the most digital-friendly route — a passport photo plus selfie can complete registration from abroad — but the flow is built around Belgian identity tools and is clunky for a two-day visitor.

Proximus sells eSIMs primarily attached to a mobile subscription ordered through a MyProximus account, or as a QR code handed over in a Proximus shop; its Pay&Go prepaid is a shop product where staff register your passport. Orange Belgium's prepaid follows the same in-person pattern. In practice, buying direct means a store visit during opening hours — and Belgian carrier shops in city centers often close by 18:00 and on Sundays, while Brussels Airport's counters have limited hours. Arriving by Eurostar at Brussels-Midi, there is no SIM counter in the station at all.

## Phone Compatibility

Belgium is further through the legacy switch-off than most of Europe, and the order is unusual: 3G went first.

**3G is already gone.** According to the regulator, **Belgium's 3G networks were shut down during 2025**. Any 3G-only handset is therefore already a paperweight in Belgium, not a backup.

**2G goes between 2028 and 2029.** [BIPT's 2G shutdown page](https://bipt.be/consumers/telephone-internet-tv/uitschakeling-van-de-2g-netwerken/2g-networks-shutdown) records that **Orange, Proximus and Telenet/BASE have confirmed their 2G networks will be shut down in the course of 2028 and 2029**, with each operator setting its own exact dates. Orange Belgium's own position is that its 2G network stays active up to and including **31 December 2028**, with the full shutdown from **1 January 2029**.

Two consequences are worth carrying in your head. First, a 2G-only device — an old backup phone, some trackers, alarm and lift systems, certain medical monitors, older in-car eCall units — loses service entirely. Second, and less obvious, **some 4G and 5G phones are affected too**, if they fall back to 2G for voice because **VoLTE** is not enabled. BIPT's own advice is to enable "Voice over LTE" in the device settings now, while the network is still there to test on. BIPT also notes that 4G coverage exceeds 2G coverage indoors and out, so nobody is losing reach — they are losing a fallback.

For a normal visitor with a phone from the last five years, there is nothing to do beyond keeping VoLTE on. For anyone carrying an old backup handset "just in case", the honest answer is that it has already stopped working in Belgium.

## Is your phone ready for Belgian networks?

Belgian operators deploy 4G on band 20 (800 MHz), band 3 (1800 MHz) and band 7 (2600 MHz), with 5G mainly on n78 (3500 MHz) alongside n1 and n28. An unlocked phone from the last five years will have all of them; the checklist is the same as anywhere in Europe.

One Belgium-specific note: BIPT has confirmed the industry's timeline to switch off 2G — Orange, Proximus and Telenet/BASE will retire their 2G networks in 2028 and 2029. If you carry an old backup phone or a 2G-only tracker or alarm device, it will not survive long in Belgium. ### Which handsets take a Belgium eSIM?

- **Travel eSIM:** a QR code or in-app install immediately after checkout; the profile roams onto whichever of Proximus, Orange or Telnet/BASE is strongest, and nothing else is required.

- **Orange Belgium:** the prepaid line is provisioned at the point of sale once identity is confirmed, so activation happens in front of staff rather than at home.

### Five Belgian eSIM failure patterns and their fixes

General activation errors — a profile that will not download, a scan that fails, an eSIM that installs but never registers — are covered in our [eSIM activation error guide](/faq/esim-activation-errors-troubleshooting-guide/). The five patterns below are the Belgium-specific ones.

**A. The eSIM will not install**
1. Carrier Lock lives in Settings -> General -> About and must show "No SIM restrictions".
2. The QR must be unscanned -- codes nearly always work once only.
3. Ask the provider whether the profile is still pending rather than issued.

**B. Installed, but "no service"**
1. Turn off automatic network selection.
2. Pick Proximus, Orange or Telenet/BASE manually and wait 30 seconds on each.
3. In an Ardennes valley, trying all three takes two minutes and usually finds one.
4. Wipe network settings and restart the phone.

**C. Signal bars, but no data**
1. Verify the eSIM, not your home SIM, is the selected data line.
2. Check data roaming is enabled for the eSIM line specifically.
3. If you bought Belgian prepaid, clear any outstanding identity registration — an unregistered card cannot use data.

**D. Data works, but voice does not**
1. Enable VoLTE in the device settings.
2. Confirm the handset is not a 3G-only or 2G-only device — 3G is already off in Belgium.
3. Remember data-only travel eSIMs cannot take calls; use WhatsApp, FaceTime or Signal instead.

**E. Slow in one spot, fine everywhere else**
1. Treat it as congestion or valley shadowing, not a misconfiguration.
2. Move a few hundred metres toward the valley mouth or the main road.
3. Wait out the morning EU-quarter rush rather than switching carriers.

## Belgium eSIM FAQ

### Proximus vs Orange: which is better in Belgium?

Any of the three, for city travel. Proximus is the fastest overall (93.33 Mbps median), Orange has the fastest 5G (156.37 Mbps) and the best 5G video experience, Telenet/BASE keeps the most users on 5G (19.6% availability). Ookla found no statistical difference between them for overall network quality, so itineraries that stay in Brussels, Antwerp, Ghent and Bruges will not notice the difference.

### Which documents work for a Belgium SIM?

Yes. Identification is legally mandatory for prepaid SIMs in Belgium as an anti-terrorism measure, and the carriers enforce it. BASE accepts registration through its app with a photo of your international passport plus a biometric selfie; Proximus and Orange generally expect a shop visit with your passport.

### Purchase options for Belgium eSIMs

Only with a mobile subscription through a MyProximus account, which is built for residents — or in person at a Proximus shop. That is why virtually all travel eSIMs serving Belgium roam onto Belgian networks as a guest instead of registering you locally.

### Local SIM vs travel eSIM: Belgium edition

Carrier shops, supermarkets such as Carrefour, Delhaize and Colruyt, newsagents and night shops selling top-up vouchers, and petrol stations. Brussels Airport has Proximus, Orange and BASE counters, but they sell physical SIMs only — and there is no SIM counter at Brussels-Midi if you arrive by Eurostar.

### Proximus vs Orange: coverage compared

In towns and along main roads, yes — Bastogne, La Roche-en-Ardenne, Durbuy and Spa all work. On minor roads, forest trails and especially the deep Semois, Ourthe and Lesse valleys, expect gaps on every carrier. Download offline maps before you leave the last city.

### Will Belgium carriers work with your phone?

Not if it depends on 3G, because Belgium's 3G networks were switched off during 2025. 2G survives until operators retire it across 2028–2029, so a 2G-only handset has a little life left — but a phone that falls back to 2G for voice will lose calls unless you enable VoLTE.

### Which carrier reaches the most of Belgium?

Coverage in stations and platforms is generally good on all three networks; tunnels have patchy spots. Above ground, the EU quarter's weekday congestion is a bigger speed factor than carrier choice.

### Where 5G works in Belgium

Not as a decision criterion. Even the best 5G availability is 19.6%, so 5G is a bonus you get sometimes rather than a layer you can plan around. The 4G networks are fast enough that most travel tasks never notice.

### Are WhatsApp calls usable in Belgium?
WhatsApp is tied to your original number, not to the SIM providing data, so it works unchanged over a data-only eSIM. Calls over WhatsApp, FaceTime or Signal replace the voice service a data-only plan cannot provide.

### Belgium coverage, region by region

All three cover the coastal strip from De Panne to Knokke well, and the 67 km Kusttram line holds signal the whole way. The trams and the busy summer weekends slow everything down equally; network brand is not the differentiator there.

### Can one eSIM work in Belgium and France?

Only a multi-country plan. A Belgium-only profile stops at the border, and cross-border trips are the norm here — Brussels to Lille is barely over an hour. If France, the Netherlands or Luxembourg are on the itinerary, pick a regional eSIM rather than two country plans.

### Meet the Belgium networks

Effectively no. The three networks converge in the cities and differ mainly in the countryside, where whichever has a cell closer to you wins — and that changes from one village to the next.
