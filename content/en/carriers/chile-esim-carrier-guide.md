## Where to purchase a Chile eSIM

Chile has four real mobile networks — **Entel**, **Movistar**, **Claro** and **WOM** — on top of a much smaller prepaid player, Virgin Mobile. One name you will see in older speed articles is not in that group. **Mundo Pacífico (now Mundo)** is a fixed-broadband ISP: Ookla named it the world's fastest fixed provider, and its CLP-priced fibre plans have nothing to do with an eSIM, which is a mobile product. If a comparison table lists Mundo alongside Entel for a Chilean travel SIM, the table is measuring the wrong technology.

### Entel vs Movistar: which is better in Chile?

Entel is the carrier most visitors should default to. Its 4G/5G footprint is the widest in Chile, it holds one of the largest 5G spectrum allocations, and it is the network that keeps working in towns on the Carretera Austral. It is also the carrier most likely to refuse a foreigner at the counter: Entel stores have a reputation for turning away tourists who want a prepaid chip registered on a passport, so budget for a second store if the first says no. The workaround is a bolsa loaded onto a chip bought elsewhere, or an eSIM that never touches a store.

### Rural coverage in Chile: Entel vs Movistar

Movistar is the value-and-coverage compromise. Its published prepaid offer — around **CLP 5,000 for 40 GB plus 500 minutes over 30 days** — is the single most-cited tourist deal in the market, and its rural reach is second only to Entel's. For the far north, the Lake District and Puerto Natales it is the carrier most travelers regret not choosing after the fact. Note the brand transition: Movistar's Ecuadorian business moved to Millicom, but the Chilean operation remains Telefónica's.

### Who checks your identity when you buy a Chile SIM?

Claro is the largest operator in Latin America by subscriber count and the friendliest of the three at registration — it publishes a **"soy extranjero"** passport-based path, which matters because the prepaid line now has to be tied to a verified identity. Its prepaid packs are aggressive: roughly **CLP 5,000 for 50 GB plus 500 minutes and social-media data over 30 days**. What you give up is rural depth; outside the big cities Claro's map thins faster than Entel's or Movistar's.

### Chile data deals on a budget

WOM built its brand on price and still offers the cheapest large bolsa in the market — around **CLP 8,000 for 24 GB with unlimited minutes over 30 days** — plus 7-day unlimited bolsas near CLP 4,990. Virgin Mobile starts lower still, from roughly CLP 2,000. Both are perfectly good for a Santiago-and-Valparaíso trip and a poor choice for anyone heading south or north out of the central valley.

## Your eSIM data sizing: how much you actually need

Chile's cheap local data disguises how quickly a travel plan can run dry, because the things travelers do here are data-heavy: navigation with live traffic on long drives, photo backup from a week of touring, and video calls home from a hostel that advertises Wi-Fi and delivers none. A useful rough budget is 500 MB per day for maps, messaging and light browsing; 1 GB per day if you tether a laptop; and 2 GB per day if you upload video or stream. A two-week Atacama-and-Patagonia route should start at 10 GB, mostly because offline maps need to be downloaded once and redownloaded when they update. If your itinerary includes a live-streamed trek or remote work, buy 20 GB and stop thinking about it.

### Who runs mobile networks in Chile

Chilean LTE runs mainly on bands **2, 4, 7 and 28**, with 5G concentrated in **n78 (3500 MHz)**. Any recent iPhone, Samsung Galaxy or Pixel sold in the Americas or Europe covers that set. A handset built for a single Asian market may not, and no eSIM fixes a missing band — it will simply fall back to 3G or nothing.

### What paperwork does a Chile SIM need?

Chilean prepaid chips have been tied to an identity since 2024, and since **20 August 2026** the operator must verify the person behind a prepaid number — the regulator indicates a passport is acceptable for foreign users. In practice a passport is enough at Claro and at many kiosks; a **RUT** (the Chilean tax number) is what some Entel and Movistar counters ask for, and it is not something a tourist can produce. Separately, and more seriously: a phone brought from abroad only works on Chilean networks for about **30 consecutive days** from the first local SIM insertion unless its **IMEI is registered** through the Multibanda administrative registration. That check does not apply to international roaming or to a travel eSIM, which is one reason a roaming profile is the low-friction option for a long trip.

### Will 10 GB cover a Chile trip?

Chilean prepaid does not bill overage. When the bolsa is exhausted, either the speed collapses or data stops until you buy another pack, and the "unlimited" weekly bolsas are unlimited only at capped speed after a threshold. Check whether your chosen pack's validity runs from activation or from first connection — a 30-day bolsa that starts the moment you land is a poor match for a three-week trip with a delayed landing, while a profile with a deferred start date fits one.

### Do Android phones support Chile eSIMs?

Chile eSIMs work with most unlocked handsets from 2019 onward, including iPhone XR and later, Google Pixel 3 and later, and Samsung Galaxy S20 and later. Verify the exact model number, not the marketing name, against the [device compatibility checker](/compatibility/) before you travel. Two exclusions are common: mainland-China iPhone variants, whose eSIM hardware is disabled, and carrier-locked handsets, which reject any third-party profile.

### Is tethering allowed on Chile eSIMs?

Hotspot use is generally allowed, but the ceiling varies by plan — from a single extra device on budget packs to five on a managed profile. If your trip depends on tethering a laptop, check that the plan permits it rather than discovering the block in a hotel with no Wi-Fi. A second practical note: in Chile it is usually cheaper to buy one larger 30-day pack than to top up twice, so size the pack to your trip length rather than your first-day usage.

Puerto Natales works, the Torres del Paine entrances work, and the W trek does not — at any price, on any carrier. Pucón, Puerto Varas, Valdivia and Castro all have solid coverage, and the ferry crossings carry signal near the ports. ### Easter Island (Rapa Nui) coverage

Entel's 4G makes Hanga Roa and the airport road usable, and the island's regulated tourism infrastructure means most planning happens in town anyway. Outside Hanga Roa the island is offline, so download maps, book the rental car before you fly, and do not plan a video call from the moai.

## APN settings for Chile

Travel profiles manage their own access point names, so a Roami eSIM works in Chile with no manual entry. APN settings only matter if you buy a carrier line locally — from an Entel, Movistar or Claro counter — and your phone fails to pull its settings automatically.

**iPhone:** Settings → Cellular → select the line → Cellular Data Network. **Android:** Settings → Network & Internet → SIMs → Access Point Names.

The Chilean carriers print their APN on the prepay insert rather than publishing one universal value, so copy it exactly from the packaging or the carrier's own support page. The classic failure pattern is signal bars with no data: in almost every case, correcting the APN — or deleting and reinstalling the profile — restores the connection.

## When a Chile eSIM misbehaves: four local failure modes

Generic errors — a profile that will not download, a QR scan that fails, an eSIM that installs but never registers — are catalogued in the [eSIM error troubleshooting guide](/faq/esim-activation-errors-troubleshooting-guide/). The four patterns below are the Chilean ones.

**A. A locally bought SIM connects, then dies after about a month.** This is the Multibanda rule, not a fault. A phone brought from abroad gets roughly 30 days on Chilean networks from the first local SIM insertion; without IMEI registration it is blocked. A travel eSIM or roaming profile is never affected.

**B. Signal bars, no data, on a chip you bought in a store.** The line is registered but the bolsa was never loaded, or the operator's promotional data window has quietly expired. Check the balance in the operator's app, buy a bolsa, then restart the phone.

**C. Only 3G or 4G outside the city.** Coverage, not configuration. Open network selection and try each of Entel, Movistar, Claro and WOM manually before assuming the profile is broken — a multi-network profile does this for you.

**D. Data stops at the Argentine border, or on the Chilean side of a pass crossing.** A Chile-only plan ends at the border with no warning, which is why Mendoza and Bariloche day trips catch people out. Switch to a multi-country Americas plan before you cross.

## Crossing between Chile and Argentina with the same phone

Chilean and Argentine networks do not merge; they stop at the pass. The practical crossings are **Los Libertadores** (Santiago–Mendoza, with a long high-altitude queue and intermittent signal on both sides), **Cardenal Samoré** (Puerto Varas–Bariloche) and the southern **Integración Austral** toward Río Gallegos and Ushuaia. On any of them, a Chile-only profile goes dark the moment you clear migration, and a local Argentine chip bought on the other side cannot be loaded until you find a store in Mendoza or Bariloche.

Two habits make this painless. Buy a plan that covers both countries before you leave — a South America or Americas profile roams across the pass and switches to the Argentine network on arrival — and keep offline maps for the border zone itself, where neither network is dependable while the coach is standing still. If you are crossing more than once, the regional plan is cheaper than two local bolsas and saves a second registration.

## Common myths about your eSIM

### ❌ Myth: eSIMs in Chile only work in Santiago.

**✅ Fact:** Coverage follows the whole country — Entel and Movistar both reach Patagonian towns, the far north and Chiloé. What does not exist is coverage in the empty stretches between them, and that gap is a planning problem rather than a signal problem.

### Buying a SIM in Chile: ID rules

**✅ Fact:** A passport is enough for a travel eSIM, and the regulator allows operators to verify prepaid identity with a passport for foreign users. What a visitor still cannot produce is a RUT, which is why some carrier counters refuse them.

### ❌ Myth: Moving to an eSIM means slower data than a physical chip.

**✅ Fact:** Both attach to the same towers with the same bands at the same speeds. The difference is administrative: a managed eSIM arrives already configured, while a store-bought chip depends on identity registration and IMEI registration going smoothly.
