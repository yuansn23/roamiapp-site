## Crossing borders on an eSIM

The European Union built the most traveler-friendly roaming regime on earth, and understanding it correctly — including who it does not cover — saves you from the most common mistake on this page. The framework is called "roam like at home," it has been in force since 15 June 2017, and it runs to 2032. In plain terms: a customer of a mobile operator in one EU country who travels occasionally in another EU country pays domestic prices for calls, texts and data, with no roaming surcharge.

The regime's reach is precise, and the details matter. It covers the 27 EU member states plus the European Economic Area — Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway — and, since January 2026, Ukraine and Moldova have joined the roaming area too. The United Kingdom is the notable exclusion: after Brexit, the rules stopped applying to UK travel, and while some home operators voluntarily extend domestic rates there, it is a courtesy, not a right. Operators may also apply a fair-use data limit when you roam, and any surcharge beyond that limit is capped at the EU wholesale ceiling — €1.30 per GB in 2025, falling to €1 per GB from 2027. The European Commission's [official roaming page](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/consumers/internet-telecoms/mobile-roaming-costs/index_en.htm) lays all of this out in plain language, and it is the authoritative source if you want the fine print.

One genuinely useful nuance hides in these rules, and most travel guides miss it. If you buy a local prepaid SIM in France as a tourist, that SIM is a French customer's SIM, and it enjoys roam-like-at-home treatment when you take the train to Italy — domestic rates, occasional travel, entirely within the rules. Local connectivity in Europe is not just cheap; inside the EU and EEA it is portable, with no travel eSIM required for the EU-internal legs. Which raises the obvious question: if the rules are this good, why does anyone need anything else?

## Why those protections never reach a travel eSIM

Here is the answer, and it is the hinge of the whole decision. The EU's roaming protections attach to the operator-customer relationship, not to the traveler. They cover SIMs issued by an operator in an EU or EEA country to its own customers. A travel eSIM — including a European regional plan — is issued by a provider outside that system entirely. It is not an EU customer roaming; it is a wholesale product that roams by design, on commercial terms set by the provider. None of the EU's price caps, fair-use limits or notification requirements apply to it.

That sounds like a disadvantage until you look at what the travel product offers in exchange. A regional eSIM's "rules" are its country list: every market on the list, at one flat prepaid price, with no fair-use data cliff and no expiry-of-trip surprises. It covers the UK and Switzerland — both outside the roaming regime — on exactly the same terms as Belgium or Portugal, assuming they are on the list. And it skips the counter entirely: no passport registration, no shop hours, no language barrier in a Vienna suburb at 9 p.m. The EU rules protect a local SIM across borders; a regional plan removes borders from the purchasing decision altogether.

So the honest comparison is not "protected versus unprotected" but "protected and bounded versus unregulated and borderless." For a two-week, four-country trip, the second option wins on practically every dimension a traveler feels. The one exception is the single-country stay, where a local SIM's domestic rates and a real local number still make sense.

## Coverage by Region

A regional plan is only as good as its coverage list, and Europe's geography is full of traps for the unread list. The first rule of buying a European travel eSIM: the plan's list, not the map in your head, defines where you have data. Three markets cause nearly all of the trouble.

**Switzerland** is surrounded by the EU but is in neither the EU nor the EEA. It is a genuine separate market, with separate roaming economics — Cable.co.uk's pricing survey puts an average gigabyte there at **USD 7.29**, among the most expensive in the world, against a global average of USD 2.59. Two consequences follow. A single-country EU plan — the French SIM, the Italian SIM — goes dark or expensive the moment the train enters the Gotthard tunnel's other end. And a regional plan covers Switzerland only if the provider lists it, which most major ones do, but which you confirm rather than assume.

**Norway** is the counterintuitive one. It is not an EU member, but as part of the EEA it is inside the roam-like-at-home area — so locally-issued EU SIMs work there under the same rules as at home, and regional travel plans almost universally list it. Fjord-country coverage is a separate question: signal follows the coast and the valleys, and the high interior is genuinely empty.

**The United Kingdom** left the EU's roaming regime with Brexit. Nothing in European law obliges anyone to treat the UK as home territory anymore — yet most regional travel eSIMs include it anyway, because traveler behavior demanded it. Check the list anyway. The UK's position on a plan's country list is a commercial choice, and commercial choices change.

The discipline that avoids every one of these traps takes two minutes: before you buy any regional eSIM, lay your itinerary beside the plan's country list, market by market. Every country you will spend a night in should be on that list by name. If a day trip crosses into Switzerland, Switzerland must be named, not implied. ## What a regional eSIM does at each train border

Borders are where the regional product either earns its price or fails visibly, so it is worth knowing exactly what happens. When your train crosses from France into Germany, your phone does the same thing it always does on leaving one network's footprint: it searches, finds the partner carrier's signal on the plan's list, and re-registers. The whole sequence takes seconds and is invisible unless you are watching the operator name in your settings. No message from a home operator, no settings change, no restart. Data sessions may drop mid-download during the re-registration — if you are streaming, expect a pause, not an outage. That is the entire border experience on a properly chosen eSIM, and it repeats identically at every internal EU line.

Inside the EU and EEA, that handoff repeats at every internal border with no cost consequence, because the regional plan's price already accounted for it. The interesting borders are the three from the previous section. Crossing into Switzerland from France or Italy, a list-compliant regional plan re-registers onto a Swiss carrier and keeps working — the same flat bucket of data, now on Swisscom or a peer at whatever wholesale rate the provider arranged. A single-country plan does not make that handoff; it simply loses service. The same is true at the Channel, and on the Øresund crossing between Copenhagen and Malmö, where your plan's treatment of Denmark and Sweden should be identical — a [Denmark eSIM](/denmark-esim/) comparison is worthwhile only if Denmark is the whole trip.

One physical exception worth knowing: the Channel Tunnel itself. There is a stretch of roughly twenty minutes under the Channel where no mobile network reaches, on any plan, on any carrier. The connection drops after Folkestone and returns as the train surfaces in France. Plan around the gap rather than diagnosing it.

## The Europe SIM counter route, step by step, and when it still wins

Every page like this one recommends buying before you fly, and in Europe that advice is right more often than anywhere else. But the counter route has not disappeared, and it is worth knowing exactly what it involves — because for a minority of trips it is genuinely the better product.

**What a counter purchase actually runs like:**

1. Find the operator's shop or airport kiosk — Orange, Vodafone, Telekom and their national peers all staff arrivals halls in the big hubs.
2. Present your passport for the identity registration each country's rules require; expect five to fifteen minutes including the queue.
3. Choose a prepaid pack from the board; staff will install a physical SIM or, increasingly, provision an eSIM on the spot.
4. Confirm the line registers and data flows before you walk away — and ask for the APN in writing if it does not.
5. Keep the registration receipt; you will need it to top up or recover the line later.

**When the counter still wins**, four cases: you need a real local number for domestic calls and SMS codes, not just a French number; your stay is a month or longer, where local per-gigabyte pricing compounds in your favor; your handset cannot hold a travel profile at all; or you are paying cash by preference and a prepaid scratch-card economy suits you. For every other case — and that is the overwhelming majority of two-week itineraries — the pre-purchased regional profile wins on price, on time, and on the number of foreign-language conversations required per gigabyte.

## Where to Buy

Itineraries make this concrete, so here are the three classic European rail shapes, with the connectivity behavior at each hop.

**The Renaissance loop — Paris, Milan, Vienna, Prague.** The cultural core of the continent, and four EU markets where any competent regional list is comprehensive. Paris to Milan crosses the Alps into Italy; Milan to Vienna crosses or skirts Austria; Vienna to Prague enters Czechia. All four hops are EU-internal, so both a regional eSIM and a locally-bought French SIM enjoy clean treatment — the difference shows up only in the buying experience, which the regional plan wins decisively for a visitor. Watch the list for the scenic Gotthard and Brenner routings, which brush Switzerland's border.

**The Mediterranean loop — Barcelona, the Riviera, Rome, Athens.** Spain, France, Italy, Greece: four EU markets, plus the sea between them, where a ferry is effectively offline time on every carrier. This is the loop where per-country buying hurts most — four passport registrations at four counters — and where island interiors in Greece are the one coverage caveat worth planning for. Signal on the islands follows the coast and the towns; the caldera hike is fine, the interior hike may not be.

**The Nordic loop — Copenhagen, Stockholm, Oslo.** Denmark, Sweden, Norway: three wealthy, superbly connected markets, two of which are EU members with Norway as the EEA third. On a regional list that names all three, the loop just works, including the Oslo–Bergen railway's famously remote middle stretches where any carrier's signal comes and goes with the valleys. If you extend north of the Arctic Circle, you are beyond dense coverage on any product, and that is geography rather than a plan defect. One eSIM covers the loop; buying three country SIMs covers it three times over, at three counters, in three languages.

The meta-pattern across all three loops: an EU-only plan technically suffices on two of the three, but the margin is thin — one Swiss detour, one UK extension — and the price of that thinness is re-planning your trip around your connectivity. The regional plan is the only option that never asks you to do that.

## Europe eSIM data: how much do you need?

European travel is more data-hungry than most trips of the same length, for structural reasons. The continent's transit runs on apps: city metro systems, national rail operators, and journey planners like the ones most visitors keep open all day, all of which refresh maps and timetables continuously. Navigation compounds it — a day of city walking with live maps and transit directions is real usage, and unlike in a single-country trip, you cannot rely on knowing the streets.

Streaming is the swing factor, and trains are where it happens. A few hours of video on a high-speed train can consume several gigabytes, and Europe's intercity routes are exactly where you have signal for most of the journey — the exception being that Channel Tunnel stretch and the remote Alpine and Nordic segments. Add video calls for anyone mixing work into the trip, and the realistic planning band for a two-week, multi-country European itinerary is roughly 1 GB per day, with 20 GB covering even heavy users comfortably. Regional plans are priced in buckets, not per day, so the marginal gigabyte on a bigger bundle is cheap relative to the alternative of running dry mid-itinerary.

Two habits stretch any plan further. First, download offline maps and offline transit timetables for each city at the hotel — the connection you already paid for is better spent at breakfast than rationed on a tram. Second, let your home SIM stay asleep: data roaming off on the home line for the entire trip, so background traffic never bills against you, and your number still receives SMS verification codes over the cellular voice network.

## Setting up a Europe eSIM before departure day

The install is the same five-minute routine as any travel eSIM, and doing it at home is what makes the first border anticlimactic. Buy on Wi-Fi, scan the activation code, label the line, and set it as your data line with roaming enabled — then let it sleep until you land in the first country. It registers on a local carrier on touchdown, exactly as designed. The full walkthrough, with screenshots for iPhone and Android, is in our [the activation how-to](/faq/how-to-activate-an-esim/).

Before buying, run the two checks that prevent almost all failures. Compatibility: iPhones from the XS onward, Samsung's S20 generation and later, and Google Pixel 3 onward are safe bets, but the [eSIM compatibility page](/compatibility/) is the authoritative list, and it catches the regional variants — mainland-China iPhones among them — where the hardware is disabled. **The pre-purchase checklist, compressed:**

- **Device supports eSIM** — EID visible at `*#06#`, or confirmed on the compatibility page
- **Phone is unlocked** — "No SIM restrictions" in the carrier-lock settings screen
- **QR code saved twice** — a screenshot on the phone *and* a copy in cloud email
- **Card accepted at checkout** — travel eSIM checkouts take foreign cards; local operator storefronts do not always
- **Passport handy** — required if you choose a locally-issued pack like Orange Holiday, which registers the buyer
- **Profile installed on home Wi-Fi** — never over airport Wi-Fi on arrival day

## When a Europe eSIM misbehaves: border, cap and tunnel fixes

Four failure patterns are specific to this continent, and all four are diagnosable from your settings screen in under a minute.

**A. The plan goes dark crossing into Switzerland or the UK.** If your plan's list omits the country you just entered, the phone either attaches to nothing or roams on a network your plan does not cover. Fix: check the country list first, then force a manual network selection under Settings → Mobile Networks. If the plan genuinely excludes the market, no setting rescues it — that is the coverage-list trap from earlier, and the fix happens at purchase, not on the train.

**B. Data collapses mid-trip on a locally-bought SIM.** This is the RLAH fair-use cap doing its job: a French SIM used heavily in Italy can exhaust its roaming data allowance and slow to a crawl even though domestic data remains fast. The domestic bundle still works at home rates, and topping up restores roaming speed — with any surcharge capped at the EU wholesale ceiling. Travel eSIMs are immune to this particular cliff because they price every border into one flat bucket.

**C. Hotspot refuses to work or crawls.** Tourist packs — Orange Holiday among them — often cap tethered data separately from handset data, sometimes at a daily slice. Verify the tethering clause before blaming the profile, and if laptop work matters, favor a fixed-data plan over an unlimited-with-asterisks one.

**D. The connection drops exactly at a tunnel or sea crossing.** The Channel Tunnel's twenty-minute blackout, the Gotthard and long ferry stretches are physics, not configuration. If bars vanish between two known-covered points, wait — the profile re-registers by itself on the far side, usually within seconds of exiting.

Anything outside these four — a profile that will not download, an install that never registers, a scan that fails — is device-level rather than Europe-level, and the [error-by-error troubleshooting catalogue](/faq/esim-activation-errors-troubleshooting-guide/) covers those in the order you will meet them. The [eSIM setup guide](/faq/how-to-activate-an-esim/) remains the reference for the install itself.

## Europe eSIM questions the decision page should answer

### Using a Europe eSIM abroad

No. Roam-like-at-home rules cover customers of EU and EEA operators, and a travel eSIM is not one. The plan roams by commercial design instead, which is why its country list — not EU regulation — defines your coverage. The upside of being outside the regime is that the plan's flat price already prices every border on the list.

### Local SIM vs travel eSIM in Europe

For a multi-country trip, usually not. A locally-bought EU SIM genuinely does roam at domestic rates inside the EU and EEA, so the French SIM works in Italy — but you registered a passport at a counter for that privilege, and the plan still has no treatment for Switzerland or the UK unless you investigate. The local SIM wins on long single-country stays and on getting a real local number.

### Is Switzerland included in a Europe regional eSIM?

It depends entirely on the provider's list, and Switzerland is the single most important name to check because it sits inside so many itineraries — Milan, the Gotthard route, Geneva day trips — while being outside the EU and EEA entirely. Most major regional plans include it. Verify it by name before purchase.

### What about the UK and Norway?

Norway, as an EEA member, sits inside the EU roaming area, and regional plans list it nearly universally. The UK is outside all EU roaming rules post-Brexit; inclusion in a regional plan is a commercial decision that most major providers make, but that you confirm on the list rather than assume. Both countries work like any other on a compliant regional plan.

### Europe eSIM plans and prices

Yes, within the plan's validity window and data bucket — many regional bundles run 30 days, which covers the classic month-in-Europe trip with room to top up if the itinerary grows. Check the validity start condition, which on most products begins when the eSIM first registers in Europe, not at purchase.

### How do I know how much data to buy?

Size to the itinerary, not the continent: 1 GB per day is a realistic ceiling for navigation, transit apps, messaging and social sharing, so a 15–20 GB bundle comfortably covers two weeks including train streaming. If you work remotely, add the video-call load on top. Top-ups apply to the installed profile, so under-buying is recoverable.

### Are anonymous SIMs legal in Europe?

For a locally-issued pack like Orange Holiday, yes — French rules require identity registration for prepaid lines, handled online at purchase or at the point of sale. For a travel eSIM issued outside Europe, usually not, because the provider is not an EU operator and is not issuing a European customer line. Either way you never stand in a counter queue.

### Can I use a Europe eSIM on ferries and flights?

On a ferry, only in port or within coastal coverage — the middle of the Adriatic is satellite territory, and ship Wi-Fi is a separate paid service. In-flight connectivity runs on the aircraft's own system, not a mobile network, so your eSIM profile has nothing to attach to at altitude. Plan both as offline time and sync beforehand.

### Can I share my Europe plan with a travel partner?

Only if the plan permits tethering, which varies by product — check the clause before buying if you plan to consolidate two people onto one profile. Two separate cheap profiles are usually the more resilient arrangement anyway: one phone losing service does not take both travelers offline.

### Which operator wins on speed in Europe?

On the west-coast corridors — Paris–Brussels, Milan–Rome, much of the German network — expect continuous 4G with 5G patches and enough throughput to stream. Speed dips in tunnels and mountain sections are normal and re-cover within seconds of exiting. The unbroken stretches are the exception rather than the rule, and they cluster on the scenic routes listed in the coverage table above.

### How does a regional profile's validity actually start?

Most travel eSIMs start their validity clock when the profile first registers on a network in the region, not at purchase — so buying a week before departure consumes nothing. Confirm the start condition in the plan's terms, because a minority of products start the clock at checkout, and that difference matters if your dates might slip.

### Does one eSIM cover the micro-states — Monaco, Andorra, Liechtenstein, San Marino?

Not automatically, and this is a genuinely common surprise. The micro-states sit geographically inside Europe but are separate markets, and a plan's country list treats them as such — Andorra and San Marino appear on Orange Holiday's roster, while Monaco is the one travelers most often miss before a Nice-based trip. The Nice–Monte Carlo day trip is the classic case: check the list for Monaco by name, or expect your phone to fall off the plan's coverage the moment the train crosses into the principality.

Profiles do not expire from disuse on most travel products, and re-enabling an installed profile for a return trip is a settings toggle — though the data bucket and validity will have run down, so expect to top up or add a fresh bundle rather than to reuse the original allowance. On a travel eSIM, data simply stops — no overage billing, no silent charges — and a top-up through the provider's app or dashboard restores it on the same installed profile, no reinstall needed. On a locally-bought EU SIM roaming abroad, the fair-use cap slows you rather than stopping you until you recharge. Either way, do the top-up from a hotel connection rather than a station platform; the dashboard needs a working internet link to reach.

Mostly, with the same gaps as any rail corridor. Night trains alternate coverage with long dark stretches through countryside and tunnels; Vienna–Venice and Paris–Nice are the classics where half the journey is offline. Board with everything synced, and treat the sleeping hours as the backup plan.
