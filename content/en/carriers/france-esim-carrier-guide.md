Picking a France eSIM means choosing between four networks that split the wins — speed, price and shop-only tourist rules — and this guide breaks down each one. **If you read nothing else:** France runs four mobile networks, and only one of them sells a tourist eSIM you can buy without setting foot in a shop. On speed, Orange is the network to beat: a median 5G download of **281.3 Mbps**, the fastest overall network at 122.77 Mbps, and the best 5G consistency (77.9%) in the country per Ookla's H2 2024 report.

Free Mobile posts the highest 5G availability (74.0%) and the lowest prices — its entry made French mobile data among the cheapest in Europe (1 GB costs about USD 0.20, 9th of 237 markets). SFR and Bouygues Telecom sit in between: strong networks, but with no visitor-friendly prepaid eSIM worth the detour. For almost every visitor, the practical choice is a [France travel eSIM](/france-esim/) that roams onto these networks — or, if you want a French phone number with calls and texts, Orange Travel, the rebranded Orange Holiday eSIM, bought online before you fly. After guiding enough travelers through their first France eSIM, we know the usual confusion points; the sections below address them one by one.

France is both Europe's biggest tourist destination and one of its cheapest data markets, yet its carriers are oddly unwelcoming at the point of sale: the standard prepaid paths assume you live there. This guide walks through what each of the four networks is actually good at, which one you can realistically buy from as a visitor, and the coverage realities that no headline ranking shows — the Paris metro, the TGV corridors, and the empty middle of the country.

Two independent measurement systems back everything below. Ookla's Speedtest Intelligence ranks the four networks on speed, consistency and quality of experience. ARCEP, the French regulator, measures them again on real transport corridors and publishes a per-address coverage map. Together they answer both questions a visitor actually has: how fast is it, and will it work where I am going.

### Orange vs SFR 5G: which is better in France?

SFR recorded the **second-fastest 5G median at 226.19 Mbps** — a figure that would be a national headline in most of Europe — behind Orange's 281.3 Mbps and ahead of Bouygues's 196.54 Mbps. On overall download it measured 101.82 Mbps, third of the four.

**Buying in as a visitor:** SFR is the awkward one. Its own stores frequently do not sell prepaid tourist SIMs and will point you to a kiosk or a convenience store instead, and the account flows assume French payment methods. SFR La Carte starter packs do exist in retail channels, but there is no visitor-oriented eSIM product.

**What it costs:** the retail prepaid route exists, but because it is not designed for visitors, the effective cost includes the time spent finding a shop that will transact.

**What goes wrong:** the mismatch between the network and the counter. Nothing about SFR's radio performance is the problem; the purchase path is.

**Where it pays off:** it mostly does not, for visitors. On a multi-network travel eSIM you will use SFR towers when they are nearest with no effort at all — which is the sensible way to get access to this network.

### Bouygues Telecom: the best fixed line and the friendliest prepaid counter

Bouygues is third on mobile 5G (196.54 Mbps) but **first on fixed broadband**: 305.15 Mbps down, 245.58 Mbps up at 19 ms, with the best fixed consistency at 86.5%. On ARCEP's independent mobile audit it tied Orange at the top of the high-density table for pages loaded in under five seconds (94%) and for video streaming (97%).

**Buying in as a visitor:** this is the exception worth knowing. Bouygues stores are the one operator's own shops that reliably sell a tourist prepaid SIM over the counter, and the chain's prepaid cards are stocked in tabacs, Relay kiosks and supermarkets.

**What it costs:** a Bouygues tourist prepaid bundle is a practical buy if you want a physical French card on arrival; the eSIM route is still Orange Travel or a travel eSIM.

**What goes wrong:** the mobile network is the weakest of the three big ones in rural France — 71% of rural pages under five seconds against Orange's 79% — and on TGV lines Bouygues measured last, with 58% of calls held and 61% of pages loaded.

**Where it pays off:** a Paris stay where you want a physical French SIM bought in person with minimal friction, and any hotel with Bouygues fibre in the walls.

### Lowest-priced plans in France

Free's network position is unusual: it recorded the **highest 5G availability in the country at 74.0%** while finishing last on ARCEP's national voice-quality table (84% of calls of perfect quality, against Orange's 91%). It built its network late and still leans on a national roaming agreement with Orange outside its own footprint, which is exactly why it can put most of its users on 5G while still measuring behind its hosts on rural reliability.

**Buying in as a visitor:** Free sells online and through self-service machines and kiosks, with French-only interfaces and account flows that assume a French bank card. There is no tourist prepaid product.

**What it costs:** nominal prices are the lowest in the market; the real cost for a visitor is the friction of not being able to buy one easily.

**What goes wrong:** the gap between Free's subscriber experience and a visitor's. Free's roaming allowance is bundled into its plans rather than sold as separate passes, but that generosity is for its own subscribers — the products a short-stay visitor can actually reach are not the €2 and €19.99 plans.

**Where it pays off:** indirectly, everywhere in France. Free's 2012 launch set the price ceiling for the whole market, which is why the travel eSIM you buy for France costs a fraction of the equivalent in Canada or the United States.

**What you need.** A passport is the document that matters. Operators commonly also record an address, and a hotel or rental address is normally accepted — this is a registration formality rather than a residency test, and there is no biometric or selfie step of the kind Belgium requires for prepaid cards.

## Local SIM vs Travel eSIM Costs

Free Mobile's role in this market is not a marketing line — it is measurable. The operator launched on 10 January 2012 with exactly two plans: **€2 per month** (60 minutes, 60 SMS) and **€19.99 per month** with unlimited voice, SMS and data. It signed up 2.6 million subscribers in under three months, and the shock forced the whole market down: France's statistics institute INSEE calculated that mobile plan prices fell around 9.5% in the first nine months of 2012, and the average French mobile bill dropped from €27.30 in 2012 to €14.30 a decade later. Free has pledged to keep the €2 and €19.99 price points unchanged until at least 2027, per parent group iliad's reporting.

Two caveats are relevant to visitors. First, Free built its network late and still leans on a national roaming agreement with Orange outside its own footprint — its coverage is population-wide for 4G but thinner in remote areas than Orange's. Second, Free sells online and through a handful of stores and kiosks; there is no tourist prepaid product, and the account flows assume a French bank card. So Free is the reason your travel eSIM is cheap, more than it is a carrier you will buy from directly.

If you want a French number cheaply and have the patience for paperwork, a Free SIM (about €10) plus the €19.99 plan remains the resident-style budget route. For stays measured in days, the friction outweighs the saving.

### Taking your France eSIM overseas

Three things about Free's roaming are worth knowing before you plan around it.

**The allowance is bundled, not sold separately.** Free includes international roaming inside its plans rather than selling a roaming pass on top, which is the opposite of how some competitors price it. That generosity, however, is a subscriber benefit: it attaches to the plan, the account and the French billing relationship, none of which a short-stay visitor has.

**Roaming has a fair-use ceiling, by law.** EU rules are written for periodic travel. If over a four-month window you spend more time and data abroad than at home, your operator may contact you and then apply a surcharge capped at the regulated wholesale rate. This applies to any French line, Free included — it is not generous roaming, it is regulated roaming.

**Free's national roaming is a different thing entirely.** Free's dependence on Orange outside its own footprint is a domestic arrangement, not an EU one, and it is why Free's coverage is described as population-wide for 4G rather than comprehensive. When you read that Free is "on Orange where it matters", that is this agreement, not Roam Like At Home.

For a visitor, the practical upshot is that Free's roaming reputation is not something you can buy. What you can buy is a travel eSIM that runs on the same four networks without needing Free's account at all.

## France eSIM coverage: Orange vs SFR

Every carrier publishes a coverage map, and every carrier's map is a marketing document. France has something better: **ARCEP**, the regulator, runs [monreseaumobile.arcep.fr](https://monreseaumobile.arcep.fr/), where you can check declared coverage and measured quality at a specific place rather than at a province level.

**How to use it for a specific address.** Enter the address — your hotel, the farmhouse you rented, the venue of the wedding you are attending — and the tool returns the declared coverage from each of the four operators for voice and SMS, 4G data and 5G data, broken down into outdoor coverage, indoor coverage and major transport routes. Coverage is expressed in four levels: no coverage, limited coverage, good coverage and very good coverage, where "very good" is defined as outdoor data pretty much everywhere and indoor data in most buildings. That indoor/outdoor split is the part carrier maps never show you, and it is exactly where an old stone building in the Marais or a chalet in the Alps behaves differently from the field next to it.

**How to use it for a route.** The same tool accepts transport corridors, so you can look up a specific motorway, a TGV line, an RER branch or the métro systems of Lille, Lyon, Marseille, Paris and Toulouse, and see the measured quality of each operator on it. This is how you answer the question "will I be able to take a call on the train to Lyon" before you buy anything.

**What it will not tell you.** The declared coverage layer is a modelled prediction that ARCEP verifies for accuracy; the quality layer comes from ARCEP's own field testing, which is a snapshot of a test campaign rather than a live feed. Neither predicts the cell you are standing under at 18:00 on a Friday. Use the tool to rule out an address, not to promise yourself full bars.

**If it is wrong, say so.** The site also lets you report a coverage problem or a discrepancy between the published map and your own experience — a genuinely useful option if you are staying somewhere the map claims is covered and your phone disagrees.

## Rural coverage in France: which carrier wins?

France's 30th-place global ranking for mobile speed (median 132.91 Mbps, August 2026, per Ookla's Speedtest Global Index) hides as much variation as any stat on this page. The map breaks down like this:

- **The Paris métro** is better than its reputation. Stations and platforms have coverage from all four operators, and many central lines hold signal in tunnels; outer lines still have gaps. Anticipation beats frustration: download your transit app's offline schedules.
- **TGV corridors** are mostly strong. The high-speed lines out of Paris toward Lyon, Bordeaux, Lille and Brussels pass through populated country, and Orange — the network that carries most passengers best in testing — holds data well enough for video calls for long stretches. Tunnel sequences and cuttings still drop calls.
- **The countryside is the real variable.** France is Europe's largest country by area after Russia and Ukraine, and the Massif Central, the deep Loire hinterland and parts of Normandy and Brittany thin out quickly on secondary roads. Towns are covered; the road between two towns may not be. Before a rural leg, check ARCEP's official [coverage maps](https://www.arcep.fr/) rather than a carrier's marketing map — ARCEP publishes measured 2G/4G/5G coverage per operator per commune.
- **The Riviera in August** is a congestion story: every network slows when 20 million visitors arrive. Early-morning connections work; 8pm on the Nice seafront does not.

### What ARCEP's 2025 mobile audit found in France

ARCEP's [2025 mobile quality-of-service audit](https://en.arcep.fr/news/press-releases/view/n/mobile-quality-of-service-in-metropolitan-france-201125.html), published in November 2025 from more than a million measurements taken in every department between June and August 2025, is the most useful dataset a traveller can read, because it measures the corridors rather than the cities. The findings, by form of transport:

| Where you are | Calls held for 2 minutes (average) | Web page under 5 seconds (average) | Best operator |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Metro lines | 96–97% across all four | 93% | Orange (95% web, 97% calls) |
| RER and Transilien | 81–88% | 84% | Orange and Bouygues (85%) |
| Roads | 92–95% | 87% | Orange (90%) |
| Intercités and TER | 52–63% | 65% | Orange (69%) |
| TGV high-speed lines | 56–74% | 64% | Orange (70% web, 74% calls) |

Read the bottom two rows again, because they contradict the assumption that fast trains mean good data. On TGV lines, a web page loaded in under five seconds in only **64% of cases on average**, and Bouygues measured 61% on browsing and 58% on calls. Intercités and TER regional trains were no better at 65%. The Paris métro, meanwhile, came out best of all the corridors at 93% for browsing and 96–97% for calls — the opposite of what most travellers expect.

The rural picture from the same audit: **Orange led in rural areas on every speed threshold**, posting 79% of pages under five seconds against SFR's 75%, Free's 72% and Bouygues's 71%, and 14 Mbps average upload against SFR's 11, Bouygues's 9 and Free's 8. Nationally, perfect-quality calls ran 91% for Orange, 88% for Bouygues, 87% for SFR and 84% for Free.

The practical translation for a visitor: **if your itinerary is city-only, network choice is irrelevant. If it includes a TGV leg, a regional train or a rural road, Orange-based coverage — directly or through a multi-network travel eSIM — is worth seeking out.**

Border behaviour is the other thing travelers miss. A France-only eSIM goes dark the moment you cross into Belgium, Switzerland or Spain — and with Brussels barely 90 minutes from Paris by train, that happens more often than people plan for. ## Corsica and the Alps: where French seasonal load bites

The two big holiday regions fail in different ways, and neither failure is really about the network.

**Corsica** is covered on the same terms as mainland France. A France plan works there with nothing extra to buy — Orange Travel's own coverage list explicitly names Corsica — and the coastal towns are well served. Ajaccio, Bastia, Calvi and Porto-Vecchio all carry strong 4G and growing 5G, and the ferry ports hold signal as you arrive. The problem is the interior. Corsica is a mountain in the sea: the passes, the remote west-coast coves and the **GR20** trail run through terrain where there is no commercial reason to build coverage, and several mountain refuges have none at all. If you are walking the GR20, download the entire route before you start, carry a paper map, and if being contactable genuinely matters, that is a satellite messenger's job rather than any eSIM's.

**The Alps** fail differently, because the load pattern is seasonal rather than geographic. The resorts themselves are well covered — Val Thorens, Tignes, Val d'Isère, Courchevel and La Plagne all open from late November — and France remains the world's leading ski destination with **56.1 million skier days in the 2025/26 season**, per Domaines Skiables de France, the industry body. That volume concentrates into a short window: the Christmas and New Year weeks and the February half-term periods, when three different school-zone calendars overlap and the same valley hosts a full resort's worth of people.

What that means at the network level is congestion in a confined space, not coverage loss. A resort village has a cell built for its resident population; on a February Saturday it hosts several thousand extra devices asking the same mast for a map, a lift-status refresh and a photo upload. Mornings at the lift base and the après-ski hours in the village centre are when it shows. Higher up, the problem changes character: the high passes and the roads between resorts are genuinely thin, and a cable car or a valley road can drop you out of coverage entirely.

The Riviera follows the same logic in summer, as the existing metro-and-TGV section notes: every network slows when the August crowds arrive, and early-morning connections work where evening ones do not.

The rule for both regions is the same. Coverage where people live; congestion where people gather; nothing where there is only mountain. Download offline maps for the valley, keep the phone in the pocket at the lift base, and check ARCEP's per-address map before you commit to a remote rental.

## Cross-border coverage

France has six land neighbours and France-only plans die at every one of them, but three border zones cause disproportionate confusion because they do not behave the way travellers assume.

**Belgium** is the simplest case: inside the EU, inside the roaming zone, and 90 minutes from Paris by train. An EU-issued SIM needs nothing bought for the crossing, and a France-only travel eSIM stops at the border. If Brussels or Bruges is on the itinerary, buy a regional plan rather than two country plans.

**Geneva is the trap.** Switzerland is not in the EU and not in the EEA, so **Roam Like At Home does not cover it** — a French line that roams beautifully in Germany will be charged normal roaming rates the moment it attaches to Swisscom, Sunrise or Salt. This matters more than the map suggests, because the Geneva metropolitan area straddles the border, and border towns such as Annemasse and Ferney-Voltaire sit close enough that a French phone can find a Swiss network stronger than the French one. Two habits protect you: switch to manual network selection and pick a French operator before you get near the border, and if Switzerland is a real part of the trip, buy a plan that lists it.

**Monaco is the same trap in a smaller package.** The principality sits inside the French coastline, uses French train services, and is not part of the Roam Like At Home zone — so a French line can incur roaming charges during a day trip to Monte Carlo without any obvious sign that you left the country. Only a plan that names Monaco in its coverage list avoids this.

The underlying rule is worth memorising, because it is the opposite of how people reason about roaming: **Roam Like At Home depends on where the SIM was issued, not on where you are standing.** Belgium, Spain, Italy, Germany and Luxembourg are inside the zone; Switzerland, Monaco, Andorra, the UK and the Western Balkans are not. Coverage lists that explicitly name Switzerland and Monaco — as Orange Travel's does — are doing you a commercial favour, not a regulatory one.

## First, check your phone

France uses the standard European band set: 4G on 800 MHz (B20), 1800 MHz (B3) and 2600 MHz (B7); 5G on 700 MHz (n28), 2100 MHz (n1) and 3500 MHz (n78). Any unlocked handset sold in Europe in the last five years has all of them, and most global flagships do too. The two checks that actually cause failures are eSIM support and carrier lock — verify both in one pass with the [handset compatibility checker](/compatibility/), and if you want the underlying mechanics first, start with [the eSIM activation explainer](/faq/what-is-esim-activation-and-how-does-it-work/).

Two France-specific notes. First, **Orange Travel requires eSIM-capable hardware**, and Orange publishes its own compatibility list on the store page — check your model there before buying, because the plan is not sold as a physical SIM on that route. Second, **a locked phone will fail on any French network**, and the failure looks like an activation error rather than a lock error, which sends people hunting for the wrong problem. Check Settings → General → About → Carrier Lock first.

## APN Settings That Actually Work

Travel eSIM profiles carry their own APN and configure themselves — do not touch anything unless data fails. If you buy direct from a French carrier and automatic setup does not run, these are the values:

| Carrier | APN | Username | Password |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Orange | `orange.fr` | orange | orange |
| SFR | `sl2sfr` | blank | blank |
| Bouygues Telecom | `bouyguestelecom` | blank | blank |
| Free Mobile | `free` | blank | blank |

iPhone: Settings → Cellular → tap the eSIM line → Cellular Data Network. Android: Settings → Connections → Mobile Networks → Access Point Names → add a new one. Save, toggle airplane mode, retest.

**When a manual APN is genuinely required:** a carrier-issued prepaid card whose automatic configuration did not run; a profile installed from a typed activation code rather than a scanned QR; a handset old enough to ignore carrier settings. On a managed travel eSIM it is essentially never required, and on Orange Travel the APN configures itself — the Orange network sets it on first attach, so entering anything by hand is more likely to break a working line than fix one.

**Where to find it on your phone, exactly:** on iPhone the path is Settings → Cellular → your eSIM line → Cellular Data Network, and the fields stay blank until you tap into them. On Android the path is Settings → Connections → Mobile Networks → Access Point Names, then the plus icon to add a new entry and the three-dot menu to save. If the eSIM, rather than your home SIM, is the line with data enabled, and the APN still does not matter, stop editing it and check the data-line and roaming toggles instead.

### Five France eSIM failures you will actually hit, and the fixes

Persistent errors — downloads that fail, QR codes already used, profiles that install but never register — are covered in our [eSIM activation errors troubleshooting guide](/faq/esim-activation-errors-troubleshooting-guide/). The five below are the ones France produces.

**A. No signal at all on arrival**
1. Confirm data roaming is enabled for the eSIM line specifically.
2. Restart the phone once after landing — the handset often keeps looking for home networks until it reboots.
3. Check the eSIM is enabled in the SIM manager and set as the data line.

**B. Orange Travel installs but will not connect**
1. Wait for the profile to complete its first attach; Orange sets the APN automatically on the network.
2. Do not register the eSIM until you actually need to — an one-time works immediately, and registration is only required to keep using it past 30 days.
3. If the plan window has opened and expired on a previous trip, top up from the app rather than reinstalling.

**C. Signal bars but no data**
1. Compare the APN with the table above — this is the one market where manual entry is occasionally needed.
2. Confirm the eSIM, not the home SIM, is the active data line.
3. Remember a data-only profile cannot receive SMS; keep the home SIM active for codes.

**D. Full bars, but calls fail**
1. A data-only travel eSIM has no voice service at all — use WhatsApp, FaceTime or Signal.
2. On Orange Travel, check the French number is active and the plan has not lapsed.
3. Confirm the handset is not locked to your home carrier, which blocks registration on French networks.

**E. Slow everywhere, on one network only**
1. A single-carrier profile is the cause; congestion and rural shadowing are on you.
2. Switch to a multi-network profile, or use manual selection to try another operator.
3. Re-test outside the peak window — on the Riviera in August, or at the lift base in February, the cell itself is the bottleneck.
