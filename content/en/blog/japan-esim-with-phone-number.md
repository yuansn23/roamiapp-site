**Short answer:** a normal travel eSIM for Japan is **data-only** — it gives you mobile internet, but no Japanese phone number and no SMS. If you need to receive a verification code, make an ordinary phone call, or hand a local number to a school, landlord or bank, you need a different product: a **voice + data eSIM** from one of the few providers that issues numbers to visitors, or a **dual-SIM setup** that keeps your home number alive next to your Japan data line.

This guide explains exactly which Japan eSIMs come with a phone number, who can actually get one, and what to do if you only need data but still have to receive one SMS.

## 1. What "a Japan eSIM with a phone number" actually means

The phrase gets used loosely, so it is worth separating three different things:

- **A Japanese number (070 / 080 / 090).** A real local mobile number that other people in Japan can call and text. Only voice-capable plans from Japanese providers issue these.
- **Your own number, kept alive.** Your home carrier's number stays active on a second line while a Japan data eSIM handles the internet. Nothing about your number changes — you simply keep receiving SMS on it.
- **A VoIP number.** An app-based number (WhatsApp, Google Voice, Skype or similar) that works over data. Useful for calls, but many services will not accept it for identity verification.

Most confusion comes from mixing these up. A cheap $10 Japan data eSIM solves the second and third cases well and the first not at all.

### A Japanese mobile number: 070, 080 and 090

Mobile lines in Japan begin with 070, 080 or 090. They can place and receive ordinary calls, send and receive SMS, and they are what a Japanese business, school or landlord means when it asks for a phone number. Only voice-capable plans sold by Japanese providers issue them, and they are the only one of the three options here that a Japanese service will treat as a normal mobile line.

### A 050 number: familiar prefix, narrower usefulness

050 is Japan's prefix for IP telephony. A 050 number looks domestic and costs very little to run, but it is not a mobile line, which decides what it can do. Some Japanese businesses accept it for a callback and many systems will not send it an SMS at all. In practice a 050 number solves the "I want people in Japan to be able to ring me" problem and leaves the "I need to pass SMS verification" problem exactly where it was.

### App-based and VoIP numbers

Numbers issued by messaging and calling apps live outside the Japanese numbering plan altogether. They are convenient for talking to hotels and restaurants and for calling home, and they are also the numbers most likely to be rejected outright by age checks, banking, ticketing and account-verification systems. If the only thing you need is a two-way voice channel, an app is enough. If the requirement is to prove you are reachable on a real number, it is not.

## 2. Do you actually need a Japanese phone number?

For a normal holiday, usually **no**. Maps, messaging apps, translation, restaurant bookings through apps and hotel check-in all work over data. Our [start-here overview](/blog/japan-esim-ultimate-guide/) covers the data-only path end to end.

### Where a number genuinely changes the trip

You are in the minority who genuinely needs a Japanese number if you:

- Expect **SMS verification** for a Japanese service (a domestic app, a membership, some booking platforms).
- Will **sign a lease, open a school account, or start a job** in Japan.
- Need to **receive calls from Japanese businesses** that will not use messaging apps.
- Are staying **long enough** that a local number becomes part of daily life (roughly a month or more).

If none of those apply, a data-only eSIM plus your home number is cheaper and simpler than any voice plan.

### Why the Japanese providers work differently from the app-based brands

Voice plans are sold by Japanese **MVNOs** — operators that lease capacity from Docomo, au or SoftBank rather than running their own network. Two practical consequences follow. Some of them require you to enter **APN settings** by hand after installation, so budget a few minutes for setup rather than expecting a one-tap QR code. And if you choose a physical SIM, it has to be collected at an airport counter or shipped to a Japanese address, which is awkward if you have already landed.

The hybrid row is the one people overlook. If all you need is to receive the occasional bank or login code, you do **not** need a Japanese number at all — you need your existing number to stay reachable, which dual SIM handles by default.

## 4. Which Japan eSIMs come with calls and SMS in 2026

Only a handful of providers will issue a real Japanese number to someone who is not a resident. Three names come up repeatedly, and they are not interchangeable.

### Mobal — the tourist-friendly default for a real Japanese number

Mobal is built specifically for foreign visitors and offers voice + data eSIM plans from around **¥1,650 per month**, with a real **070 / 080 / 090** number. Identity is verified with your passport: either present the original at a pickup point in Japan, or upload your ID before the SIM is shipped. Because it is designed for visitors, English support is standard rather than an afterthought. They also sell a 5G unlimited **tourist data eSIM** if you decide you only need data after all.

### Sakura Mobile — English support on the Docomo network

Sakura Mobile runs on the **Docomo** network, which has the widest rural coverage in Japan, and its voice + data eSIM starts at around **¥3,278 per month** including tax plus a **¥5,500** setup fee. It is a common choice for students and working-holiday travellers. One caution worth repeating: voice plans in Japan are subject to strict identity rules, and Sakura Mobile's own documentation notes that voice + data service may require **photo ID issued in Japan** (a residence card or Japanese driver's licence). Requirements vary by product, so confirm with the provider before you pay.

### HanaCell — for people who live outside Japan and visit often

HanaCell issues a real Japanese number that you keep between trips, priced at around **US$69** for the SIM plus about **US$12 per year** from the second year. The trade-off is explicit: it is **not available to people who live in Japan**, and applications and support are in Japanese only. If you visit Japan several times a year and want one stable number, it is a good fit; for a single trip it is overkill.

### International eSIMs with calling: what they do and don't give you

Several international eSIM brands sell plans that include a limited number of **outgoing call minutes** or an add-on calling feature. Those minutes are typically provided through a voice partner and usually give you a **non-Japanese number** — useful for calling hotels or restaurants, not for receiving Japanese SMS. If a plan's description says "calls included" without saying it provides a Japanese number, assume it does not.

## 5. Why a Japanese Voice eSIM Cannot Simply Be Emailed to You

### The delivery rule, in plain terms

This is the part of buying a Japanese number that surprises people most, and it follows directly from the identity rules. Because a voice line has to be tied to a verified identity, at least one of the main visitor-facing providers states plainly that its voice and data eSIM **cannot be sent by email**. The access code has to be posted to an address outside Japan, or collected in person in the country. In other words, you apply in advance, upload your identification, and wait for delivery before the number can be activated.

### What activation actually involves

When the package arrives, you enter the eSIM's 32-digit EID on the provider's website, and the profile is then enabled with your new Japanese number assigned to it. This is not a five-minute airport task like scanning a travel eSIM QR code. Budget days rather than minutes, and start the process before you book anything that depends on being reachable on a Japanese number the moment you land.

### Why data-only brands can be instant and voice plans cannot

An international data-only eSIM is not a Japanese voice contract. It carries no number, makes no call, and therefore sits outside the verification regime entirely — which is why it can arrive as an instant emailed QR code. The emailed QR code and the posted access code are not two versions of the same product; they are two different products under two different sets of rules.

## 6. Japan's SIM Rules Changed in 2026: What Visitors Should Know

### What the amendment does

If you are buying a real Japanese number, the 2026 reform matters mostly for what it does not change. Proof of identity was already mandatory for a voice line carrying an 070, 080 or 090 number before the amendment, and it stays mandatory afterwards — the number is the part of a Japanese SIM that has always been tied to a verified person.

What the reform adds is symmetry: checks are being extended to **data-only** SIMs that previously escaped them, and carriers are expressly permitted to accept a passport from a foreign national with no Japanese address, which covers the short-stay traveller. Carriers also gain the right to turn away a customer who already holds an excessive number of registered lines, a measure aimed at bulk-registration fraud. The commencement date is fixed by cabinet order rather than by the date of passage, so counter practice can lag the text of the law.

### Which purchases it touches, and which it does not

Three cases, in descending order of how much you will notice. A data-only eSIM bought from an international provider before you fly is not a Japanese SIM contract and has never involved an identity check, so nothing changes there. A voice plan with a real 070, 080 or 090 number has always required verification, and still does — and that is the product this guide is about. Prepaid data SIMs bought at a Japanese counter sit in the middle, and that is the purchase where the paperwork is genuinely changing.

### What this means for your trip

Carry your passport whenever you set out to buy a SIM in Japan, and arrange a Japanese number before departure rather than after arrival, because verification and delivery are the steps that consume time. If you only need data, the 2026 changes do not touch you at all — which is another argument for deciding on the number first and the data plan second. The counter-by-counter detail, including what each airport channel requires, is in our [guide to buying a Japan SIM card](/blog/japan-sim-card-guide/).

## 7. Can a tourist get a Japanese phone number without a residence card?

Yes, but only through the foreigner-friendly route. Japan's mainstream carriers — **Docomo, au, SoftBank and Rakuten Mobile** — require a **residence card**, which is only issued for eligible stays of 90 days or more. That rules out a standard carrier contract for tourists, short business trips and most digital nomads.

### The two workarounds that actually exist

The workaround is that a small number of providers operate in the gap: **Mobal**, **HanaCell** (overseas residents only) and **Sakura Mobile** will issue a number without a residence card, subject to passport or photo-ID verification. In other words, you can get a Japanese number, but not the cheap mainstream plans — and you should expect a verification step rather than a fully instant online signup.

## 8. What a Japanese Number Actually Unlocks

### The bookings that still assume a phone call

Away from the cities, a working phone number is still the default way to confirm things. Ryokan and minshuku, particularly family-run ones, often confirm a reservation by phone rather than email. Reporting an incident with a rental car usually means a call to the rental desk. Booking a clinic appointment, or arranging for a delivery to arrive at a set hour, both assume someone can reach you. None of these are impossible without a number — hotels will often call on your behalf — but each of them is easier with one.

### The applications that will not accept anything else

The harder constraint is digital. A number of Japanese services only accept a Japanese **mobile** number for verification: app accounts, membership and points schemes, and the entry lotteries for concerts, theme-park slots and collectible-card releases. A number that can receive an SMS while you are still outside Japan is disproportionately useful here, because that is how people enter Japanese lotteries and reserve timed museum tickets before they fly. At least one visitor-focused provider makes incoming SMS free while roaming precisely for this reason, while outgoing calls and messages abroad are charged separately.

### When the answer is simply no

LINE is the default communication tool in Japan, and most hotels, restaurants and tour operators are perfectly happy to use it, email or an app-based call. If your itinerary is cities, hotels and restaurants, a data-only plan plus your home number for verification codes covers essentially everything. A Japanese number earns its cost when a specific named service — a school, a landlord, a clinic, a lottery — insists on one.

## 9. How to receive SMS verification codes on a Japan eSIM

If your goal is simply to receive a code, the fix is usually not a Japanese number at all. Two approaches work:

1. **Keep your home number active.** Put your Japan eSIM in the phone as the data line and leave your home SIM enabled for calls and texts (with data roaming off). Codes from your bank, email provider or home services then arrive on your own number as they always have. The mechanics are the same dual-SIM setup described in our [dual-SIM setup walkthrough](/blog/japan-esim-installation-guide/).
2. **Register with a Japanese number only when the service demands it.** Some domestic Japanese platforms and reward schemes will only accept a local number. If you hit one, that is the point at which a Mobal or Sakura Mobile voice plan starts to pay for itself.

### The two catches nobody mentions

Two practical caveats. First, keeping the home line on means the phone has to stay in dual-SIM mode, which costs a little battery. Second, if your home carrier charges for **incoming SMS while roaming**, check that first — most do not, but the ones that do can be expensive.

## 10. Keeping your home number active with a Japan eSIM (dual SIM setup)

### The standard dual-SIM configuration

The configuration that suits most travellers who need 2FA:

- **Data line:** Japan eSIM.
- **Voice and SMS line:** your home SIM, with **data roaming turned off** so it never bills you for background data.
- **Default voice line:** your home number, so calls and texts behave normally.

This gives you full-speed local data on the Japan eSIM while your bank, email and travel apps keep reaching the number they already know. If you are on an iPhone with eSIM-only hardware, both lines can be eSIM profiles — there is no need for a physical SIM slot. Check that your handset supports the setup first, using our [is your phone supported?](/blog/japan-esim-compatibility-guide/).

## 11. Making calls in Japan with a data-only eSIM

If you do not need a Japanese number, calls over data are usually enough. ### What works for calls without a local number

LINE is the de facto messaging app in Japan, and most hotels, restaurants and tour operators will happily take a LINE message or an app-based call. WhatsApp works for anyone outside Japan. For calls to ordinary phone numbers, a VoIP service with cheap outbound minutes covers occasional hotel or taxi bookings.

### Where app-based calling stops working

The limits are worth stating plainly: these apps **cannot receive SMS sent to a Japanese number**, and many verification systems reject VoIP numbers outright. If a service insists on a real number, no app will substitute for it.

## 12. The Monthly Maths: Voice Plan vs eSIM Plus Your Home Line

### What a visitor voice plan actually costs, itemised

A visitor-facing Japanese voice plan starts at about **¥1,650 a month** on the Docomo network, with the data tier scaling up from there — mid-range allowances sit around ¥4,378 a month and the largest consumer tiers run into the high thousands of yen. The details around the headline price are what decide the real cost. Incoming calls and incoming SMS are free; outgoing calls are billed by the minute and outgoing texts by the 160-character message.

Voicemail is an optional extra of about ¥495 a month. Voice and SMS roaming outside Japan costs a one-off ¥990 each time you enable it, with incoming SMS still free. The data allowance resets on the 1st of each month, one month's unused data rolls over, and once the allowance is spent the connection slows to roughly 200 kbps rather than stopping. There is no activation fee and no termination fee, which is not universal in this market — several Japanese providers charge a setup fee in the thousands of yen, so compare the first bill rather than the monthly line.

### The comparison people expect to be lopsided

Most travellers assume a voice plan costs several times a data eSIM. At the entry tier that is not quite true: a month of a visitor voice plan starting near ¥1,650 sits in the same range as a two-week mid-tier data eSIM. What you pay instead is process. The voice plan needs identification, is delivered by post or collected in person rather than emailed, runs on a monthly cycle rather than a trip-length one, and in some cases carries a setup fee. A data eSIM costs minutes and nothing else.

That framing makes the decision simple. The voice plan is not expensive; it is simply a different kind of purchase, and there is no reason to make it unless something on your itinerary requires the number.

## 14. Emergency calls in Japan: 110 and 119

### Two numbers, and how they behave on a data-only line

**110** reaches the police and **119** reaches fire and ambulance. Both work from any active eSIM, without a local balance, so a data-only plan is enough for emergencies. If your phone is set to data-only on the Japan line, make sure the line is at least enabled for calls so the emergency dialler can use it.

## 15. Japan eSIM with a phone number FAQ

**Can I make calls with a Japan eSIM?**
With a voice + data plan from Mobal, Sakura Mobile or HanaCell, yes — incoming and outgoing calls on a real Japanese number. With a data-only eSIM, no; you can only call through apps such as LINE or WhatsApp, which need data.

**Can a tourist get a Japanese phone number?**
Yes, through providers that serve visitors without a residence card (Mobal, Sakura Mobile, and HanaCell for overseas residents). Mainstream carriers require a residence card, so their cheap contracts are not available to tourists.

**Does a Japan eSIM come with a phone number?**
A standard travel eSIM does not. Data-only plans carry no Japanese number and cannot send or receive SMS — that is the single most common misconception about Japan eSIMs.

**Why is my SMS verification code not arriving?**
Most often because the code is being sent to a Japanese number that your data-only eSIM does not have, or because your home SIM is disabled while you are abroad. Enable your home line for SMS, or use a voice plan if the service requires a local number.

**Can I keep my own number while using a Japan eSIM?**
Yes. Run the Japan eSIM as your data line and leave your home SIM on for calls and texts with data roaming off. This is the standard dual-SIM configuration and it keeps 2FA working.

**Do I need a Japanese number for a short trip?**
Almost never. For a normal holiday, data is enough, and keeping your home number alive covers 2FA.

**Why can a Japanese voice eSIM not be emailed to me?**
Because a voice line has to be tied to a verified identity. At least one visitor-facing provider states that its voice and data eSIM cannot be sent by email, so the access code is posted or collected in person. Data-only travel eSIMs carry no number and no verification, which is why they arrive as an instant QR code.

**Do I need ID to buy a data-only eSIM for Japan?**
From an international provider before you fly, no — it is not a Japanese SIM contract. At a counter inside Japan, the position is changing: a 2026 amendment extends identity verification to data-only SIMs and provides for passport checks on short-stay visitors, with the commencement date set by cabinet order.

**Will a 050 number work for SMS verification?**
Usually not. 050 is Japan's IP telephony prefix rather than a mobile range, and verification systems that specifically ask for a mobile number will generally reject it. If a service insists on a Japanese mobile number, you need a voice plan with an 070, 080 or 090 number.

**Is a voice plan more expensive than a data eSIM?**
Less than most people expect at the entry tier — a month of a visitor voice plan starting near ¥1,650 is close to a two-week data eSIM. What you are really paying is process: identification, delivery time and a monthly cycle instead of a trip-length one.
