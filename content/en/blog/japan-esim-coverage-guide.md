**Short answer:** Japan has three main mobile networks — **NTT Docomo, KDDI (au) and SoftBank** — plus Rakuten Mobile as a fourth operator. A Japan eSIM runs on one or two of them depending on the provider. **Docomo has the widest rural and mountain coverage; SoftBank is strongest in cities; au sits between the two.** For anything beyond Tokyo, Osaka and Kyoto, choosing an eSIM that can use Docomo is the single most useful decision you can make.

## 1. Which networks do Japan eSIMs use?

Japan's market is built on three national carriers:

- **NTT Docomo** — the largest by subscribers, and the network with the most extensive coverage outside the big cities, including mountains and remote islands.
- **KDDI (au)** — the second-largest, with strong urban and expressway coverage and a heavy investment in 5G.
- **SoftBank** — third by subscribers, known for fast urban data and for sharing rural 5G infrastructure with KDDI.
- **Rakuten Mobile** — the newest national operator, building its own network and using roaming where it does not yet have coverage.

Travel eSIMs do not build networks; they buy access. That means **your coverage is the carrier's coverage, not the eSIM brand's** — a point worth holding on to when comparing providers, because it makes the underlying network the deciding factor rather than the logo. The provider-by-provider breakdown of which network each one uses is in our [provider-by-network breakdown](/blog/japan-esim-providers-comparison-guide/).

## 2. Docomo vs SoftBank vs au for Japan eSIM: how they differ

| Network | Strongest where | Weaker where | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NTT Docomo** | Rural areas, mountains, islands, long-distance rail | Occasionally congested in dense city centres | Anything outside the major cities |
| **KDDI (au)** | Cities, suburbs, expressways; broad 5G rollout | Very remote mountain terrain | Balanced nationwide trips |
| **SoftBank** | Central Tokyo, Osaka, urban 5G speeds | Rural and mountain coverage | City-focused trips, heavy urban data |

The practical rule is simple: **if your itinerary stays in Tokyo, Osaka and Kyoto, any of the three will serve you well. If it includes Hokkaido, the Japanese Alps, Shikoku or remote islands, Docomo is the safer pick.**

Note that KDDI and SoftBank run a joint venture, **5G Japan**, which shares base-station assets in rural areas. That has narrowed the historic gap between the two outside the cities, though Docomo's own footprint remains the widest overall.

## 3. Japan Mobile Bands: Why Coverage Depends on Your Phone Too

Every Japan eSIM guide tells you to choose a network. Almost none mention that a network can only reach you on a frequency your handset actually supports — and Japan uses several bands that are close to unique in the world. The low bands, the ones that carry signal into mountains, basements and thick-walled buildings, are exactly the ones most often missing from phones sold in North America or Europe.

### The 800 MHz trap: Band 19 and Band 18

Japan's two largest networks put their foundational coverage on 800 MHz blocks that do not line up with anyone else's. NTT Docomo uses **Band 19**; KDDI (au) uses **Band 18**, which is often reported in phone specifications as Band 26. Neither is the same as the American Band 5 or the European Band 20, even though all four get described loosely as "800 MHz" or "850 MHz". A handset engineered for another continent can simply omit them.

The consequence is easy to feel and hard to diagnose. Without Band 19, your phone cannot use Docomo's low-band layer at all, so it falls back to Band 1 (2.1 GHz) and Band 3 (1.8 GHz). Those travel shorter distances and penetrate buildings less well, so you still see a healthy signal in a city and then lose it first in tunnels, basements and rural valleys — which is precisely when you wanted it.

### Band 8: the low band your phone probably already has

SoftBank's low band is **Band 8** at 900 MHz, a globally harmonised block that sits inside essentially every iPhone and most international Android flagships. That flips the usual advice on its head. Docomo has the better map, but SoftBank has the better fit for a phone bought outside Japan, because the band your handset is most likely to support is the band SoftBank actually uses. KDDI sits between the two: it holds the Japan-specific Band 18 as well as the widely supported 700 MHz Band 28.

### 5G bands: n77 travels, n79 does not

On 5G the difference is sharper. SoftBank, KDDI and Rakuten all deploy mid-band 5G in **n77**, which is the frequency range most 5G handsets support worldwide. Docomo uses n78 and **n79** — and n79 at 4.5 GHz is effectively a Docomo signature, appearing outside Japan mainly in devices sold in mainland China. A 5G phone bought in Europe or North America will often see Docomo's 5G only where n78 is deployed, and will spend more of the trip on LTE.

| Band | Type | Who runs it | What it means for a foreign phone |
|---|---|---|---|
| B1 (2.1 GHz) | LTE | All four carriers | Present almost everywhere |
| B3 (1.8 GHz) | LTE | All four carriers | Present almost everywhere |
| B8 (900 MHz) | LTE | SoftBank | The low band your phone most likely has |
| B18 / B26 (800 MHz) | LTE | KDDI (au) | Japan-specific, often missing abroad |
| B19 (800 MHz) | LTE | Docomo | Japan-specific, often missing abroad |
| B28 (700 MHz) | LTE | All four carriers | Widely supported, good rural reach |
| n77 | 5G | SoftBank, KDDI, Rakuten | The 5G band global phones support |
| n78 | 5G | Docomo, KDDI | Common internationally |
| n79 (4.5 GHz) | 5G | Docomo | Rare outside Japan and China |

### How to use this before you buy

Check your exact model's band list — the model number, not the brand — before you decide between a Docomo-routed and a SoftBank-routed plan. Manufacturer specification pages always publish it, and our [device-side checks for Japan](/blog/japan-esim-compatibility-guide/) show where to find the figure on iPhone, Samsung and Pixel handsets. If your phone lists Band 8 and Band 28 but not Band 19, a SoftBank or KDDI plan will hold up better in real use than the coverage maps alone would suggest.

## 4. Population Coverage vs Land Area: Reading Japan's Numbers Honestly

### Why 99.9% can still mean a dead zone

Japanese carriers advertise population coverage above 99.9%, and the number is honest — but it measures people, not square kilometres. KDDI itself states that au reaches more than 99.9% of the population and roughly **60% of the country's land area**. Japan's population is packed into a handful of coastal plains, so a network that is effectively complete for residents can be two-fifths empty for anyone standing in a national park.

### Where the missing 40% actually is

The uncovered area is not random. It is the mountains of Hokkaido, Tohoku and Chubu, the interiors of Shikoku and Kyushu, and the smaller islands in between — which are also the places travellers specifically go out of their way to see. Two things follow. Treat any single coverage percentage as a promise about people rather than about your itinerary. And plan around the gaps instead of being surprised by them, because the routines that fix this are cheap and take five minutes.

### The rural checklist that works on any network

Build the habit before you leave the airport rather than the morning you drive into the mountains: download offline maps for the region you are entering, save your accommodation's address and phone number as a screenshot, and keep a note of the nearest train station to your destination. None of it depends on signal, and all of it removes the failure modes that travellers complain about most.

## 7. Coverage on the Move: Shinkansen, Metro, Ferries and Mountain Roads

### The Shinkansen

The Tokaido Shinkansen between Tokyo and Osaka is well covered on all three networks and is the most-cited test route in reviews. Expect brief dropouts in the long tunnels through the mountains, and expect them on every carrier, because the tunnel rather than the brand is the problem. If a call matters, take it before the train enters a tunnel section rather than mid-way through.

### Underground shopping streets and station basements

Japan's larger stations, department-store basements and underground shopping arcades are covered on all networks, but depth and construction change which band reaches you. This is where Band 19, Band 18 and Band 8 earn their keep, and where a handset without Japan's low bands notices first. If you have ever wondered why your phone works on the platform and not in the subway concourse, this is the answer.

### Ferries, islands and the coast

Coastal coverage follows two rules: the main island is usually fine, and the crossing between islands usually is not. KDDI extended its satellite coverage to the waters within 24 nautical miles of the coastline in January 2026, which says a lot about where the industry sees the gaps, though that layer belongs to the carrier's own subscriptions rather than to a travel eSIM. For island hopping, download what you need while you are still on land.

### Mountain roads and ski areas

Docomo has the widest footprint in the Japanese Alps, Hokkaido and the national parks, but only if your phone supports Band 19. Without it you are on Docomo's higher bands and may do no better than you would on SoftBank. If your trip is built around driving or hiking, check the bands before you check the price — a cheaper plan on a network your handset can actually use beats a premium plan on one it cannot.

## 8. Does a Japan eSIM give you 5G?

Yes, on networks and plans that support it — **5G is available in Japan's major metro areas**, and travel eSIMs on SoftBank, KDDI and Docomo can access it where the local network has deployed it. In practice:

- **Central Tokyo, Osaka and Nagoya** have broad 5G coverage.
- **Suburban and rural areas** are still largely 4G LTE, which is fast enough for anything a traveller does.
- **Your phone must support the bands used in Japan.** Most modern iPhones, Samsung Galaxy and Pixel handsets do; check our [phone compatibility for Japan](/blog/japan-esim-compatibility-guide/) if your device was bought outside the usual markets.

Two caveats worth stating plainly. First, **5G is a coverage question, not a plan feature** — a provider advertising "5G ready" still depends on where you are standing. Second, **"5G" on a travel eSIM can mean either a genuine local 5G connection or a fast 4G LTE connection**; for travel use, the practical difference is small. Japan's regulator, the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, publishes national 5G deployment targets, but the number that matters to you is the one on the carrier's own coverage map, not the national figure.

## 9. Satellite-to-Phone in Japan: What Launched in 2026

### Three carriers, one constellation

In April 2026 all three national carriers launched satellite-to-smartphone services within weeks of each other: KDDI's au Starlink Direct, SoftBank Starlink Direct and docomo Starlink Direct. All three ride SpaceX's Starlink Mobile constellation, so the underlying technology is the same and the differences sit in the apps and extras wrapped around it. KDDI is furthest along. A year after launch it reported more than four million users, and in January 2026 it doubled its domestic coverage area by extending service from Japan's territorial waters out to the 24-nautical-mile contiguous zone, as the carrier's [own announcement](https://newsroom.kddi.com/english/news/detail/kddi_nr-1129_4719.html) sets out.

### What the service actually does

The satellite layer carries messages, location sharing, earthquake alerts and data inside supported apps, with YAMAP, WhatsApp, X and LINE among the compatible names. On all three carriers it is currently free and requires no application. That is a deliberate choice while the networks build out: the carriers are absorbing the cost of the satellite layer inside normal subscription fees.

### Why a travel eSIM cannot use it yet

Satellite-to-phone is sold as part of a Japanese carrier subscription, not as a feature of an international travel eSIM, and no data-only tourist plan includes it today. Treat satellite connectivity as Japan's safety net for residents, and as a reliable indicator of where the terrestrial gaps are — the carriers are investing in orbit precisely because the towers stop somewhere.

## 10. Japan eSIM coverage in rural, mountain and island areas

This is where network choice earns its keep. Three patterns come up repeatedly among travellers:

- **Ski resorts and national parks** — Docomo's coverage reaches further into Hokkaido, Nagano and the Northern Alps. If you are renting a car and driving into the mountains, this is the difference between navigation working and not.
- **Remote islands** — Okinawa's outlying islands and smaller Inland Sea islands vary; Docomo is generally the most reliable, and some islands have genuinely limited service on any network.
- **Rural rail and road corridors** — coverage follows the line, so intercity trains and expressways are usually well covered, while the gaps appear between them.

A useful habit: before a rural leg, **download offline maps for the area** regardless of network. It costs nothing and removes the one dependency you cannot control.

## 11. Japan's 3G Networks Are Gone: What That Means for Your Phone

### The shutdown timeline

Japan finished switching off 3G in 2026. au closed its 3G network in March 2022, SoftBank followed in April 2024, and NTT Docomo switched off the last 3G service in the country in 2026. There is now no 3G fallback anywhere in Japan, on any network.

### VoLTE is the price of admission

The practical consequence is that there is no legacy layer left to fall back on: wherever you have a signal it is 4G or 5G, and any voice call is carried as VoLTE. That matters for reach, not just for calling — a device without Japanese VoLTE certification can show a strong data signal in central Tokyo and still drop to emergency-call-only inside a tunnel or a mountain valley, which is exactly where a visitor would notice it. Most internationally sold handsets from the iPhone 8 onward handle this automatically; the exceptions are older models, grey-import stock and cheap devices picked up for one trip.

### What to check before you fly

Three checks, in order: does the phone support the Japanese LTE bands listed above, does it support VoLTE, and is it carrier-unlocked. If all three are yes, a data-only Japan eSIM will work normally, and the only remaining variable is which network the plan runs on.

## 12. How to check Japan eSIM coverage before you buy

There is no substitute for the carriers' own maps, and all three publish them:

- Check the **Docomo**, **KDDI (au)** and **SoftBank** official coverage maps for the specific towns on your itinerary.
- Cross-check with independent network-experience data from **Opensignal** or **RootMetrics** if you want a second opinion on real-world speeds rather than theoretical coverage.
- Confirm the **network your chosen eSIM uses** — it is usually listed on the plan page or in the provider's app.
- Remember that **coverage maps show outdoor signal**. Basements, thick-walled temples, underground shopping streets and mountain valleys are weaker on every network.
- **Check that your phone is carrier-unlocked.** A handset still tied to a home carrier's instalment plan cannot accept a Japanese eSIM at all, so unlock it before you travel rather than discovering the problem at the airport.
- **Know whether your eSIM is roaming-type or local-type.** Most Japan eSIMs are sold by **MVNOs** — resellers that lease capacity on a Japanese network instead of owning one. Some route your traffic through overseas servers (typically 150–200 ms round-trip latency, and usually no Japanese IP address), while local-type providers terminate directly on the Japanese network (often 20–50 ms). For maps and messaging the difference is invisible; for Japanese-only apps, low-latency video calls or gaming it is not.

If your trip is city-only, this step takes five minutes. If it includes rural driving, it is worth doing properly, because switching eSIM after you land is more hassle than choosing the right network first.

## 13. Disaster Connectivity: 00000JAPAN, Alerts and Cross-Carrier Roaming

### 00000JAPAN: free Wi-Fi when the network fails

When a large earthquake or typhoon hits, Japan's carriers jointly open a free public Wi-Fi network under the single name **00000JAPAN**. There is no password and no registration: you select it in your Wi-Fi list and you are online, whatever carrier you are on and whatever device you are holding. It was first used commercially after the April 2016 Kumamoto earthquake, and following a 2023 revision of the industry guidelines it can also be triggered by a major network outage rather than only by a natural disaster. Japan's [Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications](https://www.soumu.go.jp/english/) coordinates the framework with the carriers. Two caveats are worth remembering: the connection is deliberately unencrypted, so keep card details and logins off it, and delete the network from your phone afterwards so it does not reconnect silently.

### Borrowing another carrier's network

Japan also operates a cross-carrier arrangement commonly called Japan roaming. When a single carrier's service fails across a wide area, that carrier's customers can temporarily use another operator's network. It was introduced by Japan's mobile operators in 2026 and exists for exactly the scenario a travel eSIM cannot cover by itself.

### Emergency calls and public alerts

Police is **110** and fire or ambulance is **119**. Both work from any active line, including a data-only eSIM with no local balance, as long as the line itself is enabled for calls. Japan also pushes earthquake and tsunami warnings to handsets through the standard cell-broadcast system, so leave emergency alerts switched on and keep location services available during your trip.

## 14. Japan eSIM Coverage FAQ: Docomo, au and SoftBank

**Which network is best for a Japan eSIM?**
Docomo for wide and rural coverage, SoftBank for city speed, and au for a balance of the two. For anything beyond the main cities, a Docomo-capable plan is the safer choice.

**Is 5G available on a Japan eSIM?**
Yes, in major metro areas on 5G-capable networks. Rural areas are mostly 4G LTE, which is still fast. Availability depends on your location and your phone's band support, not just the plan.

**Will my Japan eSIM work in rural Hokkaido or the Japanese Alps?**
Partly. Coverage exists along the main routes but thins out in mountains and national parks. A Docomo-based eSIM gives the best odds, and offline maps cover the remaining gaps.

**Do all Japan eSIMs use the same network?**
No. Ubigi uses KDDI and Docomo; Airalo, Holafly and Nomad mainly use SoftBank and KDDI. The network, not the brand, determines your coverage.

**Does Japan require IMEI registration for an eSIM?**
No. Japan does not require IMEI registration, so you can activate an eSIM without pre-registering your device.

**How many networks does a Japan eSIM connect to?**
Typically one or two of the three national carriers, depending on the provider and plan. Multi-network plans switch automatically between their partner networks, which is why our own plans emphasise automatic local network switching.

**Where these coverage claims come from.**

**Does my phone need specific bands for Japan?**
It helps a great deal. Japan's low bands — Band 19 on Docomo and Band 18 on KDDI — are specific to the country, while SoftBank's Band 8 is globally common. A phone without the Japanese low bands still works, but it loses signal first in tunnels, basements and rural areas.

**Which Japanese network has the widest coverage?**
By land area, Docomo, and by a clear margin in the mountains and on remote islands. Population coverage is above 99.9% on all three carriers, so the difference only becomes visible once you leave the cities.

**Can I use satellite connectivity on a Japan eSIM?**
Not currently. Satellite-to-phone in Japan is tied to the carriers' own subscriptions and is not part of an international travel eSIM plan. A data-only eSIM still reaches 110 and 119, and 00000JAPAN Wi-Fi is opened during major disasters.

**Is 3G still available in Japan?**
No. Every 3G network in the country has been retired, so there is no non-VoLTE fallback left — see the timeline above. That turns VoLTE support into a coverage question, not only a calling one.
