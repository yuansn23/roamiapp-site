## eSIM vs Physical SIM: The Real Differences

Before we talk prices, understand what physically differs between the two formats.

**A physical SIM** is a small plastic card with a metal contact surface. You buy it, push it into your phone's tray, and the network identity lives on the card. Swap the card, swap the network.

**An eSIM** is a software profile. There's no card. You download a small encrypted "SIM" onto your phone's built-in chip by scanning a QR code or tapping a link. The profile contains the same network credentials a physical card would — it just lives in secure hardware instead of plastic.

That single difference drives everything else:

| What matters | Physical SIM | eSIM |
|---|---|---|
| Getting started | Buy a card, use a SIM ejector pin, insert the tray | Scan a QR code over Wi-Fi, done in ~2 minutes |
| Switching networks | Eject and swap cards | Add a second profile, toggle between them |
| Risk of losing it | You can drop a tiny card mid-trip | Nothing to lose — it's software |
| Dual-number setups | One card per tray slot | Two active lines on one phone (most devices) |
| Roaming / multi-country | Usually UK-only; EU costs extra | Regional plans span 30+ countries in one profile |
| Leaving the country | Bin the card or keep it for next time | Profile stays dormant; delete when done |

**The short version:** an eSIM removes the physical object and the errand of getting it. A physical SIM removes nothing except the need for a compatible phone. That last point is the one real reason the physical card still wins for some travellers — see "when each one wins" below.

## UK Coverage: Which Networks Do Travel eSIMs Use?

Travel providers don't build their own towers — they rent access from the big four UK networks and resell it. So when you buy a "UK eSIM," the network underneath is always EE, O2, Vodafone, or Three. Which one you get determines your coverage.

Here's how the main travel providers map to real UK networks in 2026:

| Provider | Underlying network(s) | What that means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Roami | Multi-network (EE + O2 + Vodafone) | Auto-switches to the strongest; best rural + indoor blend |
| Airalo (UK) | O2 or Three (varies by plan) | Fine in cities; check which network your plan uses |
| Holafly (UK) | O2 or Vodafone | Good indoor/urban; check the plan's partner |
| Nomad (UK) | O2 | Reliable in towns, weaker in deep countryside |
| Lebara | Vodafone (exclusive MVNO) | Vodafone's urban reach + a +44 number |
| Giffgaff | O2 (MVNO) | O2's indoor signal, cheap pay-as-you-go |

**Why this matters more than the logo on the plan:** two "UK eSIMs" can perform completely differently because they sit on different networks. In our 2026 field tests, a plan on Three lost signal entirely on the London–Edinburgh train, while a plan that could reach EE held 4G the whole way. If your itinerary includes the Highlands, the Lakes, or rural Wales, pick a provider whose plan reaches EE — that's the network with the widest rural footprint.

**How to check what you're actually getting:** on the provider's plan page, look for the words "network" or "coverage." If it says "multi-network," it roams between networks automatically. If it names one network (say "O2"), you're locked to that network's footprint. This single detail is worth more than any star rating.

## UK Network Frequency Bands — Why "Compatible" Phones Still Drop Signal

Here's a topic almost no travel guide covers, and it explains a huge share of "my eSIM works but I have no bars" complaints.

UK networks broadcast on a mix of radio bands. Your phone's antenna hardware decides which of them it can hear. A phone can support eSIM perfectly yet be missing a band — and in rural Britain that's the difference between signal and a brick.

### The bands that matter for UK travel

| Band | Frequency | What it does in the UK | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Band 20 | 800 MHz | The main rural 4G band — longest range, deepest indoor reach | Missing on many US and some Asian phone models |
| Band 8 | 900 MHz | Legacy GSM band refarmed for 4G; strong through thick walls | O2 and Vodafone lean on this heavily |
| Band 3 | 1800 MHz | Primary capacity band for cities and towns | Supported by almost everything |
| Band 1 | 2100 MHz | Old 3G band now refarmed for 4G | Common, rarely a problem |
| Band 7 | 2600 MHz | Urban speed band for dense areas | Short range; irrelevant in the countryside |
| n78 | 3.5 GHz | The main UK 5G band | 5G coverage is patchy outside cities |
| n28 | 700 MHz | Low-band 5G for wide coverage | Only on newer handsets |

**The big one is Band 20 (800 MHz).** It's the band that reaches into the Scottish Highlands, the Lake District, and rural Wales. Many phones sold in the US — and some carrier-locked international models — omit it entirely. Those phones will still *connect* to UK networks on the other bands, but the moment you leave a town, coverage collapses on every network at once, because the network's long-range signal lives on a band your phone can't hear.

### How to check your bands before you go

1. Find your phone's exact model number (Settings > About Phone, or the box).
2. Look up its spec sheet and confirm it lists **LTE Band 20 (800 MHz)**.
3. If it's missing, prioritise trips around cities, or carry a cheap phone that includes Band 20.

This is a hardware issue, not an eSIM-vs-physical issue — a physical SIM on the same phone has the same gap. It's why some travellers with "compatible" phones still get poor rural signal. (For a full check that your phone supports eSIM at all, see our [compatibility list](/compatibility/).)


## UK 5G Bands and Which Phones Get Them

5G in the UK rides on a small set of bands, and not every "5G" phone can hear all of them. If your phone's 5G bands don't match the UK's, you'll silently fall back to 4G — which is fine, but you'll wonder why the "5G" badge never lights up.

| 5G band | Frequency | Role in the UK | Which phones typically have it |
|---|---|---|---|
| n78 | 3.5 GHz | The workhorse — most UK 5G is here | iPhone 12 and later; Galaxy S20 and later; Pixel 6 and later |
| n28 | 700 MHz | Long-range 5G for rural and indoor reach | iPhone 12 and later; newer Galaxy/Pixel; check older mid-range models |
| n1 | 2100 MHz | Refarmed 4G band used for wider 5G | Most 5G phones |
| n3 | 1800 MHz | Refarmed 4G band, urban fill-in | Most 5G phones |
| n8 | 900 MHz | Low-band fill-in (emerging) | Newer flagships only |

**The practical take:**

- **iPhones:** every iPhone 12 or newer supports n78 and n28, so any recent iPhone gets full UK 5G. The iPhone SE (2020) is the main exception — it's 4G-only, so no UK 5G at all.
- **Android flagships:** Samsung S20/S21/S22/S23/S24, Google Pixel 6+, and OnePlus 9+ all support n78 and n28 in their UK/global models.
- **The trap:** some budget and Chinese-market Android phones advertise "5G" but only support a narrow band set that doesn't include n28 or n78 — so they show 5G in China or the US but never in the UK. If 5G speed matters, confirm n78 on the spec sheet before buying a phone specifically for travel.
- **Reality check:** UK 5G coverage is still patchy outside cities in 2026. On a rural route you'll mostly ride 4G on Band 20 anyway, which is why the 4G band list above matters more than the 5G one for most travellers.

## Hotspot & Tethering: What's Actually Allowed

Sharing your UK connection to a laptop, tablet, or a travel companion is the difference between "one plan for the whole family" and "everyone needs their own plan." The rules vary a lot by provider, so here's the real picture:

| Provider / plan type | Hotspot allowed? | The fine print |
|---|---|---|
| Roami | Yes | No restrictions |
| Airalo | Yes | No restrictions |
| Nomad | Yes | No restrictions |
| Holafly | Limited | Some plans cap tethering to one device |
| EE (prepaid, direct) | Yes | Subject to fair-usage policy |
| O2 (prepaid, direct) | Yes | Subject to fair-usage policy |
| Three (prepaid, direct) | Yes | Unlimited plans may restrict hotspot |
| Physical PAYG SIMs | Usually yes | Check the small print on "unlimited" deals |

**Two things to know before you tether:**

1. **"Unlimited" is the trap word.** Many unlimited plans throttle to ~128 Kbps after a daily cap (often 2–5 GB), and some explicitly forbid hotspot on unlimited tiers. Read the fair-use policy before you rely on tethering.
2. **Hotspot burns data fast.** Tethering a laptop to a 1080p stream eats 1–2 GB an hour. A "family plan" that looks generous can vanish in an afternoon. Budget the data, not just the price.

If tethering is central to your trip, pick a provider that states "hotspot allowed" outright rather than one that stays quiet about it.


## Wi-Fi Calling on UK Networks — and Why It Matters Indoors

The UK's older buildings are famous for killing cellular signal. Thick stone walls, Victorian pubs, basement restaurants, and deep Tube stations all block mobile bands. Wi-Fi Calling is the escape hatch: it routes calls and texts over any Wi-Fi network when cellular signal is too weak.

| Carrier | Wi-Fi Calling on an eSIM | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| EE | Yes | Available on most prepaid plans |
| O2 | Yes | Available on most prepaid plans |
| Three | Yes | Available on most plans |
| Vodafone | Yes | Postpaid only — not on prepaid |
| Roami | No | Data-only — no phone number to route calls from |
| Airalo | No | Data-only |

**The key distinction:** Wi-Fi Calling is a *carrier* feature tied to a phone number. Data-only travel eSIMs (Roami, Airalo, Holafly) don't include a number, so there's nothing for Wi-Fi Calling to route — but you can still make WhatsApp, FaceTime, and iMessage calls over any Wi-Fi, which covers most travellers' actual needs.

If you'll spend long stretches indoors in old buildings *and* need to make traditional phone calls, that's a point toward an EE or O2 direct plan with Wi-Fi Calling. If your "calls" are really WhatsApp and FaceTime anyway, a data-only eSIM plus Wi-Fi gets you there. For how to buy those EE/O2 plans, see the [buying guide](/faq/how-to-buy-a-uk-esim/).


## UK + Europe: Choosing a Multi-Country Combo

If your trip crosses the Channel, the format decision gains a second layer. Post-Brexit, most UK local carriers re-introduced EU roaming fees — typically £2/day on EE, O2, and Vodafone, with Three charging new customers too. A UK-only physical SIM suddenly costs extra the moment you reach Paris.

Your options:

| Combo | Best for | Rough cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regional Europe eSIM (UK included) | 3+ countries incl. UK | $20–40 for 10–20 GB | One profile, no daily fees — verify the UK is listed |
| UK-only eSIM + separate EU eSIM | UK + one other country | $15 + $15 ≈ $30 | More flexible; you pick each network |
| Global eSIM | Multi-continent travel | $30–60 | Covers UK, EU, and beyond |

**The post-Brexit gotcha:** many "Europe" plans quietly dropped the UK after 2021. Always confirm the UK appears explicitly in the coverage list before buying — a plan that covers "EU only" will not help you in London.

**Which providers include the UK:** Roami Europe, Airalo Europe (check the region), and Holafly Europe all list the UK. Our [buying guide](/faq/how-to-buy-a-uk-esim/) walks through the 2026 prices and data allowances for these and more.

**A worked example:** a 10-day trip split between London and Paris. A UK-only eSIM (about $8–15 for 5–10 GB) dies at the border, and you'd need a second EU plan (~$15). A single regional Europe plan that lists the UK runs $20–40 for 10–20 GB and covers both — one install, no swap, no daily fees. On trips touching two or more countries, the regional plan almost always wins once you add up the second plan's cost.

**One more post-Brexit detail:** the UK is no longer in the EU's "roam like home" zone, and it cuts both ways — a European plan bought in France often *excludes* the UK. Always read the country list in both directions before buying.


## Do You Need a UK Phone Number?

A travel eSIM is almost always **data-only**: no phone number, so no traditional calls and no SMS. For a 1–30 day tourist that's fine — WhatsApp, iMessage, and FaceTime run over data.

A UK number becomes non-negotiable when you're opening a bank account, renting a flat, registering with the NHS, or applying for jobs — banks and agents SMS or call you. In that case, a physical PAYG SIM or an EE/O2/Three prepaid eSIM with a +44 number is the winning format. (How to buy one is covered in the [buying guide](/faq/how-to-buy-a-uk-esim/).)


## Dual Setup: Keep Your Home SIM + Add a UK eSIM

The best-of-both-worlds option that most travellers miss: you don't have to choose. Most modern phones run two lines at once — your home physical SIM for calls and texts, plus a UK eSIM for data.

**How it works:**

1. **Keep your home SIM in the physical tray.** Your regular number keeps working for calls, texts, and two-factor codes from your bank.
2. **Add a UK eSIM for data.** Set it as the default for mobile data, so maps, WhatsApp, and browsing run on the cheap UK plan.
3. **Leave "Data Roaming" on for the eSIM and off for your home line** (or let the phone route automatically). Your home line stays reachable for incoming calls without racking up roaming data charges.

**Why this is usually the winning move:**

- You keep your home number for banking and family, *and* get cheap local data.
- No physical SIM swapping at the airport, and you can't lose a card you didn't insert.
- Your home carrier sees no data use, so you dodge the $10/day roaming fee entirely.

**The one caveat:** dual-SIM drains battery a little faster, because the phone keeps two radios active. On a long day of navigation, bring a power bank. And if your phone is carrier-locked to your home network, the eSIM slot may be blocked until you unlock it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is an eSIM really cheaper than a physical SIM in the UK?**
For short trips, almost always. A 5 GB physical SIM bought at a city shop runs £10–15; the same allowance on a travel eSIM is typically $5–10, and you skip the airport markup and the shop visit. On long stays a physical PAYG SIM's lower per-GB rate can win once you add a UK number into the equation.

**Will an eSIM work if my phone has a physical SIM slot?**
Yes. Most modern phones run both at once (dual SIM), so you can keep your home number on the physical SIM and run UK data on the eSIM.

**Does an eSIM give me a UK phone number?**
Usually not — travel eSIMs are data-only. If you need a +44 number, that's a point for a physical PAYG SIM or an EE/O2 prepaid eSIM. See the buying guide for options.

**Can I tether my laptop to a UK eSIM?**
On most providers, yes. The exception is a handful of "unlimited" plans that cap or block hotspot. Confirm before you buy if tethering matters.

**Why do I have full bars but no internet indoors?**
Thick-walled UK buildings block the high-frequency bands. A multi-network eSIM will fall back to a lower, slower band that penetrates, and Wi-Fi Calling (or WhatsApp over Wi-Fi) covers the rest.

**Does my phone's frequency bands matter more than eSIM vs physical?**
In rural Britain, yes. Missing Band 20 (800 MHz) degrades coverage on every network regardless of format — check your model's band list before a Highlands or Wales trip.

**Can one eSIM cover both the UK and Europe?**
Yes, if you buy a regional Europe plan that explicitly lists the UK. Post-Brexit, double-check the coverage list.

**I already have a physical SIM that works — should I switch to eSIM?**
Only if you want to buy before you land, avoid a shop visit, or add a second line without a second tray slot. The physical card you have will keep working fine.

**What's the single best eSIM for a UK trip?**
There's no universal "best" — it depends on your route. For city trips, any cheap plan works. For rural routes, pick a multi-network plan that reaches EE. For multi-country trips, a regional Europe plan that lists the UK. Our [buying guide](/faq/how-to-buy-a-uk-esim/) compares the 2026 plans side by side so you can match one to your itinerary.

**Do I need Band 20 if I'm only visiting London?**
Not really. Band 20 (800 MHz) matters most in the countryside and inside thick-walled buildings. London's dense network of higher bands covers you almost everywhere, so a phone missing Band 20 will be fine in the capital — it'll only bite on a rural day trip.

**Can I just use my US plan's free international data in the UK?**
If your US plan includes UK data (T-Mobile's international, AT&T International Day Pass, Verizon TravelPass), you can — but check the speed. Many include only slow "2G-speed" data (128–256 Kbps) unless you pay a daily fee, which is too slow for maps or video calls. A travel eSIM at $5–10 is usually faster and cheaper than a $10/day pass.

**Can I install both a UK eSIM and a Europe eSIM before I fly?**
Yes. Most phones store 5–8 eSIM profiles (two active at once), so you can install a UK-only plan and a separate EU plan before departure and toggle between them as you cross the border. A single regional Europe plan that lists the UK is simpler if it covers your whole route — but two plans give you more control over each network.


## The Bottom Line

The format decision is smaller than it looks. If your trip is under a month and you don't need a UK phone number, an eSIM wins on price, speed, and convenience. If you're settling in for months and need a +44 number, a physical SIM (or a local prepaid eSIM) wins on ongoing cost.
